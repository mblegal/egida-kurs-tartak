## Introduction

The same Monday, 13:45, band saw no. 2 in the EGIDA Foundation hall. Andrij is just finishing the 42nd beam from job order DP-2026-042 (3 pieces will carry over to Tuesday according to the update signed by foreman Marek). He puts down the pusher, turns off the main switch, waits 40 seconds for the band to stop completely, removes his hearing protectors and gloves. At 13:50 Marek walks up with a second A4 form, this time green. "Andrij, the job order was the shift input. The log is the shift output. Every physical worker at the EGIDA sawmill has been leaving a written trace for the next shift since 2024, describing what happened between 6:30 and 14:00. Before you sign the timesheet, you fill in this form. Not 'more or less'. Exactly: how many hours the saw ran, how long it was off, which bands you took down and for what reason, which faults you reported. It is your word about what happened. Remember: your relief operator from the second shift reads this log at 14:15, and your entries decide whether he starts without surprises."

Andrij takes the form. Title: "Operator's shift log – band saw no. 2". Shift number: **ZMI-2026-042-01** (same job order, first shift = morning). Date: 20.04.2026. Operator: Andrij Tkaczenko, code H-02. Planned hours: 6:30–14:00. The log has eight main fields and two tables. Andrij is seeing it for the first time, but from Marek's description he already understands the point: this is not "paperwork for paperwork's sake", it is a record of two kinds of information travelling through two different channels. First, **information for accounting**: saw machine running hours, volume of raw material processed, consumed bands (each band costs 180–240 PLN, and the sawmill averages 120 bands per year per saw). Second, **information for production**: downtimes, faults, technical observations that must reach the shift dispatcher before the second shift starts at 14:15.

In lesson 1 you learned to read the job order: the paper that goes from the office down to the machine. In lesson 2 you are learning to fill in the log: the paper that goes from the machine back up to the office. These two documents together **close the information loop** of the sawmill. The job order says what you were supposed to do; the log says what you actually did. The dispatcher compares both documents the next morning and sees whether the plan matches the execution. If the job order says "45 beams priority B" and the log says "42 beams + 3 tomorrow, reason: no raw material from 10:30", the dispatcher knows you are not to blame for the delay: logistics is. That very log will be his evidence, and without it the conversation the next day becomes a matter of memory against memory, which in any sawmill eventually ends badly for the operator who was standing at the machine.

## Objectives

After this lesson:

1. You fill in the **eight main fields of the shift log**: shift number, date, operator, machine, machine working hours (so-called machine running hours), input raw material volume (m³), output product volume (m³ or pieces), output ratio (ratio of output to input, as a percentage). Each field is marked with a letter A–H in the log; you fill it in with a blue pen, in block capitals.
2. You fill in the **downtime table** by four types: **technical** (breakdown, tool change), **material** (no raw material, poor quality), **organizational** (no assignment, waiting for a decision), **personal** (mandatory break, company-doctor visit). Each downtime is logged with three numbers: start time, end time, total minutes. A typical day contains 30–90 minutes of combined downtime spread across 3–6 entries.
3. You fill in the **table of used bands**: band number (assigned by the warehouse), mounting time, removal time, estimated number of cuts, reason for removal (natural dulling, cracking, species change). For a band saw a typical shift involves 2–4 bands; with difficult raw material (dry oak, beech) up to 6 bands.
4. You describe **reported faults** in a free-text field (max 400 characters, about 60 words). Three-part format: **what** (what it concerns), **when** (time of detection), **what I did** (escalated, shut down, replaced). You do not describe causes you are guessing at: diagnosing causes is the mechanic's job, not yours.
5. You understand the **two-way flow of the log**: the original copy (white) stays with the first-shift foreman and reaches the shift dispatcher at 14:15; the duplicate copy (green) stays by the machine and is read by **your relief operator from the second shift** as his first task at 14:15. You know that the log has **two audiences**: the dispatcher (numbers, accounts) and the relief operator (operational knowledge, machine condition).

## Content

::: info
**Why the log has two audiences and why that matters for how you write.** If you write "band number 7 used up, I fitted number 11", that is enough for the dispatcher (he knows one band left the warehouse and another came in), but it **is not enough for the relief operator**. At 14:15 your relief stands at the same band saw as you, reaches for the log, reads "fitted number 11" and wonders: "when exactly, on which species, how many cuts did it make, should I watch it?". Without that information the relief either asks the foreman (10 minutes lost) or works blind and risks an accident. That is why good practice is this: write full sentences with times and species, e.g. "08:40 removed band 7 (spruce, approx. 140 cuts, natural dulling), fitted band 11 (new from warehouse)". 25 extra characters in the log = 20 minutes saved for the relief. Across a month that is 8 fewer hours wasted on phone calls between shifts.
:::

### Eight main fields of log ZMI-2026-042-01

| Field | Name | Andrij's value on 20.04.2026 |
|-------|------|---------------------------|
| A | Shift number | ZMI-2026-042-01 (shift 1 = morning) |
| B | Date | 20.04.2026, Monday |
| C | Operator (name, code) | Andrij Tkaczenko, H-02 |
| D | Machine | Band saw no. 2, Wood-Mizer LT40 |
| E | Saw machine running hours | 6:54–13:42 = 6.8 h |
| F | Input raw material volume | 4.12 m³ (38 spruce logs from pack SW-A-190420) |
| G | Output product volume | 2.56 m³ (42 beams 100 × 200 × 4000 mm) |
| H | Output ratio (G/F) | 62.1% (within industry norm 58–65% for spruce beam production) |

**Machine running hours** (field E) is the time of actual saw operation, measured by the electronic counter in the controller panel (tied to main-motor operation monitoring). This counter starts when the band accelerates to working speed and stops when you turn off the main switch. Andrij started the shift at 6:30, but the saw ran from 6:54 (after the morning walk-around, STOP test, 30 s idle run, first test cut). He finished at 13:42; the remainder until 14:00 was taken up by cleaning and the log. The difference between "operator working hours" (7.5 h) and "saw machine running hours" (6.8 h) comes from downtimes and non-productive time, and that difference is **unavoidable, not waste**. The accounting department uses the machine-running-hours number to calculate depreciation of the saw and to plan servicing intervals (main bearings every 2000 machine running hours, belt inspection every 500), so an inaccurate entry here distorts both the cost calculation and the maintenance schedule.

**Output ratio** (field H) is the single most important shift result for the dispatcher. 62% means that from 4.12 m³ of raw material came out 2.56 m³ of finished product, and the rest (38%, i.e. 1.56 m³) is sawdust, waste, bark and edgings. For spruce beam production the industry norm is 58–65% (oak 45–55%, pine 60–68%, beech 40–50%). If for a week your ratio stays below 55% on spruce, the dispatcher starts looking for the cause: a badly set wedge, a dull band, poor sorting of raw material at the input. A single low-ratio shift is not investigated: every operator has a bad day, every pack of logs has its share of knotty pieces. But a pattern across five or six consecutive shifts is something the dispatcher treats as a signal that something in the process has shifted, and the log is the first place he looks for the explanation.

### Downtime table – four types

A downtime is any moment during the shift when the saw was not running even though the operator was present. You split it into four types, because each type has a different "problem owner" in the sawmill:

- **Technical (T)**: breakdown, band change, tension adjustment, guide cleaning. **Owner**: mechanic, maintenance department.
- **Material (M)**: no raw material in the yard, raw material of wrong dimensions, mismatch with the job order (lesson 1). **Owner**: yard warehouseman, dispatcher.
- **Organizational (O)**: waiting for a decision from the foreman, change of priority, meeting, training. **Owner**: foreman, dispatcher.
- **Personal (P)**: mandatory breakfast break (15 min), lunch break (30 min), visit to the company doctor, physiological need. **Owner**: the operator, i.e. you.

Example downtime table from Andrij's shift (real values):

| Start | End | Minutes | Type | Description |
|-------|-----|---------|------|-------------|
| 08:40 | 08:54 | 14 | T | Band change 7 → 11, natural dulling |
| 09:15 | 09:30 | 15 | P | Mandatory breakfast break |
| 10:30 | 10:48 | 18 | M | Waiting for spruce delivery (no raw material, job order changed by Marek to 42 + 3 tomorrow) |
| 11:50 | 12:20 | 30 | P | Mandatory lunch break |
| 13:30 | 13:42 | 12 | T | Band tension adjustment (from 2250 to 2350 PSI) |
| – | – | **89** | | **Sum of downtimes** |

89 minutes out of a 7.5 h shift = 19.8% of the time. That is within norm (15–25% for a shift with a band change and mandatory breaks). If the total exceeded 30%, the dispatcher starts asking about the cause. If it dropped below 10%, the dispatcher starts checking whether the operator did not forget the mandatory breaks (OHS, Labour Code art. 134: 15 min mandatory after 6 h of work, plus a separate meal time regulated by the workplace rules).

### Table of used bands

Each band has a three-digit warehouse number assigned on delivery intake. You record four pieces of information: number, mounting time, removal time, estimated number of cuts, reason for removal. The estimated number of cuts is your observation, not a measurement: you count roughly the boards sawn since mounting (for a band saw one board = 1 lengthwise cut + 1 crosswise cut = 2 cuts, so 20 boards = about 40 cuts). Andrij's table:

| Band number | Mounted | Removed | Cuts (est.) | Reason |
|-------------|---------|---------|-------------|--------|
| 7 | (yesterday, 13:50) | 08:40 | ~140 | Natural dulling |
| 11 | 08:54 | 13:42 | ~190 | Tension adjustment, left on for the next shift |

Band 7 was mounted by yesterday's second shift (by whom? You can see that in the previous log: proof that the log is a chain, not isolated documents). Band 11 stays on the saw for your relief: he will start his log with "mounted by previous shift, band 11, approx. 190 cuts, condition: good, continuing". In this way three saw shifts (6:30–14:00, 14:00–22:00, 22:00–6:00 or a two-shift variant) create a continuous record of a band's life, from the warehouse to full consumption (800–1200 cuts for spruce, 300–500 for oak).

### Free-text field: reported faults

Three-part format: **what** / **when** / **what I did**. Example from Andrij's log, written in block capitals:

> "13:30 – GRINDING NOISE DURING BAND SPIN-UP. SHUT DOWN, REPORTED TO MAREK. RESUMED AFTER TENSION ADJUSTMENT FROM 2250 TO 2350 PSI. MAREK'S OBSERVATION: PROBABLY PLAY IN UPPER ROLLER, REQUESTING MECHANIC INSPECTION TUESDAY MORNING."

Notice: Andrij describes **what he saw and what he did**, but he **does not diagnose** ("play in the upper roller") as his own conclusion, only as Marek's observation. This is important, because if on Tuesday the mechanic discovers a different cause (e.g. a cracked bearing), there will be no complaint along the lines of "the operator claimed it was play". Andrij wrote only what he knows for sure: the noise, the shutdown, the escalation, the quoted opinion of the foreman. The rest goes into the "Faults requiring repair" table in the dispatcher's log, not yours.

::: warning
**Three common junior mistakes when filling in the log.** Mistake one: **"more or less" instead of specific times**. "Around 8 in the morning I changed the band" is useless to the dispatcher: he needs "08:40". The difference between "around 8" and "08:40" is 40 minutes, which on a 7.5 h shift is 9% of the time, enough to distort the machine-running-hours accounting. Mistake two: **skipping "personal" (type P) downtimes**. The junior thinks: "the breakfast break is mandatory, everyone knows about it, why write it down". You write it down, because without an entry your shift looks like "7.5 h of machine running" instead of "6.8 h of machine running + 0.75 h of mandatory breaks". The difference then becomes a complaint about "why is your output so low", when in fact it was normal. Mistake three: **diagnosing instead of describing**. Instead of "grinding noise 13:30, shut down, reported to Marek", the junior writes "damaged bearing of upper roller". You do not know that. You describe facts (noise, shutdown), observations (Marek said something), not diagnostic conclusions, which belong to the mechanic.
:::

::: tip
**How to fill in the log quickly without sacrificing accuracy.** Do not fill in the log only at 13:45 at the end of the shift: you forget details. Keep an **operator's notebook** (A6 notepad, always in the pocket of your work jacket) and write down four things in it during the shift: time of each band change, time of every downtime longer than 5 minutes, time of every question to the foreman, time of every fault. When at 13:45 you sit down with the official log, you already have a list of times from the notebook: you transcribe, you format, you do not try to reconstruct from memory. The notebook is not an archival document (it is your own aid), so you can use abbreviations in it, draw, cross out. You fill in the archival log from the notebook. This habit saves 15 minutes on every shift and eliminates 90% of "when was that" errors.
:::

::: example
**Andrij's case – log ZMI-2026-042-01 filled in 13:45–13:58.** Andrij sits down at the small desk, takes out the green A4 form, pulls the A6 notebook from his pocket. The notebook says: "6:54 start, 8:40 band 7 → 11 spruce approx. 140 cuts, 9:15–9:30 breakfast, 10:30–10:48 no delivery Marek changed job order to 42+3, 11:50–12:20 lunch, 13:30 grinding adjustment 2250→2350 Marek observation, 13:42 stop". He transfers it to the log: eight fields A–H in 3 minutes, downtime table in 4 minutes (6 entries, total 89 min), band table in 2 minutes (2 entries), fault field 3 minutes (60 words in block capitals). At 13:58 he signs the "Closing operator" field and writes the time 13:58. He stands up, takes the white original to Marek (Marek initials the "Approving foreman" field), puts the green copy into the log folder next to the saw. At 14:02 Andrij is ready to close his timesheet. At 14:15 the relief reaches for the green copy and reads for 5 minutes before touching the saw, and he knows that band 11 has made about 190 cuts, was left on after a tension adjustment to 2350 PSI, and the mechanic is coming Tuesday morning to check the upper roller.
:::

## Key terms

Po polsku / In English / En español / Українською:

- **Dziennik zmiany operatora / Operator's shift log / Diario de turno del operador / Журнал зміни оператора** – a written document filled in at the end of each shift by the machine operator; contains machine running hours, raw material volumes, downtimes, used bands, faults; archived for five years.
- **Motogodziny / Machine running hours / Horas de funcionamiento / Мотогодини** – the time of actual saw operation measured by the electronic counter in the controller panel; different from operator working hours, usually by 0.5–1.5 h less because of downtimes and non-productive times.
- **Wskaźnik wydajności / Output ratio / Índice de rendimiento / Коефіцієнт виходу** – the ratio of output product volume to input raw material volume, expressed as a percentage; industry norm: spruce 58–65%, pine 60–68%, oak 45–55%, beech 40–50%.
- **Typy przestojów T/M/O/P / Downtime types T/M/O/P / Tipos de parada T/M/O/P / Типи простоїв T/M/O/P** – four downtime categories at the EGIDA sawmill: technical (breakdown), material (no raw material), organizational (waiting for a decision), personal (mandatory breaks); each has a different "problem owner".
- **Numer taśmy / Band number / Número de cinta / Номер стрічки** – a three-digit warehouse code assigned to a band on delivery intake; recorded in the table of used bands together with mounting time, removal time, number of cuts and reason for removal.
- **Format trzyczęściowy usterki / Three-part fault format / Formato tripartito de avería / Трискладовий формат несправності** – the fault-description scheme for the free-text field: what (description of the phenomenon), when (time), what I did (escalation, shutdown, replacement); it never contains a diagnosis of the cause.
- **Łańcuch zmian / Shift chain / Cadena de turnos / Ланцюг змін** – the continuous record of the machine's life across successive shifts (first → second → third); the relief operator reads the green copy of the previous shift's log as the first task after taking over the machine.

## Link to practice

In the training workshop you will get three exercises. First: manual filling-in of a shift log from dictated information (the instructor reads out events from a simulated five-minute day: "6:54 start, 7:30 I adjust the wedge 4 minutes, 8:15 foreman phone call 8 minutes, 9:00–9:15 breakfast, 10:00 band change 12 minutes, 11:00 raw material ran out 22 minutes, 12:00–12:30 lunch, 13:15 I finish everything, 13:30 I start the log", and you have to fill in all fields in 10 minutes). Second: interpreting a colleague's log (you receive a filled-in green copy and answer three questions: how many machine running hours, what percentage of downtimes, what do you have to watch on the band mounted for your shift). Third: recognizing three typical mistakes in a log (two logs are shown, one correct, one with three mistakes of the "more or less" type, skipping personal breaks, diagnosing instead of describing). Passing threshold: recognizing all three mistakes.

## Trainer notes

This lesson is pragmatic and may feel boring to trainees ("paperwork"), but it has **fundamental importance for the documentation chain** of the whole crew. It is worth saying openly at the start: "every minute not filled in or filled in badly in the log becomes 15 minutes of work for someone else: the dispatcher, the relief, the mechanic, the accountant. That is why 15 minutes of filling in the log is an investment, not a loss." A second typical pushback: "at my previous sawmill we talked about this verbally after the shift". Response: the EGIDA sawmill introduced the written log in 2024, because an audit showed an average of 3% of output lost per year due to gaps in inter-shift communication (annually 35–40 thousand PLN per single saw). A third risk: the trainee spends excessive time on "writing nicely" (full sentences, ornate descriptions). Here the trainer reminds them: the log is a working document, not a literary one; **block capitals, short fact descriptions, no interpretation**. Introductory exercise: the trainer gives trainees an example of a log filled in perfectly and a log filled in "more or less"; trainees point out five differences. They usually find three (precise times, full downtime types, three-part fault format) and need help with two (the entry about a band carried over to the next shift, the absence of a diagnosis in the fault description).
