## Introduction

Yesterday you learned **the machines that cut**: the saws. Today you'll meet **the machines that don't cut**. Machines that **feed**, **carry**, **shift**, **lift**, and **prepare** the material. These machines **surround you all day**.

And here is **the paradox** you must understand from the start: **support machines are often more dangerous than the saws**.

Why? Because **a saw stands in one place**. You know where it is. You see the danger zone. You hear the sound. A support machine, a forklift, a conveyor, a grapple, **moves**. It is where you are. It can come up **from behind**, **from the side**, **without warning**. A saw cuts only what you feed it. A forklift can **drive into a person** standing around the corner.

In Polish sawmills **most fatal accidents** are not accidents with the saw. They are accidents **with the forklift**, **stack collapse**, **being hit by a log**. PIP statistics show that out of 100 accidents in the wood industry, about **35–40 involve support and transport machines**.

In this lesson you'll meet the **five** most common "non-cutting machines" in a sawmill and **where your safe zone ends** around each of them.

## Goals

After this lesson:

- You recognize the **forklift** (Polish: **wózek widłowy**), the **roller conveyor** (Polish: **przenośnik rolkowy**), the **belt conveyor** (Polish: **przenośnik taśmowy**), the **grapple / manipulator** (Polish: **chwytak**), and the **debarker** (Polish: **korowarka**).
- You know that the **pedestrian zone** ends where the **forklift zone** begins, and they never overlap.
- You understand the **visibility rule**: if the forklift operator **does not see you**, **you do not see him**.
- You remember that a **wood stack** is also a "machine", it can topple over without warning.

## Content

### The forklift: the most dangerous machine in the sawmill

A **forklift** (Polish: **wózek widłowy**, also colloquially **"widlak"**) is a small vehicle with **two metal forks** at the front that **lift and carry** heavy loads, pallets, lumber stacks, single logs.

It looks innocent. It drives into the hall, comes up under the load, lifts, drives on. But **three things** make it the most dangerous:

**1. The forklift is quiet.** Forklifts (especially new electric ones) **make almost no noise**: particularly in a hall where saws are also working. A pedestrian often **doesn't hear an approaching forklift**, even from 5 m away.

**2. The operator sees little behind.** Behind the forklift, due to construction (engine, cabin, load in front), **the operator has a large blind spot**. If he reverses, he may **not see a person** 2 metres behind him.

**3. Braking distance is long.** A forklift with a 1-tonne load **doesn't stop like a car**. Emergency braking distance is **2–4 metres**. If you run out under its wheels, **the operator won't manage to brake**.

**Pedestrian safety zone around the forklift:**

- **The pedestrian line**: a yellow line painted on the floor. **You never step out of it** without a hand sign to the operator.
- **Distance from the forklift**: minimum **2 metres** when the forklift is moving.
- **Never behind the forklift**: that is the operator's blind spot. If you must walk behind a forklift, **first give the operator a hand sign**, **wait** for him to nod his head, **then walk through**.
- **Never under the load**: if the forklift carries pallets or logs up high, **don't walk under them**. The load can fall.

::: warning
**The most dangerous accident** with a forklift: a pedestrian **crouches to pick something up** from the floor and doesn't see the approaching forklift. The forklift operator **doesn't see the pedestrian** behind a stack of boards. The forklift **drives into the back** of the crouching pedestrian. Result: death or paralysis. **Rule**: when you crouch in a hall with forklifts, **first look around 360°**, then crouch. Or crouch **against a wall**, where a forklift cannot drive in.
:::

### The visibility rule: the most important 3 words

In every hall with forklifts the **visibility rule** applies:

> **If the operator doesn't see you, you don't see him.**

This rule has three practical consequences:

1. **Make eye contact.** Before walking in front of or behind the forklift, **look the operator in the eye** (through the cabin window). Wait for him to **nod** or **stop the forklift**. Only then do you walk.
2. **Don't assume he sees you.** The operator may have **the sun in his eyes**, **his head turned**, **a radio conversation**. Your assumption "he probably sees me" is **the most common cause of accidents**.
3. **Wear a high-visibility vest.** PPE for a helper in a hall with forklifts **always includes a high-visibility vest** (yellow or orange). It's your **"see me"** in the dim light of the hall.

### Roller conveyor

A **roller conveyor** (Polish: **przenośnik rolkowy**) is a **row of metal rollers** mounted in a frame, on which material **rolls** (logs, boards). It can be:

- **Passive (gravity)**: the rollers turn slowly when material runs onto them. The material moves **due to the slope** of the path.
- **Active (powered)**: the rollers **rotate electrically**, pulling the material at a set speed.

**Danger zone of the roller conveyor:**

- **Hands between the rollers**: entrapment. When a roller turns, it easily catches a glove, finger, hair.
- **Foot under the conveyor**: a sliding log can fall if it goes over the edge.
- **Line of material movement**: never stand **on the axis**, because a board may strike.

::: example
At the roller conveyor, most often a helper **shifts or adjusts a log** that has jammed. **Rule**: before touching the log **stop the conveyor** (the button next to it). Never put your hands **between rotating rollers** or **under a moving log**.
:::

### Belt conveyor

A **belt conveyor** (Polish: **przenośnik taśmowy**) is a **rubber or plastic belt** that moves over rollers. Used in a sawmill to transport **sawdust**, **chips**, **small wood pieces** to silos or boiler rooms.

**Danger zone of the belt conveyor:**

- **The belt ends** (where the belt wraps around a pulley), **maximum entrapment**. This is where fingers, hands, legs are lost. **Never put anything in the area of a belt end.**
- **The belt's line of motion**: the belt moves fast (1–2 m/s). If it catches a loose sleeve, hair, string, it pulls in within a second.
- **Dust around the belt**: belt conveyors often raise dust. **PPE: mask + goggles** are mandatory nearby.

::: warning
**A classic accident** with a belt conveyor: a helper **has a loose overall sleeve**. The sleeve catches on the belt end. In a second **sleeve + arm + body** are pulled into the mechanism. Result: arm amputation or death. **That's why PPE** at belts is **a fitted overall**, **without loose elements**, **without jewellery**, **without long hair on the outside** (tied up, under a cap).
:::

### Grapple (manipulator)

A **grapple** (Polish: **chwytak**, or **manipulator**, or **suwnica chwytakowa**) is a **mechanical hand** hanging from an **overhead crane** (a movable bridge above the hall) that **grabs and carries** heavy logs.

The grapple looks like **two huge hooks** or **pincers** that open and close. The operator controls it from a **panel** or **the crane cabin**.

**Danger zone of the grapple:**

- **Under the moving grapple**: **NEVER**. A log weighs 500–1000 kg. If the grapple lets go (failure, error), the log falls on your head.
- **In the grapple's working area**: **NEVER**, if the operator doesn't know you are there. The grapple can hit you even without a load.
- **At the moment the grapple opens**: the log **drops by 5–20 cm**. Stand **2 metres from the placement spot**, never directly under it.

**Rule**: before entering the grapple's working area, **always make eye contact with the operator** and **get a hand sign**. Remember, the crane operator **looks down from a height of 6–8 m**. He has a **different perspective**. He may **overlook you**.

### Debarker

The **debarker** (Polish: **korowarka**, or **korowanka**) is a **rotating machine** with **many knives** that **strips bark** from logs. The log enters on rollers, the debarker **rotates around the log**, the knives remove the bark. After leaving the debarker the log is **"naked"**, ready for cutting.

**Danger zone of the debarker:**

- **Inlet/outlet of the machine**: the log enters and exits with great force. **Never put hands** into the inlet or outlet.
- **The area around the machine**: the debarker **throws pieces of bark** at high speed. They can be **fist-sized** and fly **5–10 metres**. The danger zone is **a radius of 8 metres**, with particular risk **toward the bark exit**.
- **The debarker noise**: **very high** (90–100 dB). Without hearing protection **you do not work** nearby.

::: tip
The debarker produces **the most bark** in the entire sawmill. A helper often **takes the bark** from the receiving conveyor to the bin. **Always wear goggles**: pieces of bark may fly toward the head. **Always wear gloves**: bark has **splinters** that drive into the skin.
:::

### A wood stack as a "machine"

It sounds strange but a **stack of lumber** in a sawmill behaves like a **machine**. It can:

- **Topple over** without warning (bad construction, wet wood that warped).
- **Slide a few boards** off the top layer (vibrations from machines working nearby).
- **Collapse** when a forklift **hits it** from the side (the operator didn't notice).

**Danger zone around a stack:**

- **Right next to a stack taller than your head**: danger zone.
- **In the stack's "fall line"**: if it toppled over, where would it fall? **Don't stand there.**
- **After rain or in frost**: wet wood **expands**, dry wood **shrinks**. The stack may be **unstable** on those days.

::: warning
**The most common stack collapses** happen in **two situations**: (1) **at night/morning**, when temperature changes sharply and the wood "works", the stack quietly shifts; (2) **during loading/unloading**, when a forklift **comes too close** and **shakes the stack**. A helper who **walks up to a stack in the morning** after a frosty night **first observes the stack from 5 metres away**: whether there is a board sticking out, whether the stack is straight. Only then does he come closer.
:::

## Practice link

In the second week, when you're in the hall, the foreman **will show you the yellow lines** on the floor, pedestrian zones and forklift zones. **Learn them by heart.** First task: **walk from station A to station B** using **only the pedestrian lines**. The foreman observes you. If you cross the forklift zone, he corrects you.

After two days **the forklift operator** will himself notice: "this new one always looks me in the eye before walking through". This is **a trust signal**. The operator will start **nodding to you** sooner, letting you through first.

After a week **helpers with longer experience** accept you. "We're going together to the debarker", an invitation to teamwork. **Never go alone** to the area of the grapple, debarker, or a tall stack, always **with someone who already knows it**.

## Trainer notes

**Pedagogical aim:** **awareness of motion** in the hall space. Unlike with saws (where the machine stands), here we teach **dynamic vision**: the trainee must understand that the danger zone **moves with the machines**.

**120-minute rhythm:**
- 10 min, short recap of L6 (3 saws + STOP).
- 25 min, group reading of the content with a tutor, with special emphasis on the **visibility rule** and the **forklift's blind spot**.
- 35 min, **a tour of the hall** (the best option!), showing the yellow lines, demonstrating the forklift's blind spot, locating the grapple working area. You can ask the forklift operator for a "what I see" demo (from the cabin).
- 15 min, "Key terms" section.
- 25 min, "Check yourself" + a discussion about **wood stacks**: has anyone ever seen a stack collapse.
- 10 min, buffer.

**Helpful materials:**
- **A tour of the hall**: essential. It's hard to explain the forklift blind spot without showing it.
- **A demo from the forklift cabin**: the operator invites the trainee into the cabin and shows "from where I see a pedestrian and from where I don't". This is **a very strong impression**.
- High-visibility vests (one for each trainee), so they have PPE on the tour from the start.
- A hall plan with **yellow lines** drawn on it, can be hung on the classroom wall.

**A common trainee mistake:** "**I'm fast, I'll spot the forklift**". Explain: **the forklift operator is innocent**. An accident doesn't require someone to drive badly. It's enough that simultaneously: you crouch for something, he reverses, **neither of you sees the other**. There is no "fault", there is **a faulty procedure**. The procedure: **always look around 360° before crouching in the hall**.

**Trainee questions:**
- "What if the operator hasn't noticed me and is driving toward me?", **Shout loud** ("STOP!" or whistle) and **step to the side**, never backwards (because the forklift may also be behind you). **Don't run**: it's easy to trip in a hall with sawdust.
- "What about quiet electric forklifts?", All new forklifts must have a **sound signal** (beep-beep) **when reversing**. Old forklifts do not, you have to be doubly careful.

**Practical recommendation:** organize a **"control crossroads"**: each trainee **says out loud** what he is doing as he walks the hall ("I look left, check the forklift, look right, check the stack, look ahead, walk"). After two weeks this becomes a reflex.

Tomorrow we'll learn the **difference between wet and dry wood**: because this fundamentally changes how you carry it, how you stack it, how you transport it. This closes the entire **Week 2 on the material**."
