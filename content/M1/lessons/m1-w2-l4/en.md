## Introduction

The three previous lessons showed you **ideal wood** — sapwood, heartwood, rings, clean species, even boards. Today I'll show you **real wood**.

Because real wood **is not ideal**. Every tree that grew in a forest had **its life** — it went through winds, frosts, winters, insect attacks, fungal diseases. Every log has on it **traces** of that life. We call those traces **wood defects**.

The word "defect" sounds negative, but it's a **technical** term, not a moral judgement. A wood defect is simply a **feature** that **lowers the wood's usable value**. Some defects are small (a small knot in the middle of a board — can stay). Others are big (rot reaching one metre into the wood — board for the boiler room).

In this lesson you'll learn to **recognize and name** the five most common types of defects. This is not knowledge for the quality controller — it is knowledge for **you**, the helper. Because you are the first one to take a board into your hands, hand it to the operator, or put it on the stack. **Your eye** decides whether you noticed a defect or not.

A helper who **does not see defects** is a cost to the plant. A helper who **sees and speaks** saves the plant money — and saves himself trouble.

## Goals

After this lesson:

- You recognize the five basic wood defects: **knots** (Polish: **sęki**), **splits** (Polish: **pęknięcia**), **sweep/warp** (Polish: **krzywizny**), **rot** (Polish: **zgnilizna**), **blue stain** (Polish: **zasinienie**).
- You distinguish a **sound knot** (Polish: **sęk zdrowy** — intergrown) from a **dead knot** (Polish: **sęk martwy** — loose, may fall out).
- You apply the schema **CHECK → NAME → DECIDE** for every board you take into your hands.
- You know that a defect **changes the quality class** of the board, and the quality class **changes the price** and **use** of the material.

## Content

### The schema: CHECK → NAME → DECIDE

Before we start talking about specific defects, remember the **most important thing** in this lesson. A helper who picks up a board does **three steps in his head**, **always in the same order**:

1. **CHECK** (Polish: **SPRAWDŹ**) — pass your eyes over both sides of the board and both its ends. Look for **anything that isn't smooth, straight, clean wood**.
2. **NAME** (Polish: **NAZWIJ**) — if you see something unusual, **name it in Polish** in your head: "sęk" (knot), "pęknięcie" (split), "skręt" (twist), "zgnilizna" (rot), "zasinienie" (blue stain).
3. **DECIDE** (Polish: **DECYDUJ**) — is this something you'll handle yourself (small, cosmetic — onto the stack) or something where **you call the foreman** (big, structurally dangerous).

After a month these three steps **will take you 2 seconds** per board. After half a year — **automatic**. But for now — we learn consciously, step by step.

::: tip
On your first day, when you pick up a board, **say out loud** in your head: "I'm checking… I'm naming… I'm deciding". Sounds silly? Doesn't matter. This is **the fastest method to build a habit**. After a week you'll no longer need to say it — your eyes will do it themselves.
:::

### Defect 1: Knots — traces of branches

A **knot** (Polish: **sęk**) is the place where a branch grew out of the trunk. Every knot is **a piece of a former branch** pressed into the trunk wood. Knots are **inevitable** — if there were branches on the tree, there will be knots in the boards.

Knots are divided into **two types** that look different and mean different things:

**Sound knot (intergrown)** 🟢 — Polish: **sęk zdrowy**

- Appearance: a **darker circle** of wood pressed into the board. Around the knot **there is no gap**, the wood is smoothly fused.
- Origin: the tree lived with the branch all the way to felling — the branch is **part** of the trunk wood.
- Meaning: **acceptable** in most classes. A small aesthetic defect, but does not lower strength.

**Dead knot (loose)** 🔴 — Polish: **sęk martwy**

- Appearance: a **dark circle** surrounded by a **gap**, **slot**, or **black ring** with bark remnants. Sometimes **falls out when knocked**.
- Origin: the branch **died before the tree was felled**. The wood of the branch died, but stayed pressed into the trunk.
- Meaning: **dangerous**. A dead knot can fall out of the board → hole → board for the boiler room or pallets.

::: warning
**A dead knot near the edge of a board** is **always** a signal to call the foreman. If the knot falls out during use (e.g. after the board is nailed up as cladding on a wall), there will be a **hole** there. This isn't only an aesthetic defect — in some uses (floorboards, stairs) a dead knot can cause an **accident**.
:::

### Defect 2: Splits — cracks in the wood

A **split** (Polish: **pęknięcie**) is a **crack** running along the wood's rings. It forms when **the wood dries too fast** — the outer part shrinks while the inner part hasn't yet, and tension builds up that tears the wood.

Three types of splits:

**End split** (Polish: **pęknięcie czołowe**) — at the **end of the board**, most common. Looks like a straight line going from the edge to the middle. Length: 1–30 cm.

**Side split** (Polish: **pęknięcie boczne**) — on the **side of the board** (flat surface), running lengthwise. Often along the whole length of the board.

**Frost split** (Polish: **pęknięcie mrozowe**) — occurs in some species (especially oak) when the winter has been **very frosty**. The frost "bursts" the trunk from inside. The split runs **radially** from the pith.

Splits **differ in depth**:

- **Surface** (up to 2–3 mm deep) — **cosmetic**, can be ignored.
- **Medium** (up to 1 cm) — lowers the class, but the board is still usable.
- **Deep** (going through the board) — **waste board**, goes for firewood or briquettes.

::: example
After unloading fresh lumber from the kiln, **always** check the **ends of the boards**. That's where end splits are most common — because the ends dried faster than the middle. A helper who notices "those two boards have split ends 15 cm long" reports to the foreman. The foreman either orders **the ends cut off** (the board shortens by 30 cm but you save the rest), or **sets aside** the whole board for a lower class. Without your observation **no one notices** in time.
:::

### Defect 3: Warps — a board that isn't straight

An ideal board is **straight as a ruler**. But during drying wood can **warp**. Four types of warp:

**Bow** (Polish: **sklepienie** — lengthwise bend) — the board is bent along its length like an arch. You look from the side — the middle sticks up, the ends go down.

**Twist** (Polish: **wypaczenie / skręt**) — the board is twisted around its own axis, like a **twisted sheet of paper**. One end horizontal, the other vertical.

**Cup** (Polish: **wybrzuszenie** — flat curve) — the width of the board bent into an arch. You look at it flat — the edges stick up, the middle is concave.

**Crook** (Polish: **zwężenie** — sweep) — curve to one side. The board bent like a banana, but in the plane.

A warp **is not a structural defect** — the wood is still strong. But a warped board **is hard to fit** (doesn't fit a straight wall), so it ends up in a cheaper class or in pallets.

**How to check for warp:**

- Lay the board on a flat floor. If **one corner sticks up** off the floor — there's a twist or a bow.
- Look along the board (like a rifle): if you see a curved line — there's a cup.

### Defect 4: Rot — wood that "isn't alive"

**Rot** (Polish: **zgnilizna**) is the **biological destruction of wood** by fungi. It forms when wet wood lies in warmth without ventilation. After several weeks or months the wood **loses strength** — it becomes soft, brittle, fragile.

Two types of rot:

**White rot** (Polish: **zgnilizna biała**) — the wood becomes **light, fibrous, spongy**. Most common in hardwood (oak, beech, birch).

**Brown rot** (Polish: **zgnilizna brunatna**) — the wood becomes **dark, dry, brittle as ash**. Most common in softwood (pine, spruce).

**How to recognize rot:**

- **Sight**: the wood **has changed colour** in one place (light, brown, sometimes yellowish-green).
- **Touch**: in the rotted spot the wood is **soft**. You can **push your finger into it**.
- **Knocking**: in a healthy spot the wood gives a **metallic** sound, in a rotted spot — **dull, hollow**.

::: warning
**A rotted board is NOT something for you to decide on.** You always call the foreman. Reason: a rotted board **looks** almost the same as a healthy one, but **will not hold any weight**. If it goes into the construction of a roof, wall, or stairs, then after a year or two — **the structure will collapse**. This is not "a question of class", this is **a question of safety** for the people who will live in the house made of this board.
:::

### Defect 5: Blue stain — cosmetic mould

**Blue stain** (Polish: **zasinienie**) is **blue, grey, or black spots** in the sapwood of softwood (pine, spruce). These are **staining fungi** — they live in the wood but **do not destroy the structure**.

Blue stain:

- **Occurs ONLY in sapwood** — heartwood is resistant.
- **Only changes the colour** — does not lower the board's strength.
- **Forms when fresh pine lies too long without drying** — moisture + warmth = blue mould.

This is a defect **purely visual**. In structural uses (rafters, battens) a blue-stained board is **completely OK**. In visible joinery (furniture, parquet) — **no**, because you can see it.

::: info
Blue stain is the only wood defect for which **the operator does not lower the class** by much. If you see slight blue spots on a pine board, **don't alert** the foreman. The foreman knows. Pine with slight blue stain goes into pallets, packaging, and invisible construction — and everything is fine.
:::

### Quality classes — what comes from defects

In Polish sawmills, lumber is sorted into **three or four classes**:

- **Class I (the best)** — straight board, no dead knots, no splits, no rot. Goes into furniture, floors, export.
- **Class II** — board with small sound knots, slightly warped, possible small splits. Visible building structures (rafters, trusses).
- **Class III** — board with bigger knots, clear warp, splits, blue stain. Invisible structures, pallets, packaging.
- **Waste / class IV** — board with deep splits or rot. Firewood, briquettes, compost.

**You don't sort** boards into classes. But **you receive** them sorted by the operator and **put them on the right stack**. If you see a class I board going onto the class III stack — **you say so**. If you see a class III board going onto the class I stack — **you say so all the more**.

## Practice link

In the second week, when you help with sorting or stacking lumber, **take each board slowly**. Look at **both sides** and **both ends**. If you notice anything unusual — **name it** (in your head or out loud): "dead knot near the edge", "end split 10 cm long", "rot in the corner".

After two days the foreman will notice that **you look differently** from the other new helpers. He'll start **asking you**: "and what do you think, is this board class II or class III?" Your answer doesn't have to be perfect — it's enough to **give a reason** ("the knots are sound but there's a twist, I'd say II").

After a month you may get a **real task** — initial sorting (separating waste from the rest). This is the first step of **professional advancement** in a sawmill. Helpers who don't learn to recognize defects **stay helpers**. Helpers who do learn **move on**.

## Trainer notes

**Pedagogical aim:** to build **reflexive perception of defects** + teach the schema of three steps (CHECK → NAME → DECIDE). This is the most important **metacognitive method** in W2 — the trainee is not to memorize all kinds of splits, but to **train the reflex of checking**.

**120-minute rhythm:**
- 10 min — short recap of L1+L2+L3 (anatomy + 5 species + 8 forms).
- 30 min — group reading of the content with a tutor, with special emphasis on the **three-step schema** and the **difference between sound and dead knot**.
- 35 min — **practical exercise**: 8–10 board pieces with various defects (prepared before the lesson). Each participant takes a board, performs **out loud** the schema "I'm checking… I see a dead knot in the right corner… I name it: dead knot… I decide: foreman". The other participants confirm or correct. The trainer moderates.
- 15 min — "Key terms" section — saying the Polish names out loud.
- 20 min — "Check yourself" + a discussion: **why a helper decides and not just carries**.
- 10 min — buffer.

**Helpful materials (essential!):**
- **8–10 board pieces** 50–80 cm long, with **real defects**: 2 with knots (one sound, one dead), 2 with splits (one end, one side), 2 with bow/twist, 1 with rot (BHP warning — wear gloves!), 1 with blue stain, 1–2 clean boards (for contrast).
- These boards are **ideally provided by the plant foreman** — ask trainers in advance to prepare samples.
- Magnifying glass (×4) — to show a sound knot up close.
- A small hammer or wrench — for **knocking** (acoustic test of rot).

**Common trainee mistake: confusing blue stain with rot.** Explain clearly:
- Blue stain = **colour** (blue, cosmetic, NOT structural).
- Rot = **state** (soft wood, losing strength, structural).

One test: **push a fingernail in**. Blue stain — the nail **does not go in**. Rot — the nail **goes into the wood like into a sponge**.

**Trainee questions:**
- "Can I throw out a board with a hole on my own?" — No. The foreman decides. It might be that this "hole" is a **knot that fell out after cutting**, which can be claimed back from the supplier. You inform; the foreman decides.
- "Are the classes the same in all sawmills?" — The classification is standardized (PN-EN 1611), but **specific thresholds** can differ between plants. The foreman will show you **the internal standard** of your sawmill.

**Practical recommendation:** **don't show all five defects** in the first practical part. Better show 2–3 defects **very well** than 5 defects **superficially**. The rest will come with time in the yard.
