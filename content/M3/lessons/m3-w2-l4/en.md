## Content

### Three perspectives on C classes: operator, grader, client

The same four strength classes (C16, C24, C30, C35) have **three different meanings** depending on the role from which the wood is viewed:

**The operator's perspective (Mykoła, autonomous operator P3):**

The C class is the **result of selection at cutting**. A log enters the saw, sawing produces 100 boards. The operator decides how to saw in order to maximise the number of boards meeting C24 (the most expensive standard class ordered by the client). The choice of cutting orientation, the band width, the kerf thickness, all of this affects C24 yield. Good sawing: 100 boards give 40 C24 (40% yield in the target class) plus 45 C16 (a lower class, lower price, but we sell it) plus 10 below C16 (for packaging, offcuts) plus 5 rejects (rot, cracks). Poor sawing: 100 boards give 25 C24, more waste. A yield difference = a revenue difference for the sawmill.

**The grader's perspective (Mrs. Ania):**

The C class is the **result of measurement** under PN-EN 14081-3. The grader takes a board, measures the ring width, the slope of grain, the location of knots in the cross-section, and cracks. On the basis of those five indicators she assigns a class. The class is objective (the standard), not subjective. The grader cannot "throw in a class" for the client for a surcharge, because the class results from measurements, not from discretion.

**The client's perspective (Mr. Jacek, technologist, designer, investor):**

The C class is the **input to the structural calculation**. The designer has a computer program (Dlubal RFEM, Autodesk Robot, Cadwork Lignum) or an Excel sheet with Eurocode 5 formulas. He enters: the beam span, the cross-section, the dead load (the self-weight of the structure), the live load (occupants, furniture, snow), the safety coefficients. The program calculates whether the beam **will bear up** (the MOR condition with a reserve) and whether it **will not deflect too much** (the MOE condition with a reserve). If the conditions are met for C24, the client orders C24. If not met, the client changes either the class (to C30), or the cross-section (a larger beam), or the span (an additional support). The C class is one of three variables.

The three perspectives produce **the same C24 class**, but look at it from different sides:
- Operator: "how to arrange things so that the log yields as much C24 as possible"
- Grader: "does this specific board meet the criteria for C24"
- Client: "does C24 cover my structural need"

::: info
At the sawmill nobody looks from all three perspectives **simultaneously**. The operator thinks about cutting, the grader about boards, the client about the building. That is why **translators** exist: the foreman, the sales representative, the grader in a conversation with a client. Each one translates in a single direction. Mrs. Ania, talking with Mr. Jacek, translates from the grader's perspective into the client's perspective. The foreman (Marek) translates from the client's perspective into the operator's perspective (he passes the order on to Mykoła). You, Mykoła, as an autonomous operator in M3, should understand all three perspectives, even if you yourself operate from only one.
:::

### Eurocode 5 as the framework for the client's decision

**PN-EN 1995 (Eurocode 5)** is the European standard for designing timber structures. It consists of three parts:

- **PN-EN 1995-1-1:** General rules and requirements for buildings.
- **PN-EN 1995-1-2:** General rules and fire resistance.
- **PN-EN 1995-2:** Timber bridges.

Eurocode 5 is **mandatory** for all construction projects in Poland since 2010 (previously PN-B-03150 was applied, withdrawn in 2010 in favour of the Eurocode). A designer of timber structures (architect, structural engineer, engineer) must design to Eurocode 5, otherwise the project will not receive building permission.

**What Eurocode 5 requires from the designer:**

1. **Verification of the capacity condition** (ULS, *Ultimate Limit State*): will the beam carry the load **without fracture**? He calculates the bending stress in the beam and compares it with the strength of the C class (after applying partial and modifying coefficients).
2. **Verification of the serviceability condition** (SLS, *Serviceability Limit State*): will the beam **not deflect too much**? He calculates the deflection under load and compares it with the permissible maximum (1/300 of length for residential floor beams, 1/200 for roof trusses).

Both conditions must be satisfied. Most often **it is the deflection condition** (SLS, not ULS) that decides the choice of C class. A C16 beam can carry the load (MOR sufficient), but it deflects so much (low MOE) that the floor creaks, the ceiling cracks, the doors warp. A C24 beam has the same strength as C16 with a reserve, but the deflection is significantly smaller (MOE 11,000 vs 8000 N/mm²).

**For Mr. Jacek and a residential floor of 4.2 m span:**

Eurocode 5 calculation (simplified; in the full project this is a computer program):
- Span L = 4.2 m.
- Beam cross-section 50 × 150 mm, class C24, MOE = 11,000 N/mm².
- Dead load g = 0.5 kN/m (floor weight + beam self-weight).
- Live load q = 2.0 kN/m² × 0.6 m (beam spacing) = 1.2 kN/m.
- Total load p = 1.7 kN/m.
- Maximum deflection (formula for a single-span beam under uniform load): f = 5·p·L⁴ / (384·E·I), where I = b·h³/12 = 50·150³/12 = 14,062,500 mm⁴.
- f = 5 × 1.7 × 4200⁴ / (384 × 11,000 × 14,062,500) = about 11 mm.
- Permissible deflection 4200/300 = 14 mm.
- **Condition satisfied with a 21% reserve.**

For C30 (MOE 12,000 N/mm²): deflection 10 mm, reserve 29%. For C35 (MOE 13,000): deflection 9 mm, reserve 36%.

Conclusion: **for this configuration C24 is enough**, the safety margin is healthy. Choosing C30 gives only 1 mm less deflection for an additional price. Choosing C35 has no structural justification.

### Four C classes from the client's perspective: application in practice

**Class C16 (economy, budget):**

- **Typical applications at the client:**
  - **Economy roof structures**: budget detached houses, truss spans up to 6 m with a standard rafter spacing of 90 cm, snow load zones I-III (Warsaw, Lublin, Lubuskie), roof not intended for attic use.
  - **Shuttering boards**: formwork for reinforced concrete floors and walls on building sites. Treated timber, used 10-20 times, then for fuel.
  - **Export pallets**: EUR pallets, ISPM-15 fumigated, mass production, low transport loads.
  - **Garden furniture**: benches, tables, terrace decking (if protected).
  - **Temporary structures**: scaffolding, concert stages, construction fences.

- **What the client gets for the price**: about 1300-1500 zł/m³ at EGIDA (May 2026). Fresh or pre-dried pine, visual grading G2-3/C16, no geometric selection (typical curvature).

**Class C24 (the Polish standard, 50-55% of structural production):**

- **Typical applications:**
  - **Residential floors**: detached and terraced houses, residential live load 2 kN/m², spans up to 4.5 m without intermediate support, dynamic load from walking.
  - **Standard roof structures**: detached houses with a usable attic (rooms on the upper storey within the roof structure), snow load in all zones, spans up to 8 m in prefabricated trusses.
  - **Balcony structures**: cantilever up to 1.5 m, live load 4 kN/m² (balcony), moisture-resistance requirement additionally through impregnation.
  - **Structural timber stairs**: stringers, supporting beams of steps, live load 2.5-3 kN/m².
  - **Light hall structures**: warehouses, barns, spans up to 10 m in trusses.

- **What the client gets**: about 1800-1950 zł/m³. Pine or spruce timber dried to 18% (±2%), visual grading G2-1 or G2-2, strength grading C24, an accredited grader signs the KLS-001 card, the timber is marked on the end with the code "C24 ↓" (the arrow indicates the lower tension face).

**Class C30 (reinforced floors, 15-20% of production):**

- **Typical applications:**
  - **Floors in multi-family buildings**: multi-storey terraced houses, two-storey apartment buildings, dynamic load from many users, acoustic requirement (sound insulation demands a massive structure).
  - **Light production halls**: artisan workshops, assembly halls, spans 10-15 m, roof load above 2 kN/m² (suspended equipment, ventilation ducts).
  - **Point-loaded beams**: lintels over window or door openings, load-bearing girders, balcony beams with a long cantilever (above 1.5 m).
  - **Stairs for public-use buildings**: kindergartens, schools, cultural centres, live load 4-5 kN/m².
  - **Modular structures**: prefabricated houses, timber container modules, transport-stability requirement.

- **What the client gets**: about 2200-2350 zł/m³ at EGIDA. Pine or spruce from habitats with slower growth (pine from the Tucholskie Forests, from dry habitats), dried to 18% (±2%), visual selection G2-1, strength grading C30 (accredited grader). The price is 20-25% higher than C24 on account of the **cost of selection** (only 15-20% of production meets C30 criteria).

**Class C35 (glued-laminated and high-load structures, 5-8% of production):**

- **Typical applications:**
  - **Glued-laminated timber** (*glued laminated timber*, GL24h, GL28h, GL32h): lamellas 30-45 mm thick glued with polyurethane or melamine adhesive into large-format beams (lengths up to 30 m). Application: sports halls, swimming pools, churches, timber bridges, load-bearing elements of long-span roofs.
  - **Structures with a long cantilever**: balconies 2-3 m without support, parking canopies, shelters with large overhangs.
  - **Timber bridges**: pedestrian footbridges, agricultural bridges, arched elements.
  - **Exhibition and temporary structures requiring reliability**: trade-fair pavilions, large-scale scenographic elements.

- **What the client gets**: about 2900-3100 zł/m³. Mainly **larch** (higher density) or pine from old-growth stands, dried to 14% (lower moisture required for gluing), the strictest selection (less than 8% of production qualifies). Often delivered in the form of lamellas 30 × 150 × 6000 mm, not as beams.

::: tip
Mr. Jacek, as a technologist, often gets the question from clients: **"Why not go straight to C30 or C35, it would be safest?"**. The technical answer: safety does not depend on the C class, only on the reserve in meeting the Eurocode 5 conditions. A C24 beam with a 21% reserve (as in Mr. Jacek's case) is **just as safe** as a C30 beam with a 30% reserve or a C35 beam with a 40% reserve. A reserve above 15% is sufficient for residential loads. Choosing a class higher than the minimum required is a **cost without a structural benefit**. Answer for the client: "Let us use a sufficient class, let us spend the saved money on better insulation or windows with a better U-value."
:::

### Economics of class choice: a specific example of a detached-house floor

Let us take the floor of a detached house from Mr. Jacek's project (a two-family house, floor over the ground floor, span 4.2 m, load 2 kN/m², beam spacing 0.6 m). The floor measures 10 × 8 m, so we need 10 m / 0.6 m ≈ 17 beams 8 m long. But 8 m beams are not standard (the standard length of Polish sawn timber is 4-6 m), so the project assumes 17 beams × 2 spans = 34 beams 4.2 m long with support in the middle (a structural partition wall).

Timber volume: 34 beams × 50 × 150 × 4200 mm = 34 × 0.0315 m³ = **1.07 m³**. For safety (trim, waste, reserve) we order 1.2 m³.

**Cost for three class variants:**

| Class | EGIDA price (May 2026) | Cost for 1.2 m³ | Difference from C24 |
|-------|-------------------------|------------------|----------------------|
| C16 | 1400 zł/m³ | 1680 zł | −480 zł |
| **C24** | **1850 zł/m³** | **2220 zł** | **base** |
| C30 | 2300 zł/m³ | 2760 zł | +540 zł |
| C35 | 3000 zł/m³ | 3600 zł | +1380 zł |

**Interpretation:**

The C24 vs C30 cost difference for one floor = **540 zł**. In the full budget of building a detached house (on average 700,000 zł in Poland, May 2026) this is 0.08%. The client who sent the project to the architect to swap C24 for C30 pays little for it. But **for EGIDA** this difference of 540 zł per floor × 15 floors per month = 8100 zł per month in higher margin. Because EGIDA already has C30 in stock (from earlier selection); only, not every client asks about it.

**When the client chooses C16 instead of C24** (savings of 480 zł):
- Economy roof structures without a usable attic. Roof beams do not deflect significantly (snow load is short-term; Eurocode 5 allows greater deflection).
- Timber load-bearing walls with only vertical load (compression, not bending).
- Floorboards for joisting (if the joists are sufficiently dense, the beams are not loaded in bending).

**When the client stays with C24** (the standard choice):
- All residential floors.
- Standard roof structures with a usable attic.
- Single balconies, domestic stairs.
- Structures of span up to 4.5 m without intermediate support.

**When the client chooses C30** (surcharge of 540 zł):
- Beam spans above 4.5 m without intermediate support.
- Live load above 3 kN/m² (libraries, archives, shops with compact shelving).
- Floors in multi-family buildings (apartment buildings, acoustic requirements + vibration resistance).
- Structures with a long service life and limited access for replacement (roofs with ceramic coverings, floors under concrete screed).

**When the client chooses C35** (surcharge of 1380 zł, plus the need to order specially):
- Glued-laminated structures (requirement of the GL manufacturer).
- Timber bridges, pedestrian footbridges.
- Sports halls and coverings with spans above 15 m.
- Infrastructural elements with a reliability requirement higher than that of buildings.

### Economics from EGIDA's perspective: why we offer all classes

From the sawmill's perspective, all C classes **cannot not be offered**. A pine log coming into EGIDA yields in sawing a **natural distribution** of classes, not only one:

Typical distribution from sawing Scots pine from the Drawno Forest District (fertile habitat, moderately rapid growth, age 80-100 years):
- C35: 3% of boards (boards from the central heartwood, dense, narrow ring)
- C30: 15% of boards (boards from the central heartwood and sapwood)
- C24: 45% of boards (boards from the mixed heartwood-sapwood zone, the dominant class)
- C16: 25% of boards (boards from the outer sapwood, wider ring)
- Below C16: 10% of boards (edge boards, with defects, for packaging)
- Rejects: 2% of boards (rot, cracks, rejection)

EGIDA has to **sell each class** for the yield to be profitable. You cannot throw out 15% of production as C30 because no one wants it. You cannot hold 45% of C24 in stock, because it will tie up capital. That is why **the sales representatives at EGIDA actively promote different classes to different clients**: C16 to shuttering firms and pallet makers, C24 to typical roofers and detached-house developers, C30 to multi-family developers and hall builders, C35 to gluing workshops (GL) and bridge builders.

**For Mykoła as operator this means:**

When Marek says: "this morning we are cutting pine for the C24 order for Dom-Bud Piaseczno", Mykoła knows that **in addition to C24** C16, C30 and C35 will also come out. He must be able to sort the boards from one cutting cycle into three or four class stacks. He will not waste C30 boards (throwing them onto the C24 stack because C24 was ordered), because C30 sells to another client at a higher price. He will not throw C16 into C24 "because it looks similar", because C16 on a floor would mean a complaint.

Sorting at cutting = visual **pre-grading** (the operator sees knots, ring width, curvature), and then the grader (Ania) **confirms objectively**. A good operator can predict, with 90% accuracy, which class will come out of each board. That saves the grader time: Ania confirms, rather than working it out from scratch.

### Summary of lesson four

The strength classes of timber (C16, C24, C30, C35) are the same four classes from the operator's, the grader's, and the client's perspective, but each perspective sees them differently. The operator looks at yield at cutting, the grader at measurements against the standard, the client at the application in a structural project.

The client chooses the class according to Eurocode 5 (PN-EN 1995): verification of the capacity condition (ULS, whether the beam will not fracture under load) and the serviceability condition (SLS, whether the beam will not deflect too much). Most often the SLS condition decides, because the MOE of class C16 is too low for residential floors.

The four classes have typical applications: C16 for economy trusses, shuttering boards and pallets; C24 for standard residential floors and trusses (50-55% of Polish production); C30 for loaded floors (multi-family, halls, stairs, cantilevered balconies); C35 for glued-laminated timber and large-span structures (bridges, sports halls).

Economically, the C24 vs C30 difference for a detached-house floor is about 540 zł (0.08% of the budget). A hybrid solution (85% C24 + 15% C30 for critical zones: stairs, balconies, lintels) gives the optimal cost-safety ratio. The client chooses C30 globally only for projects with systemically greater loads (multi-family, halls). C35 only for specialised projects (glued-laminated, bridges).

The grader's second language (in conversation with the client) is the **translation of the standard's numbers into a decision**: MOR 24 N/mm² becomes "the beam will carry 3.2 tons", MOE 11,000 becomes "it will deflect 11 mm, the plaster will not crack", density 350 kg/m³ becomes "the beam weighs 16.8 kg/m, it can be handled by hand". The grader's questions towards the client are not about the standard but about critical zones: stairs, balconies, lintels, spans above 4.5 m, loads above 3 kN/m². Those zones are the **actual places** of choice between classes.

The autonomous operator (Mykoła) **does not advise the client** on the choice of class. He redirects to the grader or the sales representative. But he understands the three perspectives on the classes, because in his daily work (sorting at cutting) he has to be able to predict which class will come out of which board. A good operator has 90% accuracy in visual pre-grading.

In the next lesson (m3-w2-l5) we move on to **the interpretation of moisture measurement** in the context of the client's order. Three target thresholds (8-10% furniture, 12-15% joinery, 18% roof structure), five measurement points on the stack, the operator's decision: do we release the batch to the client or dry it further in the chamber.
