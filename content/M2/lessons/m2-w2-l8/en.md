## Introduction

Monday, 7:20. Thirty minutes after Sofía showed foreman Marek the gullet crack on band saw #2, senior operator Tadeusz (52 years old, 15 years of service, UDT certified) is standing at the machine. Marek has brought a spare Wood-Mizer Silvertip band from the warehouse (51 mm wide, 1.07 mm thick, production date 2026-02-14 – two months ago). On the table: a 4 mm Allen key, a dial gauge with a magnetic base, a headlamp, a rag. Tadeusz puts on leather gloves (he will take them off before startup – no gloves near rotating parts, the rule from L2 W1). Marek looks at Sofía: "Today you stay and watch. You don't touch, you don't hand over anything I haven't asked for. You memorize every step. In six to twelve months you will do this yourself, but before that you will watch it ten times."

This lesson is **the first machine-level interaction** for the trainee in Module 2. Until now you have looked at the material (moisture, logs, defects, assortments, grades) and at the tool from the outside (four types of saws, signs of wear). Now you **step within arm's reach of a mechanical replacement**, but in the role of **witness and helper**, not operator. The lesson mantra: **"You never change it alone."** Even after six months, even after a year, even after completing the procedure on your own, the first replacement is always under supervision, and every one after that is always in the presence of the foreman or a second operator.

Band replacement is a **seven-step procedure** that you observe today and that in a few months you will be performing yourself. You will learn three **professional numbers of the trade**: tension 2200–2400 PSI, guide rollers 3–4 mm, 30 seconds of no-load run before the test cut. These numbers are checked by the foreman during the internal competency test at 3 and 6 months. If you don't know them, you don't pass.

## Objectives

After this lesson:

1. You understand the **assisting role of the junior operator** during band replacement: witness plus handing over tools on explicit request. You do not touch the band, the guards, the nuts, or the tensioner. You memorize the procedure with your eyes and with the hand that passes the tools.
2. You know the **seven steps of the band replacement procedure** on a band saw: LOTO and lockout → opening the guards → removing the worn band → inspection and mounting of the new one → setting the tension → setting the guide rollers → no-load run and test cut with a register entry.
3. You know the **three professional numbers of the trade**: band tension **2200–2400 PSI** (152–166 MPa, measured with the tension gauge), guide rollers **3–4 mm** from the band (measured with a dial gauge or a feeler gauge), **30 seconds of no-load run** before the band first contacts wood.
4. You understand what **LOTO** is (Lockout/Tagout): placing your personal padlock (with your name) on the main disconnect of the machine and a tag showing the start time of the replacement. While the padlock is in place, **no one else can start the machine**, which is a mechanical guarantee of safety for the person working inside the guards.
5. You know how to recognize **a good spare band in the warehouse crate**: production date within 12 months, no corrosion marks, a correct factory weld (visible as a line 0.5–1 mm wider than the rest of the band, with no incomplete fusion), factory tooth set.
6. You know the **role of the band welder** (Brazilon or Wood-Mizer): a device used to weld cracked bands, operated by an experienced operator or in an external service workshop. At the EGIDA Foundation sawmill the band welder is located in the workshop, but operating it is a Module 3 competency, not a Module 2 one.
7. You apply the principle **"you never change it alone"**: the first independent replacement is carried out under the foreman's supervision after 6–12 months and confirmation of competency. Every subsequent replacement is always in the presence of a second person (foreman or senior operator).

## Content

### Why you assist instead of replacing

Band replacement requires **UDT certification** (Office of Technical Inspection, UDT) for operating sawmill saws. Regulation of the Ministry of Economy of 2000 (Rozporządzenie MG 2000), § 3, states explicitly: machinery and equipment with high accident risk may be operated only by a person trained and authorized by the employer. Band replacement equals removing the guards, plus handling a rotating element (even though stopped), plus setting parameters that affect operational safety. These are not activities for an operator in the first months on the job.

But assisting, watching, handing over tools, memorizing, is **part of your path to certification**. After 3 months you begin assisting, after 6 months you perform the procedure under active supervision (Tadeusz stands next to you and comments on every step), after 12 months you pass the internal test and replace the band yourself, but **never without a second person nearby**.

::: info
At the EGIDA Foundation sawmill, a band replacement takes 25–35 minutes. Frequency: when cutting oak, every day; when cutting pine, every week (see Lesson 7, band life table). That adds up to 150–200 replacements per year. Each one is documented in the **band change log** (Lesson 5 of Week 4 in Module 1, station register) with the entry: date, time, band number, reason for replacement, who replaced it, who assisted.
:::

### The seven steps of the procedure – what you see while assisting

**Step 1: Stopping the machine and LOTO (lockout).** Tadeusz presses STOP (the emergency stop mushroom, remember from Lesson 3 of Week 1). He waits 15 seconds for the band to release the energy of the no-load run (coast-down of 8–12 s with no load, Lesson 7 of Week 1). Then he opens the box of the main disconnect (gray-green, on the left side of the saw), turns the handle to the OFF position, and **places his own padlock**, with the number 12 and the name "Tadeusz Kowalski" engraved on it. Next to it he hangs a cardboard tag: "BAND CHANGE – 7:22 – DO NOT START". While the padlock is in place, only Tadeusz can remove it. His rollers, his wrench, his risk.

**Step 2: Opening the guards.** The Wood-Mizer LT40 saw has two guards: an upper one (about 900 mm wide) over the upper tensioning wheel, and a lower one (about 700 mm) over the lower drive wheel. Tadeusz unscrews 4 M8 bolts with a 6 mm Allen key, lifts the upper guard, and hangs it on a dedicated structural hook (he does not put it on the floor, because of the risk of it falling on someone's foot). The lower guard works the same way. **Your role**: you pass the Allen key with a clear motion, handle first towards Tadeusz (never the blade or the working end). You memorize the position of the bolts, so that when a new guard is mounted you will know where Tadeusz is looking.

**Step 3: Removing the worn band.** Tadeusz loosens the tensioner (red lever on the right side of the upper wheel, counter-clockwise rotation, 3–4 full turns), and tension drops to zero. The band goes slack. Tadeusz grips it at three points (leather gloves, not mechanical ones, because leather gives a better grip) and **coils it into a triple loop** (the "three circles" technique, about 600 mm in diameter, because a 4500 mm band fits into a triple loop). The worn band goes into a red crate labeled "FOR DISPOSAL / FOR WELDING". The foreman decides whether a band with a gullet crack can be repaired on the welder (usually not, because a gullet crack indicates fatigue of the whole band, not a point defect) or whether it goes to scrap.

::: warning
Even a removed, slack band is dangerous. The 1.07 mm spring steel retains memory, and a loose band "snaps back" to its original shape. If not properly held, it can hit you in the face or the arm. Senior operators accounted for 4% of recorded cuts reported to the State Labor Inspectorate (PIP) in 2019–2023 during this phase of replacement. That is why there are three grip points and a triple loop, not one-handed handling. Your role as an assistant: you stand 2 meters from the saw, **you do not enter the zone** until the band is in the disposal crate.
:::

**Step 4: Inspection and mounting of the new band.** Tadeusz opens the cardboard box, takes out the new band (still in its factory loop), and places it on the inspection table. **Four things he checks with a headlamp**:
1. Production date on the sticker, no older than 12 months (a band sitting in the warehouse loses springiness due to moisture and oxidation).
2. No corrosion marks (rust spots on the edges and near the weld).
3. **Factory weld** – a single line 0.5–1 mm wider than the rest of the band, ideally perpendicular to the edge, with no incomplete fusion (black line in the middle) or burn-through (bluish tint). The weld is the weakest point of the band in 15–20% of fatigue cracks, so inspecting the factory weld is worth 30 seconds.
4. Factory tooth set (a zigzag visible from the side). If the teeth look "flat", the band was packed badly or mechanically compressed in storage.

After inspection Tadeusz unwinds the band from the loop (three circles → one circle → straight band) and places it on the saw wheels: first on the upper, then on the lower, teeth pointing **in the direction of cutting** (in the direction of rotation of the lower drive wheel). Initial setting: the band is loose, touching the wheels but not tensioned.

**Step 5: Setting the tension at 2200–2400 PSI.** Tadeusz turns the tensioner (red lever) clockwise. On the upper wheel of the saw there is a **tension gauge** (round dial about 80 mm in diameter, scale 0–3500 PSI). Tadeusz raises the tension slowly, watching the needle. He stops at **2300 PSI** (the middle of the 2200–2400 band). Too low: the band oscillates during cutting and the cut goes off line. Too high: the band breaks at the weld or in a gullet after a few hours. The middle of the band is the professional value. Not the minimum, not the maximum.

::: tip
Trainer: "Tension of 2300 PSI equals 156 MPa, which corresponds to a tensile force of about 30,000 newtons across the 51 × 1.07 mm cross-section of the band. For comparison, a 10 mm steel cable on a crane works at 40,000 newtons. A band saw is in the same mechanical class as a crane cable, it just lasts shorter under that load. That is why tension is the professional number you remember forever: 2200 minimum, 2400 maximum, 2300 middle of the band."
:::

**Step 6: Setting the guide rollers at 3–4 mm.** The Wood-Mizer saw has **four guide rollers** for the band: two upper (near the upper wheel) and two lower (near the working table, just before and after the cutting point). The rollers keep the band on the cutting axis, because without them the band would drift under the pressure of the wood. Distance from the roller to the back of the band: **3–4 mm**. Too close (below 3 mm): the roller rubs against the band, heats up, and causes premature wear. Too far (above 4 mm): the band has lateral play and the cut becomes wavy.

Tadeusz uses a **dial gauge** with a magnetic base. He attaches the magnet to the body of the saw, slides the probe between the roller and the band, and reads the value. He adjusts the roller mounting screw (4 mm Allen key, several movements of 0.5 mm each) and checks again. He repeats the procedure for all four rollers. **Your role**: you pass the dial gauge and record the readings in the replacement notebook (upper front, upper rear, lower front, lower rear, four numbers, each 3.0–4.0 mm).

**Step 7: No-load run and test cut.** Tadeusz closes the guards (4 M8 bolts each, 6 mm Allen key), removes the LOTO padlock and tag from the main disconnect (he writes the removal time on the tag, closing the documentation loop). He turns the handle to the ON position. He returns to the operator panel, visually checks the guards (everything closed, all bolts in place), and announces loudly: **"Starting saw number two, no-load run thirty seconds!"** (a warning to everyone in the hall). He presses START. The band spins up to working speed (30–50 m/s, 2 seconds of run-up). Tadeusz stands by the panel and listens: no squeak, no knock, an even whistling sound. 30 seconds of no-load run is a sufficient test for sudden hidden faults (80% of no-load-run failures become apparent in the first 10 seconds).

Then comes the **test cut**: a pine waste board (every sawmill keeps these in a separate crate labeled "FOR TESTING"), cutting through 30–50 cm. Tadeusz checks the cut surface with his finger (this time wearing a glove, since the band is already stopped): as smooth as 120-grit sandpaper, no waviness, no dark burn marks. If OK, the machine returns to production. If not, the replacement continues (tension adjustment, rollers, another no-load run).

**Register entry**: Tadeusz writes in the sawmill replacement notebook (the same one you learned about in Lesson 5 of Week 4 in Module 1): "07:22–07:54, saw #2, Wood-Mizer Silvertip band, series 2026-02-14, reason: 3 mm gullet crack (detected by: Sofía S., morning walk-around), replaced by: Tadeusz K., assisted by: Sofía S., tension: 2300 PSI, rollers: 3.2 / 3.1 / 3.4 / 3.3 mm, test cut: OK."

### Professional parameters – remember three numbers

Band replacement generates three numbers that form the **professional identity** of a band saw operator in Poland:

| Parameter | Value | Tolerance | Measurement tool |
|---|---|---|---|
| Band tension | 2300 PSI | 2200–2400 | tension gauge |
| Guide rollers | 3.5 mm | 3.0–4.0 | dial gauge / feeler gauge |
| No-load run | 30 seconds | min. 30 s | stopwatch or panel counter |

The foreman checks your knowledge of these numbers during the internal competency test at 3 months ("a junior should know them, even if he doesn't set them"), at 6 months ("he should know how to measure them, not adjust them"), and at 12 months ("he should know how to set them under supervision"). If you don't know them, you don't get promoted.

## Link to practice

**Exercise 1 (first assists, 3 times in the first month):** After this lesson you join as an assistant for the next three band replacements on the shop floor. Rule: **you stand 2 meters from the saw, you pass tools on explicit request**, and you note every step and every number in your personal notebook. After the third replacement you know the procedure from memory, not perfectly, but you know the order and the pace.

**Exercise 2 (professional parameters, 5 min per week):** For the first month, once a week, the foreman will ask: "Band tension, the band?" "Guide rollers, how much?" "No-load run, minimum how long?" You answer from memory with no notes. Three numbers, three seconds, no hesitation. This is not a knowledge test, this is **professional automatism**.

**Exercise 3 (replacement log, after every assist):** After every replacement you assisted on, you write in your personal notebook (not the sawmill register, Tadeusz writes in that one) the full record: date, time, band type, parameters, comments. After 5 entries Tadeusz will check your notebook and comment: "Here the band number is missing, here the lower rollers are missing, here it's OK." That is how you learn to make entries in the sawmill register, before you start doing it yourself.

## Trainer notes

**Pedagogical goal**: the first machine-level interaction of the trainee in M2. Not performance, but assistance: witness plus handing over tools. Goal: **to make the numerical parameters familiar** (2200–2400 PSI, 3–4 mm, 30 s) and to frame **the assisting role** as a stage on the path to autonomy. The mantra "you never change it alone" is a safety compass that reaches beyond Module 2.

**Week 2 bracket**: Sofía opens the week in L1 (moisture content, Monday 7:30) and closes it in L8 (band replacement, Monday 7:20 of the following week). Eight lessons, one narrative arc: observation of the material → observation of the tool → assistance at the machine. Pedagogically strong: the trainee sees the coherence of the course, not a collection of episodes.

**Three numbers of the trade**: 2200–2400 PSI, 3–4 mm, 30 s. Repeat them three times in the lesson (in the objectives, in steps 5–7, in the table). This is not didactic redundancy, it is **imprinting automatism**. Trainees from cultures where a manual worker does not normally "re-ask" a superior (much of Latin America, with the "usted" relationship toward the foreman; parts of regions of Ukraine) often do not ask if they miss something, so the key numbers must appear multiple times in the lesson, not just once.

**Pitfalls in delivery**:
1. No tension physics (Young's modulus, fatigue theory). A junior has to know "2200–2400 PSI, tension gauge, middle of the band 2300", nothing more.
2. Do not downplay LOTO. For trainees from countries with lower occupational-safety standards, a padlock on a disconnect sounds like bureaucracy. Show that it is a mechanical guarantee, not a formality.
3. Do not confuse "assistance" with "performance". A trainee after L8 cannot change a band, but can see one changed, knows the parameters, and can hand over a tool.

**Integration with previous lessons**: L2 W1 (no gloves near rotating parts) → Tadeusz takes off his gloves before startup. L3 W1 (STOP, emergency mushroom) → Step 1. L7 W1 (5-step failure protocol) → L8 is a different protocol, a 7-step planned procedure. L5 W4 M1 (documentation) → Step 7, register entry. L7 W2 M2 → Sofía detected the problem → Tadeusz replaces the band, closing the bracket.

**Preview of M3**: in Module 3 Week 2 the trainee learns the independent replacement (the same 7 steps, now he performs them under active supervision). Plus welding of a cracked band on a Brazilon band welder.

**If a trainee asks "when on my own?"**: the answer is conditional, not time-based. After 6–12 months, usually closer to 12. Condition: passing the internal competency test (7 steps, 3 numbers, LOTO, register). Pass, and you replace the band yourself, but always in the presence of a second person. Fail, and you keep assisting, with a retake of the test in 3 months.
