## Introduction

Friday, 8:00 a.m., the kiln hall of the EGIDA Foundation, chamber 2. Ten days have passed since Tuesday, when Oleksii Bohdaniuk loaded batch SO-A-260420-012 into the chamber (pine class A, 50 × 150 × 3000 mm, 6 m³, 12 boards in total). Kiln master Zbigniew Majchrzak closed the cycle at 4:00 a.m. – the temperature dropped from 55°C to 23°C over 7 hours of gradient cooling (a rate of 4.6°C/h, safely below the 6°C/h limit). Now Oleksii stands in front of the chamber door with Zbigniew. "You loaded it yourself, you unload it yourself, you check it yourself," Zbigniew says. "I am assisting, but the class decisions are yours. Remember: class A before drying does not mean class A after drying. The material has matured, now we will see what has become of it."

This Friday closes the **Week 3 arc** for Oleksii – from Monday (l6), when he sorted the same batch in the yard, through Tuesday (l7), when he loaded it into the chamber, to Friday (l8), when he unloads and reclassifies it. The same operator, the same batch, but **different states** of the same lumber. Drying is not a process indifferent to wood – it **changes the class**. A board that had a minor end check at 1/4 of its width before drying may have 1/3 of its width after drying (because desorption stresses tore the fibres where they were weakest). This is not a drying error, this is the **nature of the wood** – an organic material reacts to a change in moisture with deformation and cracking, and part of the operator's role is to **accept this change and update the documentation**, not to try to hide it.

The Week 3 mantra from lesson 1 goes: "I start, I adjust, I cut, I sort – but always under the supervision of a foreman or a senior." In lesson 8 we add the final activities of the week: **I measure, I assess, I reclassify, I update**. You are no longer in an executive role, you are in the role of a **verifier** who confirms or corrects the earlier classification decision.

## Objectives

After this lesson:

1. You check **three parameters that close the drying cycle** on the controller panel before opening the chamber: (a) **equalised temperature** (the difference between the chamber interior and the hall is less than 3°C – otherwise opening will cause a thermal shock to the boards), (b) **air humidity** in the chamber has dropped to 35–45% (similar to the hall humidity), (c) **residual wood moisture** from the in-chamber probes of 10–15% (rough, verified precisely with a hand-held moisture meter after unloading). Only when the three parameters agree does the kiln master authorise the opening.
2. You measure the batch moisture with a **resistance moisture meter** (from lesson 2 of week 2 in module 2 – electrodes driven perpendicular to the fibres, range 6–30% MC) in **four batch positions** of the A/B/C/D system: **A** the topmost board in the stack, 500 mm from the end, **B** the topmost board, mid-length, **C** the middle board of the stack, 500 mm from the end, **D** the bottommost board, mid-length. The average of the four measurements is the batch moisture. **A spread between the 4 measurements greater than 2% MC** = the cycle was not uniform, reclassification or additional drying.
3. You assess **surface checks** (visible from the outside, 0.5–2 mm wide, 20–200 mm long) and **end checks** (at the ends of the board, formed during too-rapid evaporation of vapour from the end grain) according to **two class scales**: (a) surface checks – class A allows up to **50 mm of length per running metre** of the board, class B up to 100 mm/rm, class C without an upper limit; (b) end checks – class A up to **1/4 of the board width** in depth, class B up to 1/3, class C up to 1/2. The key rule from lesson 6 of week 2: **the class of the weakest criterion** – one class B check = the whole board is class B.
4. You perform an **A→B reclassification** (downgrading) when a board after drying has exceeded the threshold of its initial class. You change the label of the specific board (from SO-**A**-260420-012 to SO-**B**-260420-012), you update the kiln log (the table "class changes after the cycle"), you update the parent batch code (a batch of 12 class A boards may become a batch of 10 A boards + 2 B boards, requiring the physical separation of the stack into two smaller ones).
5. You understand the **pedagogical rule "the class is not constant in time"**: a board in the sawmill has one class at visual classification (lesson 6 of week 2), another after sorting (lesson 6 of week 3), potentially another after drying (lesson 8 of week 3), yet another after 3 years of storage (lesson 7 of week 2 – dry vs wet wood, change of ambient humidity). Updating the class is an element of the **documentary honesty** of the sawmill – the customer pays for the current state, not the historical one.

## Content

::: info
**Why reclassification after drying is not a "process defect" but a "property of the material".** On average 5–15% of boards in a class A pine batch require reclassification to B after drying (IBDiM and Consultor Drewno studies, 2020–2023). For oak the rate reaches 20–30%, for beech even 25–40% (more extreme desorption stresses in diffuse-porous wood). This does not mean the batch was sorted poorly – it means that **drying reveals hidden defects**: microcracks invisible to the eye develop under stress, boards with initially imperceptible internal stresses crack at the boundary between earlywood and latewood. A sawmill that declares "100% class A after drying" for a pine batch is either inaccurate in its inspection or cheating the customer. A professional sawmill accepts reclassification as an element of the flow and communicates it to the client before shipment.
:::

### Three cycle-closing parameters – checks before opening

Before you put a hand on the chamber handle, you check the Lignocam controller panel together with Zbigniew. The panel displays three numbers that **must agree** with the closing protocol:

**Parameter 1: temperature inside the chamber**. Reading: 23.1°C. Temperature of the kiln hall (wall thermometer): 21.5°C. Difference 1.6°C – safely below the 3°C limit. If the difference were greater than 3°C (e.g. chamber 30°C, hall 22°C), opening the door would cause **vapour condensation** on the surface of the cooler boards (dew from the airflow) – the boards would reabsorb moisture, raising the humidity of the outer layer by 2–5% MC and spoiling the cycle.

**Parameter 2: relative air humidity in the chamber**. Reading: 42%. Hall humidity (hygrometer): 55%. Difference 13 points – acceptable (chamber dried, hall normal). If the difference were the other way round (chamber 65%, hall 55%), this would mean the cycle had not been properly finished with a drying phase. The master's decision: an additional 4–6 hours of dry phase before opening.

**Parameter 3: wood moisture from the in-chamber probes**. In chamber 2, four wood probes (driven into representative boards of the batch before the cycle started – done by Zbigniew, without your involvement) show: 11.8% / 12.3% / 11.5% / 12.1%. Average 11.9%, spread 0.8% – cycle uniformity very good. Target moisture for the export pine batch: 12 ± 2%. It fits. Zbigniew says: "We are opening. Remember, the probe readings are orientational – your task is verification with a hand-held moisture meter in four positions after unloading."

Door-opening mechanics: two side latches, one central latch, a rotary handle. The door of the Nardi chamber weighs 85 kg (150 mm of polyurethane foam insulation + steel sheet), it opens with hydraulic resistance that prevents abrupt motion. Oleksii opens it slowly – inside the chamber the warm air smells of wood, the batch stack is visible.

### Moisture measurement in 4 positions A/B/C/D

The hand-held moisture meter from lesson 2 of week 2 in module 2 (resistance model Brookhuis FMC, range 6–30% MC, accuracy ±0.5% MC, 30 mm electrodes driven **perpendicular to the fibres** to a depth of 1/3 of the board's thickness). Oleksii takes the tool out of the work case, checks the calibration (inserting into a 12% MC calibration block – reading 12.0% ± 0.3%, OK), enters the chamber together with Zbigniew.

**The A/B/C/D measurement system** is the standard for batch inspection in Polish sawmills (recommended by PN-EN 13183-2 for the resistance method):

- **Position A** – the topmost board in the stack, measurement **500 mm from the end** of the board (ends dry faster through the evaporation of vapour from the end grain, so 500 mm from the end is a representative zone, not an understated one).
- **Position B** – the same board as A, but measurement at **mid-length** (for 3000 mm = the 1500 mm position; for 5000 mm = the 2500 mm position). The middle of the board dries more slowly than the ends, so the mid-measurement gives the **upper moisture range** of the batch.
- **Position C** – the board in the middle of the stack by height (for a 12-layer stack: layer 6 or 7), measurement 500 mm from the end. The middle of the stack has the weakest air circulation, so measurement C gives a **comparison between layers**.
- **Position D** – the bottommost board in the stack, measurement at mid-length. Bottommost + middle = **the hardest zone to dry**, the last to reach the target moisture.

Oleksii takes 4 measurements:
- A (board 1, 500 mm from the end): **11.5% MC**
- B (board 1, middle): **12.2% MC**
- C (board 6, 500 mm from the end): **12.0% MC**
- D (board 12, middle): **12.8% MC**

Average: 12.1% MC. A-D spread: 1.3% (max 12.8 – min 11.5). **A spread below 2% MC = a uniform cycle**. Zbigniew nods: "A good batch. I can issue the cycle uniformity certificate. Now we check the checks."

::: warning
**The trap of measuring moisture in the end zone (closer than 300 mm from the end of the board).** The end grain of wood evaporates vapour 3–5 times faster than the middle zone (the fibre lumen is anatomically open toward the end, closed across). End moisture after the cycle may be 6–8% MC, while the middle of the batch still has 14% MC. A measurement 100 mm from the end gives an understated representative value – the batch looks "ready", but inside it is not. PN-EN 13183-2 requires a measurement **minimum 300 mm from the end**, the EGIDA Foundation sawmill uses 500 mm as standard for a safety margin.
:::

### Inspection of surface checks and end checks

After the moisture measurement, Oleksii puts the moisture meter aside and returns to the **visual assessment of checks**. Here he uses the class criteria from lesson 6 of week 2 in module 2 (Jan Pielorz – visual classification of wood defects), extended with post-drying specifics.

**Surface checks** are formed when the outer layer of the board dries faster than the middle – tensile stresses in the outer layer exceed the wood's strength across the fibres, and the fibres separate. They look like **long narrow fissures 0.5–2 mm wide, 20–200 mm long**, parallel to the fibres (that is, along the board). Class criteria after drying:

- **Class A**: up to **50 mm total check length per 1 running metre of the board**. Pine SO-A 3000 mm = max 150 mm in total (these can be 3 checks of 50 mm each, or 6 of 25 mm, or a single one of 150 mm).
- **Class B**: up to **100 mm/rm** of the board (double the A threshold).
- **Class C**: without an upper limit, as long as the checks do not run all the way through (through the entire thickness of the board).

**End checks** are formed at the ends of boards, where vapour evaporates fastest – the fibres shrink lengthwise at the ends but are held back by the rest of the board, and fissures perpendicular to the end grain form, penetrating **into the depth of the board**. Class criteria:

- **Class A**: an end check of at most **1/4 of the board width** in depth. For a 150 mm wide board = max 37.5 mm deep.
- **Class B**: at most **1/3 of the width**. For 150 mm = max 50 mm deep.
- **Class C**: at most **1/2 of the width**. For 150 mm = max 75 mm deep.

Oleksii walks up to each of the 12 boards of batch SO-A-260420-012 in turn, with a pencil and an inspection sheet. The sheet has 12 rows (one board = one row), with columns: **board no.**, **surface checks** (total length per rm), **end checks** (depth as a fraction of the width), **class after drying**, **comment**.

### Case of batch SO-A-260420-012 – reclassification of 2 boards

Oleksii assesses boards 1–10 as compliant with class A (surface checks totalling 20–45 mm/rm, end checks 1/5 to 1/4 of the width – within class A). But **boards 11 and 12** (the last two, bottommost in the stack, that is, the hardest positions for drying) look different:

**Board 11**: end check on one end, depth **50 mm** at a width of 150 mm = **1/3 of the width**. Measurement with a ruler, not by eye – precision ±1 mm. The other end of the board is without checks. Surface checks totalling 60 mm/rm (within the class B limit, the B threshold is 100 mm/rm). **Class = B** (the weakest criterion decides, that is, the end check of 1/3 of the width).

**Board 12**: end checks on both ends, depth 45 mm (one side) and 55 mm (the other side). 55 mm at a width of 150 mm = **1/2.7 of the width, that is slightly above 1/3**, classified as C (the C threshold is 1/2 of the width, so 1/2.7 is within C, not exceeding it). Surface checks 70 mm/rm (class B). **The weakest criterion: end check of 1/2.7 of the width = class C.** Hmm. Oleksii pauses – an A→C reclassification is a two-step jump, a rare one. He checks twice with a ruler: 55 mm. Zbigniew comes over, measures himself: 54 mm. Decision: **class B** (rounded down, 54 mm is at the 1/3 = 50 mm boundary, slightly above – in the case of ambiguity the EGIDA sawmill rule: "a conservative decision, classify higher"). **Class = B**.

**Result of the SO-A-260420-012 batch inspection**:
- 10 boards → class A after drying (in line with the initial class)
- 2 boards (no. 11 and 12) → class B (A→B reclassification)
- Class retention rate: 10/12 = 83%
- Reclassification rate: 2/12 = 17%

The 17% rate for class A pine is at the upper edge of the industry norm (5–15%), but it does not signal an error – it is the edge of variability. Zbigniew notes in his register: "Batch SO-A-260420-012, reclassification 17%, within the norm. No further analyses needed."

::: example
**Why boards 11 and 12 degraded and 1–10 did not.** Position in the stack: boards 11 and 12 were the bottommost – the zone of the **weakest air circulation** in the chamber, the **last zone to dry**. During the last 24 hours of the cycle, when boards 1–10 already had 12% moisture, boards 11–12 still had 15–17%. At that time the temperature was already the full 55°C – the final evaporation of vapour from the lower boards caused greater desorption stresses, checks on existing microfissures (which Oleksii did not see before drying, because they were too fine). Pedagogical conclusion: **position in the stack correlates with the risk of reclassification**. Next time you load the chamber, remember that the bottommost boards have a 2x higher risk of a class drop – it is worth placing wood already of a lower B class there, not sparing A for "unpredictable loss".
:::

### Updating documentation – label, register, batch code

After classification, Oleksii and Zbigniew return to the kiln office (10 m² next to the hall, a desk, a shelf with folders, a computer with the batch database). Oleksii fills in three documents:

**Document 1: a new label for boards 11 and 12**. The old label "SO-A-260420-012" is peeled off boards 11 and 12 (not from the remaining 10 – they keep class A). On the new label: "**SO-B-260420-012-r**" (the letter "r" at the end stands for **reclassification**, the code retrospectively ties the board to the parent batch for audit purposes, at the same time signalling to the customer that the class is post-cycle, not original).

**Document 2: an entry in the kiln log** – a physical diary (A4 format, ruled lines, entries in pencil or pen) with a fixed column structure: date of unloading, batch code, species, thickness, number of boards, average moisture, moisture spread, reclassifications, master's signature, loader's signature. Oleksii's entry:

```
02.05.2026 | SO-A-260420-012 | pine | 50 mm | 12 boards | 12.1% MC | spread 1.3% |
reclassification: 2 boards A→B (no. 11, 12, end checks 1/3 width) |
Z.Majchrzak | O.Bohdaniuk
```

**Document 3: updating the batch database on the computer**. Zbigniew sits down at the computer, opens the TartakERP application (industry software from the Polish company Abacus, used in 60% of sawmills in PL). He searches for batch "SO-A-260420-012". Edits the record: **number of class A boards**: 12 → 10. Adds a new record **SO-B-260420-012-r**: number of class B boards 2, reclassification date 02.05.2026, justification "end checks formed during the drying cycle". Saves. The database will automatically transfer 2 boards from the class A invoice to the class B invoice, and the price difference (A 950 zł/m³ vs B 750 zł/m³ × 0.225 m³ for the two boards = 45 zł of difference) will be invoiced to the client conservatively.

The customer receiving the batch sees: "10 boards class A + 2 boards class B" instead of "12 boards class A" – the total price is 45 zł lower, but **trust in the sawmill is higher**. The alternative (hiding the reclassification, shipping all 12 as A) = the client discovers it after opening the pack, calls with a complaint, the sawmill loses 150–500 zł (return transport cost + administrative cost) and a few percent of reputation.

## Key terms

- **Reclassification** (PL: reklasyfikacja; EN: reclassification / downgrading; ES: reclasificación / degradación; UK: перекласифікація) – a formal change of the quality class of a board after the drying process, based on an assessment of checks and dimensions; documented on the batch label, in the kiln log and in the ERP system; typically A→B, more rarely A→C.
- **Surface checks** (PL: pęknięcia powierzchniowe; EN: surface checks; ES: fendas superficiales; UK: поверхневі тріщини) – narrow fissures 0.5–2 mm wide along the board's fibres formed by too-rapid evaporation of vapour from the outer layer; class criterion: A up to 50 mm/rm, B up to 100 mm/rm.
- **End checks** (PL: pęknięcia czołowe; EN: end checks; ES: fendas en los testeros; UK: торцеві тріщини) – fissures penetrating into the depth of the board from the ends, formed by evaporation of vapour from the end grain along the fibres; class criterion: A up to 1/4 of the width, B up to 1/3, C up to 1/2.
- **Four-position A/B/C/D pattern** (PL: system 4 pozycji A/B/C/D; EN: four-position A/B/C/D pattern; ES: patrón de cuatro posiciones A/B/C/D; UK: система 4 позицій A/B/C/D) – the standard for measuring the moisture of a batch with a resistance moisture meter in 4 representative places in the stack (top/middle/bottom × end/middle of the board); a spread below 2% MC means a uniform cycle.
- **Moisture spread** (PL: rozrzut wilgotności; EN: moisture spread; ES: dispersión de humedad; UK: розкид вологості) – the difference between the maximum and the minimum moisture reading in the batch; acceptable threshold 2% MC, a larger spread signals non-uniformity of the cycle.
- **Reclassification suffix "-r"** (PL: kod reklasyfikacji „-r"; EN: reclassification suffix „-r"; ES: sufijo de reclasificación „-r"; UK: суфікс перекласифікації „-r") – a single-letter addition to the batch code signalling that the class is post-cycle, not original (e.g. SO-B-260420-012-r); facilitates retrospective audit and communication with the customer.
- **Documentary honesty** (PL: uczciwość dokumentacyjna; EN: documentary honesty; ES: honestidad documental; UK: документальна чесність) – a sawmill principle requiring the disclosure of every change of class, moisture or state of the material on the label and the invoice; the alternative (hiding) leads to complaints and loss of customer trust.

## Trainer notes

**Lesson time 120 minutes**. Breakdown: 15 min introduction + the Week 3 bracket (Oleksii from Monday to Friday – the same batch), 20 min pre-opening parameter check (3 parameters on the panel), 25 min moisture measurement in 4 positions (physical demonstration, if a moisture meter is available), 25 min inspection of checks (surface + end × 3 classes), 20 min reclassification on the case of batch SO-A-260420-012 (2 boards A→B), 10 min documentation update (label + register + ERP), 5 min Check yourself + discussion.

**The pedagogical rule "the class is not constant in time"** – this is the key moment closing week 3 and opening week 4 (documentation). A junior often thinks "if the board was class A before drying, it is class A after drying." This needs to be actively challenged. Method: show a physical pine board **before** and **after** the drying cycle, point out the microfissures that have grown into macrofissures after 10 days. If you do not have access to the kiln hall, photographs of the details of the checks (before/after) will also work.

**Week 3 integration**: at the end of the lesson, draw Oleksii's arc on the board:
- l1–l3: band saw (I start, I adjust, I cut)
- l4–l5: circular saw + edger + planer
- l6: sorting (SO-A-260420-012 is created)
- l7: kiln loading (SO-A-260420-012 into chamber 2)
- l8: unloading + reclassification (SO-A-260420-012 → 10 A + 2 B-r)

Extended Week 3 mantra: "**I start, I adjust, I cut, I sort, I load, I measure, I reclassify, I update** – but always under the supervision of a foreman or a senior." Eight activities, one batch, five days.

**Pedagogical risk – "contradiction with l6 W2"**: in lesson 6 of week 2 you taught that "class is a visual property visible before drying." Now you are saying "class changes after drying." This is not a contradiction, this is a **stage of the process**. Frame it as: "In l6 W2 you assessed the **raw** class before drying. In l8 W3 you assess the **post-cycle** class after drying. Both are true for their moment – the sawmill label contains both for the full documentary path."

**Bridge to M3 W4 (complaints)**: in module 3 Oleksii will already be an independent operator handling conversations with a customer filing a batch complaint.
