## Introduction

Yesterday you learned to look **inside** a log — you saw sapwood, heartwood, rings, and bark. Today we take a step in the other direction. We look at **the whole log** from the outside and ask a simple question: **what tree is it from?**

Because in a Polish sawmill, "**wood**" doesn't roll in. **Pine**, **spruce**, **oak**, **beech**, or **birch** rolls in. Each of these species has its colour, its weight, its smell, its price and — most important for you — **its own way of being grabbed and carried**.

No one will expect you to know everything after the first week. But after a month — yes. A helper who, after a month, still cannot tell pine from oak is a problem for the foreman. A helper who **already in the first week touches, smells, looks, and asks** will, in the third week, recognize species **from five metres away**.

In this lesson you'll meet **five species** that make up over 90% of the wood in Polish sawmills. You'll learn to tell them apart by colour, hardness, weight, and — in two cases — by smell.

## Goals

After this lesson:

- You can visually recognize **pine** (Polish: **sosna**), **spruce** (Polish: **świerk**), **oak** (Polish: **dąb**), **beech** (Polish: **buk**), and **birch** (Polish: **brzoza**).
- You know which of them are **softwood** (from coniferous trees) and which are **hardwood** (from deciduous trees).
- You understand why an oak board **weighs twice as much** as a pine board of the same size.
- You know that a helper touches and smells **in order to learn** — that's not "show", it's a **professional habit**.

## Content

### The most important divide — softwood vs hardwood

In a Polish sawmill you'll see two kinds of wood that differ fundamentally. Of the logs that come in, **two thirds come from coniferous trees** (pine, spruce, larch, fir), and about **one third from deciduous** (oak, beech, birch, alder, ash).

**Coniferous trees** have needles instead of leaves. Their wood is:

- **Lighter** — easier to carry.
- **Softer** — easier to cut and drill.
- **Resinous** — smells of resin, sticks to blades.
- **Cheaper** — most construction uses softwood.

**Deciduous trees** have leaves. Their wood is:

- **Heavier** — sometimes twice as heavy as softwood.
- **Harder** — slower to cut, dulls blades.
- **Without resin** — doesn't stick to tools.
- **More expensive** — used for furniture, floors, stairs, parquet.

::: info
In Poland **coniferous forests cover about 70% of the forest area**. That's why pine and spruce are the "daily bread" of a Polish sawmill. Hardwoods are rarer, more expensive, and often treated as a "special order". If you see a lot of oak in one place in the yard — that means the plant has a current contract for furniture or export material.
:::

### Pine — the wood you'll spend most time with

**Pine** (Polish: **sosna**) is the **most common** species in a Polish sawmill. Statistically, of every 10 boards you carry at work, **5–6 will be pine**.

How you recognize it:

- **Colour**: light yellow ("straw") sapwood, **reddish-brown** heartwood. The difference between sapwood and heartwood is **very clear** — the clearest of all Polish species.
- **Rings**: wide, easily visible. Dark "latewood" stripes stand out clearly from the lighter "earlywood".
- **Smell**: very pronounced, **resinous**. Fresh pine smells "of the forest". You'll know that smell after a single day.
- **Texture**: smooth, straight, even. It's easy to write on a pine board with a pencil.
- **Weight**: a pine board (4 m × 25 cm × 4 cm) weighs about **15–20 kg** when dry, **22–28 kg** when fresh.

**What it's used for**: construction (boards, rafters, battens), window and door joinery, pallets, packaging.

::: example
A pine log **"weeps resin"** from the moment it is felled. If you help with fresh pine, **wear thicker work gloves** — resin sticks to the skin and has to be washed off with turpentine or petrol. The foreman will show you where in the hall is the "petrol corner" for washing your hands after pine work.
:::

### Spruce — the smaller brother of pine

**Spruce** (Polish: **świerk**) looks similar to pine, but has a few differences that are easy to learn:

- **Colour**: almost **uniform, pale yellow-white**. The difference between sapwood and heartwood is **almost invisible** — that's the main difference from pine.
- **Rings**: narrower, denser than pine. Spruce from the Polish Carpathians sometimes has 8–10 rings per centimetre.
- **Smell**: weaker than pine, **without obvious resin**. Spruce smells "fresh" but not "of the forest".
- **Knots**: spruce has **a great many small knots arranged in circles**. Pine has fewer, larger knots.
- **Weight**: slightly **lighter** than pine.

**What it's used for**: roof construction, timber trusses, façade boards, cladding. Spruce is also a **musical-instrument wood** — soundboards of guitars and violins are made from the best spruce.

### Oak — king of Polish hardwoods

**Oak** (Polish: **dąb**) is the most important Polish hardwood. **Twice as heavy** as pine, much more expensive, used where pine isn't enough.

- **Colour**: cream-coloured **light** sapwood, **dark-brown** heartwood, sometimes almost black in the centre of old trees. Difference like in pine — very clear.
- **Rings**: very pronounced, with characteristic **"rays"** going out from the pith (these are oak's **medullary rays** — visible only in oak and beech).
- **Smell**: light, tannic. Fresh oak smells "sour". Oak wood **stains metals black** — if you put a wet oak board on a piece of iron, after a day there will be a black stain. That's why oak is **not left** on metal supports.
- **Weight**: an oak board the same size as the pine one weighs **30–40 kg** dry, **45–55 kg** fresh. **Twice as much**.

**What it's used for**: luxury furniture, parquet, stairs, railway sleepers, casks for spirits (whisky, wine).

::: warning
**Oak and beech are heavy**. An oak board 4 m × 25 cm × 5 cm in the fresh state weighs **about 50 kg**. That is **at the limit** of the daily permitted weight for one worker (50 kg in a single lift for a man under Polish standards). With oak **you always work in a pair**. You lift alone only short pieces (up to 1 m).
:::

### Beech — hard, uniform, unpredictable

**Beech** (Polish: **buk**) is the second most important Polish hardwood after oak.

- **Colour**: uniform **pinkish-white** or pale brown. Sapwood and heartwood are **almost invisible** (like in spruce, only among hardwoods).
- **Rings**: narrow, hardly visible. Beech looks "**uniform and clean**".
- **Smell**: very weak, almost none.
- **Weight**: similarly heavy to oak (30–40 kg/m³ more than pine).
- **Special trait**: beech **warps very easily** during drying (twists, bends). Beech boards have to be stacked with **heavy weights on top**, otherwise they will deform.

**What it's used for**: furniture, chairs (the classic "wooden table made of beech"), toys, tools, stairs.

::: tip
If you see in the yard **a stack of uniformly pinkish boards with large weights placed on top** — it's almost certainly beech in the drying process. **Don't take the weights off**. The foreman knows why they're there.
:::

### Birch — the white contrast

**Birch** (Polish: **brzoza**) is the least typical of our five, but you'll meet it regularly.

- **Colour**: very light, almost **white**. Sapwood and heartwood are **invisible**.
- **Rings**: thin, faint.
- **Bark**: characteristic — **white with black stripes**. You'll recognize birch by the bark alone, before you even see the wood.
- **Smell**: fresh, weak, "forest-like".
- **Weight**: in between — between softwoods and oak/beech.

**What it's used for**: birch plywood (the best plywood in the world), toys, firewood, smoking wood (birch-smoked food).

### What this means for a helper

First — **you grab differently, you carry differently**. Pine and spruce are lighter material; you can carry two full metres of pine board on your own (if it fits the weight limit). Oak and beech **always in a pair**.

Second — **you smell differently**. A helper who worked with oak yesterday and smells pine today **knows** that today is the lighter day. That's not "superstition" — that's professional orientation in the environment.

Third — **you report differently**. If you see two logs being placed in the hall and one **"smells sour"** (oak) and the other **"smells of resin"** (pine), you know they are **two different orders**. You signal the foreman: "I have two different species on one cart — should I separate them?"

Fourth — **you clean up differently**. Oak and beech sawdust are **carcinogenic** (we said this in the first week, in the lesson about dust). If you clean up after cutting oak or beech, **a mask is mandatory**, even during cleaning. After cleaning pine sawdust — a mask is recommended but not always mandatory.

::: warning
The dust of **oak and beech** — hardwood — has been confirmed by the European Union (Directive 2017/2398) as a **carcinogenic substance** (causing cancer). The dust of **pine and spruce** isn't healthy either, but it's classified as **less hazardous**. That's why the rhythm of putting on the mask **differs** from species to species. The foreman knows this and will tell you. You just remember: **with oak and beech — mask always**.
:::

## Check yourself

1. Which of these species are **hardwoods**?
   - A) Pine and spruce.
   - B) Oak, beech, and birch.
   - C) Pine, spruce, and larch.

   → **B**

2. Why does an oak board the same size weigh more than a pine board?
   - A) Because oak is dried longer and contains more water.
   - B) Because hardwood (oak) is denser and harder than softwood (pine).
   - C) Because oak isn't treated with resin.

   → **B**

3. How will you most easily recognize a freshly felled pine?
   - A) By its uniform, almost white wood colour.
   - B) By its strong resinous smell and the clear contrast of light sapwood with reddish heartwood.
   - C) By its white bark with black stripes.

   → **B**

## Practice link

In the second week, when you walk the yard with the foreman, **he'll take you to three different stacks**. He'll say: "**This** is pine. **This** is oak. **This** is beech. **Try to remember.**" On the first day you'll mix them up. On the second day you'll get two of three right. After a week — all five of the five.

Your task: **touch**, **smell**, **lift each board**. Learn the **weight by hand** — that's the most reliable way to identify wood. Pine lifts in one hand. Oak of the same length — needs two.

After two weeks **the foreman will start sending you**: "bring me a metre of pine", "prepare two oak boards in the left hall". That means he trusts you.

## Trainer notes

**Pedagogical aim:** to build a **visual, tactile, and olfactory library** of five species. The trainee after this lesson does not have to recognize 100% perfectly — basic differentiation **softwood vs hardwood and pine vs oak** is enough. The rest will come with time and constant contact.

**120-minute rhythm:**
- 10 min — short recap of L1 (you show a cross-section disc, trainees name sapwood/heartwood/rings).
- 30 min — group reading of the lesson with a tutor.
- 35 min — **practical experiment** (if available): five short pieces of wood (30–50 cm each) — one pine, one spruce, one oak, one beech, one birch. Each participant touches, lifts, smells. **The key thing** — the differences in weight will show.
- 15 min — "Key terms" section — saying the Polish names out loud.
- 20 min — "Check yourself" + a discussion: which differences are **most important** for a helper (answer: weight and carcinogenic dust).
- 10 min — buffer, questions.

**Helpful materials:**
- **Five pieces of wood** — essential. Without them the lesson is abstract. Best fresh, from a current delivery. Can be arranged from the plant warehouse.
- Photos of the bark of the five species (from the PDF "Manual woodworking" or from the internet).
- A small kitchen scale (up to 10 kg) — to compare the weight of two identical pieces of pine and oak. This is a **strong "aha" moment** for trainees.

**Common trainee mistake:** confusing **spruce with pine** (they look similar). Hint: spruce has **lots of small knots in circles**, pine has **fewer, larger knots**.

**Trainee questions:**
- "And fir?" — Fir is similar to spruce, almost indistinguishable without experience. In Polish sawmills it is often treated as "spruce" in sorting. You say: "it exists, but we treat it together with spruce".
- "And larch?" — Larch (yes, used as a "special" softwood) has a **very red heartwood**, even more so than pine. You mention it but don't go into detail — it's rare.

**Practical recommendation:** if the plant has its own samples warehouse (some larger sawmills have a "samples salon" for clients), **ask for a tour**. Half an hour in such a place is worth a week of theory.
