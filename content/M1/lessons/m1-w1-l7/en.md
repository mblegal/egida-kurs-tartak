## Introduction

Even with the best OHS, the best PPE, and the best signage — sometimes something goes wrong. Someone gets cut, burned, faints, falls. In those moments **the first 3 minutes decide** whether the victim survives and in what condition they reach hospital.

You are not a paramedic. But as a sawmill worker **you must be able to react**. The Polish Labour Code (Art. 2091) requires every workplace to have trained first-aiders and **every worker to know the basics**. This lesson is those basics.

In this lesson you will learn the **chain of survival** (4 links), the **CHECK → CALL → HELP** pattern, the **most common sawmill injuries** (cut, amputation, burn, impact), and what you do **step by step** in the first 3 minutes. You will also learn what you **must not do** — because thoughtless help can kill.

## Objectives

After this lesson:

- You know the **4 links of the chain of survival**: early recognition → early CPR → early defibrillation → professional care.
- You know **which number to call** (112) and what information to give.
- You use the **CHECK → CALL → HELP** pattern.
- You can react to **four typical sawmill injuries**: deep cut, finger amputation, burn, impact (head/chest trauma).
- You know **what NOT to do** — do not move the victim, do not remove foreign objects from a wound, do not give anything to drink.

## Content

### The chain of survival

Emergency medicine rests on four links that must work in order:

**1. Early recognition and call for help** (0–2 minutes). Someone notices the accident and calls 112. The earlier — the higher the chance of survival.

**2. Early CPR** (2–10 minutes). If the victim is not breathing, you compress the chest. Every minute without CPR = 10% lower survival chance. After 10 minutes without CPR the brain is permanently damaged.

**3. Early defibrillation** (5–15 minutes). Larger facilities and public places have an **AED** (automated external defibrillator). It is a device marked with a yellow sign of a heart and a lightning bolt. You do not have to be a doctor — the device tells you what to do.

**4. Professional medical care** (10–30 minutes). Paramedics, ambulance, hospital. What happens before they arrive often decides the victim's life and ability to recover.

::: info
In a sawmill the helper **is not a rescuer**. But is the **first link of the chain**. You **recognize** the accident, you **call for help**, you **do what you can** until the rescuers arrive. Your role ends when professional help takes over. Until then — you are the only person who can do anything.
:::

### The CHECK → CALL → HELP pattern

This is the universal sequence you use in every accident:

**CHECK — whether the place is safe for you.**
- Is the machine stopped? (Press STOP if not.)
- Is there no current, no fire, no falling objects?
- **Do not enter a zone that will kill you together with the victim.** The worst thing you can do for the injured person is to become a second victim — then no one is left to call for help.

**CALL — dial 112.**
- Give: **where** (plant address, hall, workstation), **what happened** (one phrase: "hand caught by a saw", "fall from a stack", "burn"), **how many people**, **what state** (conscious/unconscious, breathing/not breathing, bleeding/not bleeding).
- **Do not hang up first.** The 112 dispatcher will guide you through first aid over the phone — that is their job and specialty.

**HELP — within what you know.**
- If the victim is not breathing → CPR (30 chest compressions + 2 breaths, repeated; if you do not know breaths — compressions only).
- If there is heavy bleeding → pressure with a clean cloth pad on the wound.
- If conscious → leave in a comfortable position, talk, keep them awake until the ambulance arrives.
- If you do not know what to do → the dispatcher will tell you over the phone.

### The number 112 — what it is

**112** is the **European emergency number**. It works across the whole EU, from any phone (mobile or landline), even without a SIM card, even with no coverage from your own operator (the phone picks any available).

The 112 dispatcher speaks Polish but **always has access to an interpreter** (Ukrainian, Spanish, Russian, English, others). If you do not speak Polish — say in English "**I need help**" or in your language state the place and what happened. The dispatcher switches to an interpreter.

::: example
An example conversation (dispatcher = D, you = Y):

D: "Emergency centre, go ahead."
Y: "Egida sawmill, 12 Leśna Street, Warsaw. Accident at a band saw. Worker cut off his fingers. Bleeding a lot. Conscious."
D: "Understood. How many fingers?"
Y: "Three. Left hand."
D: "Ambulance is coming. What about breathing?"
Y: "Breathing, talking to me."
D: "Good. Take a clean cloth, press firmly on the wound, do not let go. Sit with him, keep him awake. Collect the fingers, put them in a plastic bag, the bag into cold water. Stay on the line."

**This conversation lasts 30 seconds.** The ambulance already knows where to go. You know what to do. The dispatcher does not hang up until the ambulance arrives.
:::

### Four typical sawmill injuries — first aid

**1. Deep cut (hand saw, knife, chip, splinter).**
- Pressure with a clean cloth pad (bandage, shirt, towel) **firmly**, for 5–10 minutes.
- Do not lift the pad "to check if it stopped". Release pressure → bleeding resumes.
- Raise the injured limb **above the heart** (arm up, leg on a support).
- Do not remove foreign objects from the wound (a piece of wood, metal). Build the pad around it.

**2. Amputation of a finger / part of a hand.**
- **On the wound**: firm pressure and a pad. Do not tie a tourniquet unless the bleeding cannot be stopped by pressure (typically with a whole-hand amputation). A tourniquet incorrectly applied → necrosis.
- **The severed part**: collect it (do not wash, do not clean), place in a clean plastic bag, put the bag in another bag with **cold water and ice** (not ice directly on the finger — frostbite). Label the bag: time, victim's name.
- Microsurgeons can re-attach a finger **within 6 hours** of amputation — hence the importance of a fast ambulance.

**3. Burn (fire, hot metal, steam, hot oil).**
- **Cool with cool (not ice-cold) water** for **15–20 minutes** — the most important first-aid step for a burn.
- **Do not peel off** clothing stuck to the burn.
- **Do not smear** butter, flour, toothpaste, oil, "home remedies" — that is a mistake that makes treatment harder.
- After cooling, cover with a clean dressing (not cotton wool — it will stick).
- For burns of the face, airways, groin, or hands — always 112.

**4. Impact (head, chest, kickback trauma).**
- **Do not move the injured person** until you know there is no spine injury. Moving may break the spinal cord — lifelong paralysis.
- Exception: if the victim is in a danger zone (under a running machine, in fire, under a falling stack) — then you must move, but **by the ankles, dragging along the body axis**, keeping the head in line with the spine.
- Talk to the victim — if conscious, keep them responding. Losing consciousness → 112.
- If vomiting — turn to the side (**recovery position**) so they do not choke.

### What NOT to do

This is a short but very important list:

::: warning
- **Do NOT move** the injured person unless the zone will kill them (fire, falling stack, machine).
- **Do NOT remove** foreign objects from a wound (wood, metal, nail). Build the pad around them, do not touch.
- **Do NOT give anything to drink or eat.** Possible surgery — a full stomach = risk of aspiration under anaesthesia.
- **Do NOT apply a tourniquet** "just in case". It compresses vessels and after 2 hours causes necrosis. Only for uncontrolled bleeding that cannot be stopped by pressure.
- **Do NOT leave** the victim alone. Even "waiting for the ambulance" — someone must stay. The victim may lose consciousness at any moment.
- **Do NOT remove** the helmet from a victim with a suspected head injury. Removing the helmet can cause additional spine injury.
:::

### Your first-aid kit — what is inside

Every workstation in a sawmill has a **first-aid kit**, marked with a white cross on a green background. Standard contents (Polish ordinance of MPiPS, 2016):

- Individual dressings (3 sizes).
- Elastic bandages (3 sizes).
- Adhesive plaster (rolls).
- Single-use latex gloves (protect you from the victim's blood).
- CPR breathing mask.
- Thermal blanket (silver-gold, against hypothermia).
- Scissors for cutting clothes.
- Eye-rinse water.

The kit **contains no medicines** (no pain pills, no antibiotics, etc.). First aid does not include giving medicines — that is the doctor's role.

::: tip
In the first week, find the kit in your zone and **open it** — see what is inside. Do not search for a bandage for the first time "during the accident". The kit must be ready to use.
:::

## Check yourself

1. A colleague has lost a finger to a saw, heavy bleeding, conscious. First thing:
   - A) I look for ice to cool the finger.
   - B) I CHECK whether the machine is stopped (I press STOP), I CALL 112, I HELP — pressure pad on the wound.
   - C) I grab the severed part and run to the car.

   → **B**

2. Hand burn from hot oil. What do you do before the ambulance arrives?
   - A) I smear butter on it, as grandma did.
   - B) I cool it with cool (not ice-cold) water for 15–20 minutes, then cover with a clean dressing.
   - C) I peel the clothing off the burn.

   → **B**

3. A colleague has fallen off a stack, lies motionless, breathing, not responding to voice. He is next to a saw station, but the saw is stopped. What do you do?
   - A) I move him to the foreman's office.
   - B) I CHECK (place is safe — saw is stopped), I CALL 112, I HELP — I do not move him, keep the head in line with the spine, wait for the ambulance. If he vomits — recovery position.
   - C) I put cold water on his forehead and wait for him to wake up.

   → **B**

## Link to practice

In the first week the foreman **will show you the first-aid kit** in your zone — you will open it together, see where everything is. He will also show you the **emergency numbers board** (usually at the entrance to the floor): 112, plant address, shift manager's name, internal numbers.

Within the first month at the sawmill you **must complete first-aid training** (usually 4 hours, with an instructor). If you have not had it yet — report to the foreman.

## Trainer notes

**Pedagogical goal:** the learner **reacts instead of panicking**. In an accident the most common mistake is freezing — a person stands and does not know what to do. The lesson teaches **the first three things** (CHECK → CALL → HELP) to get moving. The dispatcher at 112 will say the rest.

**120-minute rhythm:** 10 min recap (l6: where you are allowed — today: what if something happens anyway), 15 min chain of survival and the helper's role in it, 20 min the CHECK → CALL → HELP pattern + calling 112 (phone simulation — the learner tells the dispatcher what happened), 30 min **practical exercises on 4 injuries** (on a dummy or learners): pressure dressing, packing a severed part, burn cooling, recovery position, 15 min what NOT to do (list), 15 min first-aid kit — open, inspect, name, 10 min terms and self-check, 5 min for questions (usually many).

**IMPORTANT — 112 phone simulation:** the learner holds a switched-off phone, another learner plays the dispatcher. Give 2 scenarios: (1) finger amputation of a helper at a band saw, (2) burn at a drying kiln. The learner speaks on the phone — **where, what, how many people, state**. The trainer rates conciseness and accuracy. After each simulation — discuss.

**Special note — CPR:** This lesson is **NOT CPR training**. CPR requires proper training with a dummy and an instructor. Here you only mention that it exists, when to do it (no breathing), and that the dispatcher will guide you over the phone. Full CPR training is a mandatory 4-hour course in the first month of employment.

**Common mistake:** learners want to "do something" active (ointment, food, strong cleaning of the wound) — because "doing something" gives a sense of control. Explain: **the most important "doing" is calling for help and not leaving the victim alone**. The rest — calm and basic actions.
