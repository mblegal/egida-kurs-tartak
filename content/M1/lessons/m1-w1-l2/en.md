## Introduction

Yesterday you learned **why** a sawmill is different from other workplaces. Today we move to specifics. The first major hazard you will meet on the sawmill floor is **mechanical hazards** — hazards from moving machine parts and from heavy material in motion.

You will not operate these machines. As a helper you pass material, clean, carry. But the machines work a metre from you. That is why you must **see the hazards before they find you**.

In this lesson you will learn the **three types of mechanical hazards** (entanglement, impact, crushing), what the **working zone** of a machine is, the difference between **fixed** and **movable guards**, where to find the **emergency STOP button**, and how to behave in the three phases of machine operation: before, during, and after stopping.

## Objectives

After this lesson:

- You recognize three types of mechanical hazards: **entanglement**, **impact** (including kickback), **crushing**.
- You know what the **working zone** of a machine is and why a helper never enters it.
- You tell apart a **fixed guard** from a **movable guard** and know what to do when one is missing.
- You know the location of the **emergency STOP button** on every machine and when to use it.

## Content

### What are mechanical hazards?

A **mechanical hazard** is the risk of injury from a machine part or from the material the machine processes. Motion, force, and blade — these three together produce the most serious injuries: amputations, fractures, crushing. Mechanical hazards are visible — unlike dust or noise. But **seeing does not mean safe**. You have to know where to look.

### The three types

**1. Entanglement.** A moving machine part (roller, drive belt, rotating blade) grabs clothing, fingers, hair. Once it grabs, **it does not let go**. The body follows the clothing, the finger follows the glove, the head follows the hair. This happens in a fraction of a second.

::: warning
Do not wear loose clothing, a watch, rings, bracelets, or jewellery when you step onto the production floor. Long hair — under a cap or a hair net. **Do NOT wear gloves near rotating machines** (drill, saw, lathe). A glove caught by a blade will pull your hand into the machine faster than you can react.
:::

**2. Impact (including kickback).** Material thrown from a machine flies at 50–100 km/h. A saw cuts a board, but sometimes the board "cracks" inside, jams, and is thrown back with the same energy that was cutting it. That is **kickback**. A 30-cm piece of wood ejected from a saw hits with a force comparable to a blacksmith's hammer.

::: example
A real case from a PIP report: a helper was standing three metres behind the worker operating a circular saw. A piece of board was thrown in his direction and hit his chest. Three broken ribs and concussion. The helper was "far away", but he was standing **in the kickback line**. The kickback line is not a radius — it is a line behind the machine.
:::

**3. Crushing.** Heavy material falls, tips over, or rolls off a conveyor. A log falls from a cart. A stack of lumber tips over when someone pulls the wrong board from the bottom. A ramp drops when someone releases the lock. A body under a 300-kg log has no chance at all.

### The working zone of a machine

The **working zone** is the area around a running machine where the machine itself and its operator work. It is marked on the floor with a **yellow or red line**. The line on the floor is an order rule — not decoration.

**A helper never enters the working zone of a running machine.** Never. Not even if the operator is smiling. Not even if the operator waves a hand. If you want to come closer, stop before the line and wait until the operator **turns off the machine and gives you a sign**. Only then do you cross the line.

The difference between the **working zone** and the **danger zone** (which you learned yesterday): the danger zone is the area around the blades and cutting parts — only the machine itself works there and **even the operator does not enter**. The working zone is the larger area around the whole workstation — the operator enters it, but the helper never does.

### Guards — fixed and movable

Every sawmill machine has **guards**. These are barriers, screens, and covers that separate you from the blade.

- **Fixed guard** — bolted on, does not open. A mechanic removes it at inspection, not the operator. If you see that a fixed guard is off and the machine is running — **that is a dangerous situation**. Report to the foreman.
- **Movable guard** — a flap, cover, or screen which the operator opens to change the material. It opens only after the machine is stopped. A good movable guard has an **interlock**: when it is open, the machine will not start.

::: tip
When you see a missing or damaged guard, **you do not operate that machine and you do not come close**. You report to the foreman. The foreman decides whether the machine needs repair or whether it can still be used with a different guard. You do not decide on your own.
:::

### The emergency STOP button

Every sawmill machine has an **emergency stop button** — a big red mushroom on a yellow background. Visible from a distance. You press it **with your whole palm** (not a single finger) the moment you see:

- **Someone has fallen** into the danger zone (a colleague, an animal, a child, anyone).
- You hear a **strange sound** from the machine (rattle, grind, unusual humming).
- **Material has jammed** and nobody knows what to do next.
- Anything else that worries you.

::: info
Better to press STOP for no reason than not to press it when you must. Stopping the machine costs 3 minutes. Restarting costs 5 minutes. An amputated finger costs the rest of your life.
:::

### The three phases of machine operation — your rules

**Phase 1 — before start.** The operator checks guards, blade condition, lubrication. In this phase you **stand outside the working zone** and wait for a signal.

**Phase 2 — machine running.** Do not enter, do not touch, do not pass anything without an operator's signal. The operator's gesture "stop" = stop moving, hands down. The gesture "dawaj" (give it) = pass material **from the direction the operator shows**.

**Phase 3 — machine stopped.** **Do not touch it right away.** Blades have **inertia** — they keep spinning for dozens of seconds after the switch. Wait until the operator says "it's fine". Then you clean, pass new material, do your part.

## Check yourself

1. What does the operator's signal "dawaj" (give it) mean at a running machine?
   - A) I can enter the working zone and pass the material any way I like.
   - B) I pass material from the direction shown by the operator, without entering the working zone.
   - C) I take material from the rack myself and hand it in however is convenient.

   → **B**

2. You see that a saw has a fixed guard removed but is still running. What do you do?
   - A) I report to the foreman and I do not approach the machine.
   - B) I try to reattach the guard myself.
   - C) I carry on as usual if the operator says nothing.

   → **A**

3. When do you press the emergency STOP button?
   - A) Only when the foreman tells me to.
   - B) When I see someone has entered the danger zone, I hear a strange sound, the material has jammed, or anything worries me.
   - C) Never — only the operator presses STOP.

   → **B**

## Link to practice

In your first week on the floor, the foreman will show you the actual machines and for each one **draw the working zone in the air with his hand**. Notice that on some machines the zone is a circle with a radius of a metre, and on others it is a rectangle behind the machine (that is where the kickback flies). These are not the same areas.

The foreman will also show you **every emergency STOP button**. Memorize these locations — on every machine you pass while walking on the floor. When a colleague shouts, you have 1 second to react. You do not have time to search.

## Trainer notes

**Pedagogical goal:** from the "attitude" of lesson 1 (respect for hazards) we move to the **classification of mechanical hazards**. This is the first lesson in which the learner learns to **name hazards**. Naming builds perception — if someone does not know the word "kickback", they will not recognize the hazard in reality.

**120-minute rhythm:** 10 min revision of lesson 1 (the triad + the helper's rule), 20 min introduction of the three hazard types with demonstration (show on film or PIP photos), 30 min of reading the content, 20 min "identify the hazard" exercise (show 6–8 photos of the production floor, the learner names the hazards), 20 min on zones and the STOP button, 10 min of a multilingual terms exercise (the learner repeats the Polish term said by the trainer), 10 min of the self-check.

**"Identify the hazard" exercise:** prepare 6–8 photos (from PIP "Safe Work in a Sawmill", pp. 8–15 + your own floor photos). On each photo the learner points to at least one hazard and names it (in Polish or in their own language — we test perception, not vocabulary). Learners who have worked elsewhere often do not see kickback — that is exactly what needs training.

**Common mistake:** learners confuse "working zone" with "danger zone". Explain: danger = where the machine itself works (no people), working = where the operator stands. The helper never enters either. Use a diagram of two concentric circles: small = danger, larger = working.
