## Introduction

In l3 you saw that **at running machines you cannot hear words**, so the main language becomes gestures. But a sawmill is not only a hall full of running saws. During the day you have **several quiet windows** when communication returns to words:

- **Morning briefing** (6:45–7:00): the foreman says what you do today, who stands where, what to watch out for. Machines are not yet running.
- **5-minute safety talk** (7:00–7:05): a short reminder on one topic („today we watch for the icy log yard", „yesterday there was a jam on saw number two, let us talk about how to avoid it"). Words.
- **Coffee breaks** (9:00–9:15 and 12:00–12:30): you sit in the break room, machines are off. You talk.
- **End-of-shift report** (15:15–15:30): the foreman asks each of you in turn: „how was today?". You answer in words: „all OK" or „spilled sawdust at station five, I am reporting it".
- **Conversation with the storekeeper**: you enter the warehouse, you ask for material. No machine noise, normal speech.
- **Conversation with the foreman in the office**: when he calls you to discuss something (for example, a weekly evaluation).
- **Alarm situation**: when someone shouts „uwaga!" or „stop!", you hear it even through hearing protection, because a shout reaches 105 dB and the protection blocks only 20–30 dB.

In each of these situations you **must understand Polish words**. And in an alarm situation you **must be able to speak a few Polish words yourself**, loud and clear, without hesitation.

This lesson gives you a set: **30 Polish sawmill words** for listening comprehension (you do not need to pronounce them all perfectly), **5 alarm commands** for active speech (because these can save a life), **5 everyday courtesy expressions** (because without „dziękuję" and „przepraszam" you will not fit into a Polish crew), and a **phonetic map** of Polish sounds that are hard for foreign speakers.

This lesson will not turn you into a Pole. It will turn you into a **helper who can function in a Polish workplace from day one**, even though you are only just learning Polish.

## Objectives

After this lesson:

- You understand that a sawmill has **quiet windows** (briefing, break, end-of-shift report) where communication returns to words, and **noise windows** where only the gestures from l3 work.
- You know the **difference between passive comprehension and active production**. You know that passive comprehension is easier and that is why you start with it (30 words passively).
- You recognize by ear **30 Polish sawmill words** in 5 groups: people, places, materials, machines, commands. You can give their meaning.
- You can pronounce **5 Polish alarm commands**: UWAGA, STOP, ODSUŃ SIĘ, ZABLOKUJ, PUŚĆ, TRZYMAJ. Even if your pronunciation is imperfect, the crew will understand you.
- You can pronounce **5 everyday courtesies**: dzień dobry, do widzenia, dziękuję, proszę, przepraszam. Because without them you come across as „a stranger" in a Polish crew, even if you work well.
- You know **7 Polish sounds** that probably do not exist in your language (ą, ę, ś, ć, ń, ł, rz/ż), and you know how to approximate them.
- You have **5 strategies for learning Polish at work**: a card in your PPE pocket, a daily question to the foreman, repeating in the evening, learning with a co-helper, not being afraid of mistakes.

## Content

### Passive comprehension vs active production

Language learning has **two skills** that develop at different speeds.

**Passive comprehension (receptive)**: you hear a Polish word, you know what it means. You do not need to pronounce it. You do not need to write it. It is enough that you recognize it. Example: the foreman says „idź na plac po deskę" (go to the yard for a board). You understand that you need to go to the log yard and bring a board. You go. **You say nothing**, you just perform the task.

**Active production**: you pronounce a Polish word yourself. This is much harder, because you need to:
1. Know which word you want to say (a dictionary in your head).
2. Assemble it phonetically (Polish sounds, stress).
3. Pronounce it so another person understands.

In your first week of work, **80% of Polish communication is passive comprehension**. The foreman speaks, you react. You do not need to say much. „Tak" (yes), „nie" (no), „OK", „dziękuję" (thank you): that is enough in 9 out of 10 situations.

**Actively** you need to know only two things: (1) **courtesies** (good morning, thank you, sorry), (2) **alarm commands** (watch out, stop, step aside). Ten words in total. The rest you understand but do not produce.

After a month the ratio shifts: 60% passive, 40% active. After 3 months: 50/50. After a year you speak Polish at A2 level and help new migrants who are just starting.

::: info
**Common European Framework of Reference (CEFR)**: A0 is zero knowledge, A1 is basic communication (a few phrases), A2 is independent functioning in simple situations, B1 is fluent conversation. In this lesson we teach you **specialist A0** – sawmill vocabulary and safety commands. It is not a classical A0 (you do not learn „how are you", „where are you from"), but a **professional minimum** that lets you work from day one.
:::

### Why words, since we have gestures

From l3 you remember 5 gestures: STOP, PODAJ, CZEKAJ, ODSUŃ SIĘ, COFNIJ. They work at a running machine. But:

**Gestures need eye contact**. In the warehouse you are around a corner, the storekeeper does not see you. You speak words: „proszę dwie sosny po trzy metry" (two pine boards at three meters, please).

**Gestures do not work on the phone**. If the foreman calls you (rarely, but it happens in case of a breakdown), you speak Polish.

**Gestures are not precise**. You can gesture „pass it" but not „pass that board from the third stack on the right". That needs words.

**Gestures carry no emotion**. When the foreman wants to say „you did well, I am satisfied" or „that came out badly, be careful next time", those are words. Thumbs up is one piece of information, a conversation is another.

**Gestures do not work at the briefing**. At the briefing the foreman speaks to 8 people at once, assigns tasks, discusses hazards. In words.

That is why you need words. Not for operating the machine (a gesture is enough there), but for **functioning in the crew as a person**, not only as a pair of hands.

### 5 alarm commands (production syllabus)

Out of the 30 words, **5 commands** you must be able to say yourself, loud, without hesitation. Because these are the words that **can save a life**: yours or a colleague's.

**1. UWAGA! (u-VA-ga)**

You shout it when you see a **sudden danger** for someone who does not see it. A forklift is driving down the aisle and a colleague stands with his back to the forklift. You see it, he does not. You shout „UWAGA!". Loud, high, short. The colleague lifts his head, sees the forklift, steps aside.

Intonation: **sharp, high, short**. You do not drag „uuuu-vaaa-gaaa", you fire „u-VA-ga!".

**2. STOP! (STOP)**

You shout it when someone is **about to do something dangerous** and you want them to stop. The operator is just about to switch on the saw, but you see that the blade is mounted incorrectly (a loose screw). You shout „STOP!". The operator interrupts the motion and looks at you.

Intonation: **short, hard**. One word, one syllable, one burst.

**3. ODSUŃ SIĘ! (ot-SOON she)**

You shout when someone **stands in a hazard zone** and you see that something is about to happen. The timber stack is tipping, a colleague stands next to it. You shout „ODSUŃ SIĘ!". The colleague knows (or learns on the job) that it means „get out of there immediately".

Intonation: **loud, stress on „ot-SOON"**. You can add the l3 gesture (hand pointing to the side) if the colleague can see you.

**4. ZABLOKUJ! (za-blo-KOOY)**

You shout (or say firmly, because this is not always an alarm) when **something starts to move uncontrolled** and it needs to be stopped physically. A manual cart with timber starts to roll by itself (sloping floor, handbrake not set). You shout to a colleague: „ZABLOKUJ!". The colleague runs up and puts a wedge under the wheel.

Intonation: **firm**. You do not have to shout as with UWAGA, but you must be audible.

**5. PUŚĆ! (POOSHCH) / TRZYMAJ! (TSHY-mai)**

Two opposite commands, used when **passing or holding material**. „PUŚĆ" means release what you are holding (the operator has taken it over). „TRZYMAJ" means do not release, hold tight (something is jammed and must not be let go).

Intonation: **short, sharp**. One word, no extras.

::: warning
**You must rehearse the 5 commands out loud at home** at least 10 times each, before you walk onto the hall floor for the first time. Practice with a partner or alone in front of a mirror. The goal: in a moment of alarm the command comes out of your mouth **automatically**, without stopping to think „how do I say that?". In a moment of alarm you do not have 3 seconds to search your memory for a word. You must react in 1 second, just like you react to gestures from l3.
:::

### Why Polish sounds are hard

Polish has **7 sounds** that probably do not occur in your language (or occur differently):

**ą** – nasal „on" / „om" before a consonant, like French „on" in „bon". Example: **trąd** is pronounced „trond". **Remember**: never read ą as „a".

**ę** – nasal „en" / „em", like French „in" in „vin". Example: **pieniądze** is „pie-NON-dze". At the end of a word it is often pronounced like „e" (**się** = „she" in colloquial speech).

**ś** – soft „sh". The middle of the tongue touches the palate. Example: **odsuń się** is „ot-SOON she" (the final „się" is „she", not „seekh"). **Ź** is similar but voiced.

**ć** – soft „ch" (like English „ch" in „cheese", but softer). Example: **puść** is „POOSHCH" (last consonant soft). **Dź** is similar but voiced.

**ń** – soft „n" (like Spanish „ñ" in „niño"). Example: **dzień** is „JEN".

**ł** – like English „w" in „water". **NOT** like Polish „l". Example: **kłoda** is „KWO-da", not „KLO-da".

**rz / ż** – both pronounced like French „j" in „Jean" (or English „s" in „measure"). Example: **trzymaj** is „TSHY-mai", not „TR-zy-mai".

Sounds complicated? **Do not worry**. The foreman has heard many migrants saying „KLO-da" instead of „KWO-da" or „seekh" instead of „she". **He will understand the context**. But you have to try.

::: example
**From life**: a helper from Colombia, sawmill near Radom, third week on the job. Spanish does not have the sounds „ł", „ś", „ć" as distinct phonemes. The Colombian helper learned 30 words passively in a week (because comprehension does not require production), but active production took him a month. At first he said „KLODA" instead of „KWODA". The foreman understood. After 3 weeks he heard that „KLODA" is not „KWODA" but sounds similar enough. After 2 months the Colombian helper pronounced „KWODA" correctly. **Lesson**: imperfect pronunciation is not a problem, **not trying** is the problem. The foreman judges your effort, not your phonetics.
:::

### 5 daily courtesies

These **5 words** you must be able to say from day one:

**„dzień dobry"** (JEN DO-bry) – you say it in the morning when you arrive at work, to all colleagues and to the foreman. You say it to the storekeeper when you enter the warehouse. Do not skip it: in Polish work culture not saying „dzień dobry" is seen as disrespect.

**„do widzenia"** (do vee-DZE-nia) – you say when you leave work. Or „na razie" (na RA-zie), more informal between helpers. To the foreman you say the formal „do widzenia".

**„dziękuję"** (jen-KOO-ye) – after every help, every piece of information, every tool handover. Poles thank each other a lot and expect you to do the same. Better to say it 5 times too many than once too few.

**„proszę"** (PRO-she) – when you ask for something („proszę deskę" = a board please, „proszę pomóc" = please help). In the warehouse, in the office, with colleagues. Without „proszę" you sound like a command, even if you do not mean to.

**„przepraszam"** (pshe-PRA-sham) – when you make a mistake, when you must interrupt someone's work, when something is in the way. It is not an admission of guilt (as with English „sorry"), just normal politeness.

**These 5 words create a „human layer"** between you and the crew. Without them you are „that stranger who works here". With them you are „that colleague from Ukraine / Colombia / Venezuela / Peru who fits in well".

### 5 strategies for learning Polish at work

**Strategy 1 – a card in your PPE pocket**. Print a list of 30 words (groups 1–5) on one side, 5 alarm commands and 5 courtesies on the other. Laminate (ordinary office foil, 5 PLN for 10 sheets). Carry it in your work jacket pocket. In the break you pull it out, look, repeat. Two weeks and you will not need it anymore.

**Strategy 2 – a question of the day to the foreman**. Once a day ask about one new word. „Brygadzisto, jak to się nazywa?" (Foreman, what is this called?) and point. The foreman answers („to jest przekładka" = this is a sticker). You repeat („przekładka"). You remember. After 4 weeks you will have **20 extra words** beyond the list of 30.

**Strategy 3 – 5 minutes in the evening**. After work, before you start dinner, 5 minutes of repeating the list out loud. Even if you are tired. That is the **1 percent daily effort** that after a year gives you Polish at A2 level.

**Strategy 4 – a co-helper**. If there is a second helper in your crew (Colombian, Ukrainian, Venezuelan), learn together. One says the word, the other translates into his language. Mutual translation helps better than lonely drilling.

**Strategy 5 – do not be afraid of mistakes**. Mistakes are **normal**. Poles know a migrant speaks with mistakes for the first year. Better to say „proszę kloda" (a phonetic mistake: should be „kłoda", but understandable) than to stay silent out of shame. A **silent migrant** is received worse in a Polish crew than a **migrant who speaks with mistakes**. Speaking = trying. Trying = respect for colleagues.

::: info
**The „ask before you guess" rule**. If the foreman speaks a sentence in which you understand 7 words out of 10, but 3 are unknown, **do not guess**. Ask: „przepraszam, co znaczy X?" (sorry, what does X mean?). Better to stop the conversation for 10 seconds than to do the task wrong because you guessed. The foreman **prefers** a trainee who asks over a trainee who pretends to understand. Because the second one will make a mistake, and a mistake costs more than 10 seconds of explanation.
:::

### How a Polish helper helps a migrant

In many sawmill crews the helper is not only a migrant but also **a Pole at the start of his career** (often young, 20–25). That Polish helper can be an **invaluable resource** for you, if you play it well:

- During breaks you ask him about words you do not understand.
- When the foreman speaks fast, the Polish colleague translates for you („he says you should go to the yard").
- Sometimes the Polish helper himself becomes an interpreter: when the foreman explains something especially complicated, he asks the Polish colleague: „explain this in simpler words".

In exchange you show him **something from your side**: maybe a wood-working detail from your home country, maybe a word in your language that catches his interest. Cultural exchange builds companionship.

Today you learned that a sawmill has **quiet windows** (briefing, break, end-of-shift report, conversation with the storekeeper, conversation with the foreman), in which communication returns to words. The gestures from l3 are not enough.

**Two types of language skill**:
1. **Passive comprehension** (receptive): you hear = you understand. Easier. First month: 30 words passively.
2. **Active production**: you say it yourself. Harder. First month: 10 words actively (5 commands + 5 courtesies).

- **People**: brygadzista, operator, pomocnik, magazynier, mistrz, zmiana.

## Notatki dla trenera

**Pedagogical goal**: equip the trainee with a **minimal professional Polish syllabus** for the first week of work in a sawmill. 30 words passively + 10 words actively (5 commands + 5 courtesies). This is not a language course in the full sense, it is an **operational inventory** that lets the trainee function in the crew from Monday.

**120-minute rhythm**:

- **0–15 min**: opening. Question to the group: „when do you use words, not gestures, in a sawmill?". Collect answers. List of quiet windows (briefing, break, report). Introduce the concepts „passive comprehension" vs „active production" (with a real-life example: we all know songs in languages we do not speak, that is comprehension without production).
- **15–50 min**: **30 sawmill words**. Trainer displays the list on the board or projector. Reads each word out loud (Polish pronunciation), trainees repeat in chorus. After each group of 6 a short check: trainer says a word, trainees point at their card (or answer in their language). After 5 groups, pair rotation: one trainee says a word, the other translates. 10 minutes rotation.
- **50–80 min**: **5 alarm commands**. Trainer shows each command individually, with intonation. Trainees repeat 5 times in chorus. Then a simulation: trainer stages a scene („imagine a forklift drives toward Matthew"), trainees shout the right command (UWAGA!). Each command in 2 scenes of 30 seconds. **Goal**: the command leaves the mouth in 1 second without thought.
- **80–95 min**: **Polish phonetics**. Board with 7 hard sounds. Each sound: example word + slow pronunciation + normal pronunciation. Trainees repeat. We do not demand perfection, we demand effort. Trainer listens and corrects the worst errors (e.g. trainee says „ł" as „l" – we show lip and tongue position).
- **95–110 min**: **5 courtesies**. Role-playing: trainer as „foreman", a trainee as „helper". Scripts: (1) entering the plant in the morning (dzień dobry), (2) thanks after help (dziękuję), (3) request for a tool in the warehouse (proszę), (4) interrupting a conversation to report something (przepraszam), (5) leaving the plant (do widzenia). Each trainee goes through one script.
- **110–120 min**: 5 learning strategies. Hand out the printed list of 30 words + 5 commands + 5 courtesies (laminated, credit-card size). Discuss. Key terms. Announce l5 (documents).

**Frequent mistake (1)**: trainees are **shy about shouting commands loudly**. In an alarm situation they utter a quiet, unintelligible „uwaga" that nobody hears. **Correction**: the trainer shouts „UWAGA!" at full volume in front of the group, then each trainee individually shouts at full volume in front of the group. Repeat until every trainee shouts without hesitation. This is a **psychological barrier**, not a linguistic one: the migrant is afraid to be loud because raising the voice means aggression in his culture. In a Polish sawmill a loud „UWAGA" shout is a **normal safety procedure**, not aggression. The trainer must say this clearly.

**Frequent mistake (2)**: trainees **pretend to understand the foreman's sentences** instead of asking. The result: poorly done work, a mistake, sometimes an accident. **Correction**: introduce the **„7 out of 10" rule**. If you understand 7 out of 10 words in a sentence but 3 are unknown, you ask. Exercise: trainer says a sentence with 1–2 unknown words („idź na plac po magazyniera" = go to the yard to the storekeeper), trainee asks „co znaczy X?", trainer explains. Repeat 5 times until the „co znaczy?" question becomes automatic.

**Practical recommendation**: print for each trainee an **A6 flashcard** with 30 words + 5 commands + 5 courtesies. Double-sided print: side 1 – Polish words + phonetic pronunciation, side 2 – translation in the trainee's languages (EN, ES, UK – one line each). Laminate. Carry in the PPE pocket for 4 weeks. After a month throw it away: no longer needed.

**Link to the next lesson**: l5 „Basic Workstation Paperwork". After spoken language (l3 gestures, l4 words) we move to **written language**. The trainee will learn 4 documents that are at his workstation: job card, attendance log, minor-incident log, tool-use authorization. He will learn which he signs himself and which the foreman fills in. This closes the communication triad: non-verbal (gestures) → verbal (words) → written (documents).
