## Introduction

Second lesson of Week 3. In the first lesson you established **who you are in the sawmill** — a helper who "helps, but doesn't operate". Now **the specifics**: **what you do with your hands for most of the day**.

The answer is: **you carry**. **You carry timber.** Boards, laths, wedges, stickers, offcuts, sometimes logs (though logs less often — those go by cart). Manual handling of timber is **40–60% of your working day**. And it's the activity that **ruins your spine the fastest**.

A key statistic from the Polish **CIOP-PIB** research (Central Institute for Labour Protection): **50% of all chronic spine injuries** in manual workers come from **incorrect manual handling**. Not from falls, not from machine accidents — from **daily, repetitive, bad carrying**. And that 50% does not arrive **immediately** — it arrives **after 5–15 years** of work. But when it arrives, it **does not leave**. Disc degeneration L4-L5, herniation, osteoarthritis — for life.

This means that if **now**, in the first month of work, you learn the **correct technique** — 40 years into retirement you will **walk straight**. If you don't learn, or "give it a pass because it's faster" — in 15 years you'll be **on a disability pension** with a spine that's useless.

In this lesson you'll get **specific numbers** (how many kilos you may carry, man and woman, single lift and continuous), the **technical rule** (bent knees, straight back, load close to the body) and **five situations** in which you **always call a second person** — no discussion.

## Objectives

After this lesson:

- You know the **limits set by Polish law** for manual handling: **man 30 kg continuous / 50 kg occasional**, **woman 12 kg continuous / 20 kg occasional** (under the Minister of Labour Regulation of 14 March 2000, as amended).
- You understand that **team handling** (two people) caps at **25 kg/person** for men, **10 kg/person** for women — and that this is NOT "twice as much for two".
- You know the **lifting technique**: feet apart, squat with bent knees, load close to the torso, straight back, **legs lift — not the back**.
- You know **when you ALWAYS call a second person**: unknown weight, length > 2 m, wet wood, oak/beech hardwood, height > 4 m.
- You understand that **repetitiveness** (how many lifts per day) matters **just as much** as the weight of a single lift.

## Content

### Where the numbers come from — the Minister of Labour Regulation

Polish law precisely establishes **how much a worker may carry by hand**. Legal basis: **Minister of Labour and Social Policy Regulation of 14 March 2000 on health and safety at manual transport work** (as amended — 2017 among others). This is NOT a "recommendation". It is **law**. An employer who orders you to carry more **breaks the law** and is subject to PIP (Labour Inspectorate) penalties.

The figures (current, as of 2026):

| Type of work | Man | Woman |
|---|---|---|
| **Manual, single lift (occasional work)** | **50 kg** | **20 kg** |
| **Manual, repeated during the shift (continuous work)** | **30 kg** | **12 kg** |
| **Team (2 persons)** | **25 kg per person** | **10 kg per person** |
| **Above 4 m height** (lifting onto a shelf, stack) | 50% of the limit | 50% of the limit |
| **Above 7.5 hours per day** | 50% of the limit | 50% of the limit |

::: info
**What does "occasional" vs "continuous" mean?** Occasional — **less often than once per hour**, incidentally. E.g. you carry a heavy crate once a day from the office to the yard. Continuous — **regularly, tens of times per shift**. E.g. you feed boards to the saw for 6 hours. **In the sawmill, most of a helper's transport is CONTINUOUS** — because you repeat the same action. That means **your real limit is 30 kg** (man) / **12 kg** (woman), NOT 50/20.
:::

### How much a board really weighs

For the numbers to make sense — let's look at **actual timber**:

**Dry pine board, 25 × 150 × 3000 mm** (standard size): ca. **5 kg**. You'll manage alone, no problem.

**Same pine board, but wet (50% moisture)**: ca. **8–9 kg**. Still fine alone.

**Dry oak board, 40 × 200 × 3000 mm**: ca. **17 kg**. Still alone, but already near the continuous limit (30 kg).

**Wet oak board, 40 × 200 × 3000 mm**: ca. **28 kg**. At the edge of the continuous limit — **practically always take a second person** for repetitive work.

**Fresh pine beam, 100 × 100 × 4000 mm**: ca. **25 kg**. Still possible alone, but:
  - length 4 m = **uncomfortable weight distribution**
  - **always team-handle** (one person front, one back)

**Wet oak baulk, 100 × 200 × 5000 mm**: ca. **95 kg**. This is **clearly 2–4 people** or a cart.

**Fresh pine log, diameter 40 cm × 4 m**: ca. **480 kg**. **Always a cart or overhead crane**, never by hand. A human cannot move it.

::: warning
**The trap**: wet wood weighs **1.5–2 times more** than dry (you know this from Lesson 8, Week 2). Learners who in the dry season got used to "managing an oak board alone" — in the wet season **break their back**. **Always check — wet or dry?** You recognise wet wood by its darker colour and often cold touch. If the timber came out of the mill recently (hours, days) — it is **wet**.
:::

### Lifting technique — five steps

Correct lifting technique (from the **PIP OHS Handbook** and CIOP-PIB guidelines):

**Step 1: Preparation**. Stand **directly above** the load. Feet shoulder-width apart, one slightly forward. Look at the load — assess whether you can move it alone.

**Step 2: Squat**. **Bend your knees**, not your hips. Back **straight** (not hunched). Belly engaged (natural stabilisation of the spine). Head up.

**Step 3: Grip**. Grip with **both hands**, firmly. If the board is long — grip **closer to the centre**, not at one end. If no handhold — **anti-slip gloves**.

**Step 4: Lifting**. **Legs lift, not the back**. Straighten your knees, **keeping the load close to your torso** (as if hugging a child). Do not lean back, do not twist your torso. The back stays **straight** throughout.

**Step 5: Carrying**. Load **close to the body**. Short, steady steps. **Do not twist your torso** — if you want to turn, **turn with your feet**. Before setting down — **squat again with bent knees**, do not bend forward.

::: example
**Home test**: take a chair. Lift it "**lazily**" — bend at the waist, grip, straighten the back. Did you feel tension in **the lower back**? That is exactly what ruins the spine. Now **the same move** correctly: squat with bent knees, grip, straighten the knees. Did you feel tension in **the thighs**? Good — that's how it should be. The legs are **the strongest muscle in the body**, and that's how nature designed them for lifting loads. The back — for holding the torso. **Not for lifting.**
:::

### Five situations when you ALWAYS call a second person

There are **five situations** where **without discussion you take a second person**:

**1. You don't know the weight.** A new delivery arrives, you see a baulk, you don't know what it weighs. **Don't try alone.** First ask the operator, or lift *slightly* — if there's no resistance, you try. If you feel **heavier than you thought** — **put it down, call someone**.

**2. Length exceeds 2 metres.** A 2-metre board you can still carry alone (3 m boards too, with effort). But at **length > 2 m** weight distribution is uncomfortable — **one side drops into your hand**, the other lifts up. This is **not a matter of kilograms, but of geometry**. **Team-handle** — much better.

**3. Wet wood and oak/beech species.** Wet + dense wood = trap. A board that **seemed** okay by weight — is 1.5× heavier than you thought. **Do not risk it.** Wet oak, wet beech, wet ash — **always team-handle** if length > 1 m.

**4. Lifting over 4 m in height.** Onto the top of a stack, onto a top warehouse shelf. The limit drops by **50%** — i.e. a man may only carry 15 kg continuous, 25 kg occasional. And on top of that — lifting upward is **much harder** technically (arms weaker than legs). **Use a lift truck or build the stack in steps** — don't hoist boards above your head.

**5. You are tired or injured.** If you have **a sore back from yesterday**, a knee after the match, fresh off a cold — **today you call a second person for everything heavy**. This is not weakness. This is **injury prevention**. One bad lift by a tired worker ends in a **6-week sick leave**. The employer prefers 6 minutes with a second person over 6 weeks without you.

### Team handling — how to do it

If two of you carry a board — there are **rules**:

- **One leader.** Always one person says "**lift**", "**move**", "**stop**", "**down**". The other **does not make independent decisions**. Without a leader — movements unsynchronised and the load always **overloads the weaker person**.
- **People of similar height.** If one is 195 cm, the other 165 cm — the load slides onto the shorter one (the board hangs at an angle). Where possible, **pair people of similar height**.
- **Firm grip, gloves.** Slippery board + sweaty hand = drops halfway. **Anti-slip gloves** (EN 388 category).
- **Visual communication.** Can you see your partner? If you can't — **do not start**. 5-metre board, two people at the ends — you must see each other or have **a third coordinating person** behind.
- **Order of setting down.** Never does **one person set down first** alone. You both set down **together**, on the leader's command: "**down**".

::: tip
**Why 25 kg/person, not 50 kg/two**: because **synchronisation is not perfect**. In motion, in a turn, in a sudden load — **more than half the weight** lands on one person for a fraction of a second. That's why the law builds in a buffer: even as a pair you don't exceed a sum of 50 kg (man) instead of the theoretical 100.
:::

### Number of repetitions — the forgotten dimension

One thing **rarely talked about**, which **hurts the spine the most**: **repetitiveness**.

If during a shift you lift a board **200 times** — even at the 12 kg limit (woman) — **cumulative** weight is **2400 kg = 2.4 tonnes**. Per day. **A thousand tonnes per year.** The spine withstands a limited number of **load–unload cycles** — regardless of whether the single load was 5 kg or 15 kg.

That's why the law speaks of a **daily cumulative norm**:

- **Man**: total weight carried during a shift **should not exceed 12 000 kg** over distances up to 25 m (continuous work).
- **Woman**: **3600 kg** on the same conditions.

A well-organised helper splits the work so that **between heavy series** there is **a light stretch** (sorting, marking, sweeping). **You do not carry loads 8 hours without a break.** If the foreman doesn't organise this — **you organise it yourself**. A break for micro-transport of light items = a reset for the spine.

### OHS basics: gloves, boots, PPE

For manual handling, **mandatory**:

- **Anti-slip gloves** (EN 388, category 2 or 3) — protect against splinters and slips.
- **Steel-toe boots** (EN ISO 20345 S3) — if a board drops on your foot, the steel holds up to 200 joules.
- **Hard hat** (EN 397) — in zones with overhead cranes or when you build a high stack.
- **Long-sleeved work clothes** — splinters, abrasions.

If any of these items **you don't have** — you have the right to say "**I'm not carrying until you give me the PPE**" (Art. 210 LC from the previous lesson). This is NOT your whim. It is **the employer's duty**.

## Summary

Today you learned **how to carry timber for 40 years without ruining your spine**:

- **Legal limits** (Reg. ML 2017): man **30/50 kg** continuous/occasional, woman **12/20 kg**, team **25/10 kg per person**. Above 4 m height — **50% of the limit**.
- **Five-step technique**: feet apart → bent knees → firm grip → **legs lift, back straight** → carry close to torso without twists.
- **Five second-person situations**: unknown weight, length > 2 m, wet + dense wood, height > 4 m, own fatigue/injury.
- **Repetitiveness matters as much as weight** — split the work, mix heavy with light, micro-transport breaks.
- **PPE**: anti-slip gloves, S3 steel-toe boots, hard hat if working high, long sleeves against splinters.

You have **one spine**, for a lifetime. In the sawmill there's no "spare spine" in the warehouse. **This one counts.** Today's technique = your seventy-year-olds in retirement.

## Key terms

- 📏 **Rozporządzenie MP 2000 (with 2017 amendment)** (pl) — Labour Ministry Regulation on manual handling (en) — Reglamento del Ministerio de Trabajo sobre manipulación manual (es) — Розпорядження Мінпраці про ручні транспортні роботи (uk). Polish legal basis for lifting limits. An employer who breaks it — is subject to PIP penalties.

- ⚖️ **Praca dorywcza vs stała** (pl) — occasional vs continuous work (en) — trabajo ocasional vs continuo (es) — дорійчо (принагідно) vs постійно (uk). Occasional — less often than once per hour. Continuous — regularly, tens of times per shift. In the sawmill most transport = continuous.

- 🦵 **Ugięte kolana / squat pattern** (pl) — bent knees / squat pattern (en) — rodillas flexionadas / patrón de sentadilla (es) — зігнуті коліна (uk). Lifting technique: squat with bent knees (not hips), grip, straighten legs. **Legs lift, not the back**.

- 👥 **Transport zespołowy** (pl) — team handling (en) — transporte en equipo (es) — колективне перенесення (uk). Two people carry together. Limit is 25 kg/person (M), 10 kg/person (F) — NOT 50 kg for two. Because synchronisation is not perfect.

- 🥵 **Norma dobowa** (pl) — daily cumulative limit (en) — límite diario acumulado (es) — добова норма (uk). Total weight lifted during a shift. M: 12 000 kg, F: 3600 kg (continuous work, distance up to 25 m).

- 💧 **Drewno mokre** (pl) — wet / green wood (en) — madera húmeda / verde (es) — сира деревина (uk). 1.5–2× heavier than dry. The trap: looks the same, weighs double. Always team-handle if length > 1 m.

- 🧤 **Rękawice EN 388** (pl) — EN 388 protective gloves (en) — guantes EN 388 (es) — рукавички EN 388 (uk). Gloves protecting against slips, abrasion, splinters. Mandatory for manual handling.

- 👢 **Buty S3 (EN ISO 20345)** (pl) — S3 safety boots (en) — botas S3 (es) — взуття S3 (uk). Boots with steel toe (withstand 200 J), anti-penetration insole, anti-slip sole. Standard in the sawmill.

- 🔄 **Powtarzalność** (pl) — repetitiveness / cycle count (en) — repetitividad (es) — повторюваність (uk). Number of lifts during a shift. As important as the weight of a single lift — the spine has a limited number of cycles in a lifetime.

- 🚨 **Art. 210 KP** (pl) — Article 210 Labour Code (en) — artículo 210 Código del Trabajo (es) — стаття 210 Кодексу праці (uk). Right to refuse work in hazardous conditions — including lack of PPE for manual handling. Revision from l1.

## Trainer's notes

**Pedagogical goal**: turn **abstract limits** ("30 kg") into **concrete body feeling**. After this lesson the learner should **know with their hands, not only with their head**, that a wet oak board means "call a second person", and a dry pine one means "I can manage alone". And — **learn the bent-knee technique so deeply** that after 3 months they **squat automatically** in front of a load.

**120-minute rhythm**:
- **0–15 min**: opening with a reminder of l1 (bridge: "you know where you stand, now you'll learn what you do"). The 50%-spine-from-bad-carrying statistic. Question to the group: "Who knows somebody with a bad back?" — almost always someone does. Emphasise: **they also used to carry the way you think is allowed**.
- **15–35 min**: limit table on the board. **Draw it with the group**, don't hand over a finished one. Ask: "how much may a man carry continuously?" — collect answers, rarely does someone guess. Show the truth. Emphasise the continuous/occasional difference and that **sawmill = continuous**.
- **35–55 min**: **real timber weight** — bring a dry board and a wet board of the same length into the room, oak and pine. Let each learner lift both. **Sensory learning**. "Did you feel it?" — yes, the wet one is 2× heavier. Now analyse with the group: which board fits the 30 kg limit, which doesn't.
- **55–80 min**: **five-step technique** — demonstration. First "badly" (trainer hunched, back rounded) — ask "what did you see?". Then "well" (squatted, bent knees). Each learner **tries alone**. Correct individually: "Jan, bend your knees lower"; "Olena, back straight". Repeat 3–5 times per learner.
- **80–100 min**: **five second-person situations** — list with examples from the shop floor. Team handling — demonstration in pairs. Leader, communication, setting down together.
- **100–115 min**: **daily norm + repetitiveness**. Calculation example: "I carry a 10 kg board × 400 times per shift = 4 tonnes. Is that a lot?" (yes, 1/3 of a woman's annual continuous limit in one day). Discussion: **how to split it** in a real working day.
- **115–120 min**: key terms, **oral quiz** (check questions), announcement of l3 (log yard).

**Frequent learner mistake**: "I'm strong, I'll carry 50 kg no problem". **Trainer's response**: "Today, yes. In 10 years the spine will look different. This is not a question of **whether you hold up today**, but **how many times the spine can be loaded in a life**. Muscles regenerate quickly — **intervertebral discs do NOT regenerate at all**. Every bad lift is a step towards disc degeneration. I didn't invent this — it's physiology."

**Practical recommendation**: introduce the **"assess with your eyes before lifting" rule**. The learner stands over the load, assesses for 3 seconds: *dry/wet? long/short? alone/team?* — **and only then** approaches the lift. Those 3 seconds save spines.

**Link to the next lesson**: l3 is **unloading and storing logs on the yard**. Logs are **too heavy for hands** (480 kg for a standard 4 m pine log) — there we learn **how not to stand in the roll zone**, wedging, bearers. Manual-handling technique from l2 applies to **wedges and bearers** (only 5–15 kg each).
