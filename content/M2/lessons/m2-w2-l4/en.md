## Introduction

Thursday, day seven of week two. On Monday Maricel understood moisture, on Tuesday she measured it, on Wednesday she watched the foreman chalk cuts on a sawlog. Today a new person steps into the sorting hall: **Emeka Okonkwo, 32 years old, from the city of Enugu in south-eastern Nigeria**. Before he came to Poland ten months ago, he worked for six years as a maintenance engineer at palm-oil mills. He knew tropical timber, iroko and sapele, which were used to build the workbenches at his factory. Polish oak and beech he is only starting to learn now.

At 7:15 Emeka enters the sorting hall after the first cut. By the table stands **master Jan, fifty-five years old, thirty years at the sawmill**. Jan has laid out four short pieces of lumber, each about a metre long, freshly dropped off the bandsaw. "Come here, Emeka. Before I teach you how to sort on your own, you have to **learn to see defects before they destroy a saw or a customer**. These are the four most important defects – because of them we lost two blades and one order last week."

This lesson is about **what you see on the bark and in the cross-section before a board reaches the customer**. You will learn to recognise four defects that change everything: **spiral grain, false heartwood, butt rot, bark pocket**. These are the same defects that on Wednesday Marek was looking for when he tapped an oak sawlog with a hammer.

## Objectives

After this lesson:

1. You recognise **spiral grain**: the fibres twist in a spiral instead of running parallel to the axis of the trunk. You know that a twist greater than 5° disqualifies a board from the structural class, and a twist greater than 8° causes the board to spin on its way out of the saw.
2. You recognise **false heartwood** in beech and ash: a reddish-brown, irregular core. You know that it does not reduce strength, but it lowers the visual class by 1 or 2.
3. You recognise **butt rot** in oak and spruce: a dull sound when tapped, whitish-yellow fibres, a soft core. You know that it excludes a board from every commercial class.
4. You recognise **bark pockets**: a strip of bark trapped inside the wood after a healed wound. You know that a pocket wider than 5 mm excludes a board from class A.
5. You understand **why the operator must report a defect before further processing**: an unreported defect reaches the customer, and a complaint costs the sawmill 3,000–5,000 zł plus the loss of the customer.
6. You know the **recognition tools**: the eye (spiral grain, pockets), the hand (softness of the core), a rubber mallet (tapping reveals rot), a spike (probing), a moisture meter (excess moisture in the core).
7. You know that **defects divide into mechanical ones** (spiral grain, pockets, cracks – strength) **and biological ones** (rot, false heartwood, blue stain – durability and visual quality).

## Content

### Four defects a junior operator must know

The Polish standard PN-EN 1611-1 distinguishes more than a dozen lumber defects. For an operator with six months of experience, four of them matter: they occur often in Polish raw material, they require a decision before cutting, and you can recognise them without laboratory instruments. Knots, cracks and blue stain are easier; they will return in lesson six. Today, the defects that require a trained eye.

### Defect one: spiral grain

The first piece on Jan's table: a debarked pine, 1 m long, 25 cm in diameter. The master runs a finger along the fibres: "Look at how they go." The fibres do not run straight from top to bottom. They twist to the left, as if someone wrung the trunk out like a wet dishcloth.

::: info
**Spiral grain** (*spiral grain*) is a permanent arrangement of fibres running spirally around the axis of the trunk. A genetic trait of the species plus a reaction to wind:
- **Pine**: 40–60% of trees have a twist greater than 3°, on the coast as much as 80%.
- **Spruce**: 30–50%, higher in older trees.
- **Oak**: 20–30%, with a smaller angle (1–3°).
- **Birch**: 50–70%, unfavourably for lumber.

**Measuring the angle**: draw a vertical chalk line on the trunk 1 m long. The fibres deviate from it by X centimetres. **1 cm over 1 m = 0.57°**. 5 cm = 2.86°, 10 cm = 5.71°, 15 cm = 8.53°.
:::

**Admissibility limits**:
- **Below 3°**: class A without restrictions.
- **3–5°**: class B (floors, non-load-bearing furniture).
- **5–8°**: class C (pallets, packaging).
- **Above 8°**: **rejected from structural use** (PN-EN 1912). During cutting, a board with such a twist spins on its way out of the saw (residual stress moment), endangering the operator and destroying the guide rollers.

::: warning
**Why spiral grain is dangerous**: a freshly cut board with a twist greater than 8°, after coming off the outfeed rollers, can rotate by 20–30° around its axis within a second. If the operator is standing on the saw's outfeed side, he takes the rotating edge in the hand or in the knee. **Every bandsaw operator has the duty to mark with chalk a board with a visually visible twist** (greater than 5°), so that the helper knows he must hold it with both hands.
:::

### Defect two: false heartwood

The second piece: a freshly cut beech, 80 cm long. In the cross-section Emeka sees **an irregular, reddish-brown stain in the centre, with ragged edges, as if someone had spilled tea on a white tablecloth**. The stain takes up about 30% of the cross-section.

**False heartwood** (*false heartwood*, German *Rotkern* – "red core") occurs mainly in beech, ash and alder. It is **the reaction of the living tree to mechanical damage or old age**: sapwood transforms into dead tissue of altered composition, darker, without a geometric boundary.

::: tip
**Why "false"?** In oak, pine and larch there exists **true heartwood** – the darker central part of the trunk with a regular, circular shape (dark brown in oak, slightly pink in pine). In beech and ash the heartwood normally **should not be visible**. If you see a red stain in beech, that is not oak heartwood: it is beech false heartwood.
:::

**Effect on class**:
- **Strength**: **does not reduce strength** (ITD Warsaw 2018 studies: difference below 5%). Beech with false heartwood still meets PN-EN 14081 for class C24.
- **Visual quality**: lowers the class by 1, sometimes 2. Furniture-grade beech (A) with false heartwood taking up more than 10% of the cross-section goes to class B (floors, stairs).
- **Price**: a drop of 20–40%. Beech A at 1400 zł/m³, beech B with false heartwood at 800–1000 zł/m³.

**Where it comes from**: 30–40% of beeches over 100 years old have false heartwood. Poland has many old beech stands (Bieszczady, Puszcza Bukowa), so you encounter the problem regularly.

**Recognition**: colour ranging from reddish-brown to dark brown; irregular shape with a blurred edge (true oak heartwood has a round edge); location from the butt up to the middle of the trunk; a slightly sour smell (tannic acids).

### Defect three: butt rot

The third piece: pedunculate oak, 45 cm in diameter. Jan takes a carpenter's hammer with a rubber head and taps it in three places. Near the bark: a ringing sound. Closer to the core: **a dull, muffled sound, as if striking a wet sponge**. Jan puts down the hammer, takes out a spike and drives it into the core: **it goes in 2 cm without resistance**.

**Butt rot** (*butt rot*) is the decomposition of wood by fungi (*Heterobasidion annosum*, *Armillaria*, *Fomes fomentarius*). It attacks through the roots and advances up the trunk by 5–15 cm per year. In an old oak (over 120 years), the rot can reach 2–3 m from the ground.

::: warning
**Rot excludes a board from every commercial class.** A board with rot goes for fuel or pulp. **Never for structure, never for flooring, never for furniture.** The fungi keep working in dried wood if the moisture content rises above 18%. A board built into a structure falls apart after two years.
:::

**Five recognition signals**:
1. **Tapping**: rubber mallet into the core. Ringing – healthy. Dull – suspicion. Master Jan does this ritually on every sawlog of 40 cm or more.
2. **Probing**: a spike into the suspect spot. Healthy wood admits it by 2–4 mm, rot by centimetres.
3. **Colour**: whitish-yellow fibres ("white rot"), brown patches ("brown"), sometimes greenish.
4. **Texture**: the wood falls apart into fibres under your fingers, it does not break into splinters.
5. **Resistance moisture**: the core of a sawlog with MC greater than 35% next to dry bark = an alarm signal.

**The foreman's decision**: in a sawlog with rot detected at the butt end, the foreman **shortens the butt by 50 cm past the rot boundary** (a safety margin) and treats that part as fuel. The rest of the sawlog is cut normally, because rot usually does not reach higher than 2–3 m. **The helper's role**: when you see the master tapping with the hammer, you know he is checking for rot. When the sound is dull, never start unloading that sawlog onto the saw – wait for the decision to shorten.

### Defect four: bark pocket

The fourth piece: spruce, 30 cm long. Jan turns it sideways. Emeka sees **a vertical strip of dark, dried material deep inside the wood, 8 mm wide, 20 cm long, as if someone had sealed a piece of old bark inside**.

**Bark pocket** (*bark pocket*) is a piece of bark left inside the trunk after a healed wound. Most common cause: **a broken branch**, which the tree has overgrown with layers of new growth. Less often: a tractor impact in the forest, rubbing against another tree in a storm, a cut from a tool during thinning.

::: example
**Class limits**:
- **Width**: up to 3 mm → A (barely visible), 3–5 mm → B, 5–15 mm → C, above 15 mm → D (pallets).
- **Length**: up to 1/3 of the board length → no additional restriction; 1/3–1/2 → lowers by 1 class; above 1/2 → lowers by 2 classes or excludes.
- **Frequency**: one spruce in three has a pocket at the butt. In pine less often (1 in 5). In oak least often (1 in 10 – the tree "seals" the wound with resin).
:::

**Why a pocket is a problem**:
- **For the customer**: after drying and sanding the pocket falls out, leaving a hole. In visible furniture it disqualifies the piece.
- **For the machine**: a pocket contains dust, sand, hard mineral contaminants (calcium, silicon from resin). **A saw blade that hits a pocket loses its edge** – blade life drops by 30–50%.
- **For impregnation**: a pocket does not absorb preservatives. A stained board shows a dark stripe, and the customer demands a correction.

**Recognition**: on a fresh cross-section – **a black or dark-brown vertical stripe** surrounded by light wood. Touched with a knife it cracks and crumbles (dry bark, not wood). On the outer bark of the sawlog a pocket is **a depression with a vertical line of darker bark**. During inspection master Jan runs his finger over the bark and feels depressions 2–5 mm deep.

### Why the operator reports defects before shipment

The Regulation of the Minister of Economy of 14 April 2000 (§ 3) imposes on the operator the duty to work in accordance with the instructions of the person in charge. In practice: **the duty to stop the process and report a defect that exceeds a class threshold**. If from a log ordered for veneer (class A) a board comes out with an 8 mm pocket, the operator **does not sort it into the veneer stack** – he sets it aside on a separate "awaiting foreman's decision" stack. The foreman will decide: the board goes to B (change of order), or returns to cutting into smaller pieces (cutting out the pocket, the rest as A in a shorter size).

**What happens if the operator sent a board through "because he did not notice"**: it reaches the joinery, the carpenter spots the pocket during sanding, phones in a complaint. The sawmill loses 3,000–5,000 zł on a single complaint (board replacement, transport, time). **On the third complaint of the year the customer changes supplier.** Reporting defects before shipment is economically critical: it is not paperwork, it is the sawmill's budget.

## Trainer notes

**Leading analogy**: wood is like the human body. True heartwood is the adult bone (core), sapwood is the younger layers (muscles). Rot is an infection (fungus attacks like bacteria). False heartwood is a bruise or a scar (reaction, not disease). A pocket is a cyst after a healed wound. Spiral grain is a curved spine: it does not hurt, but it affects function. The analogy works across cultures.

**Why exactly these four defects**: pragmatic selection. PN-EN 1611-1 lists fifteen defects. A junior operator must be able to recognise those that (1) radically affect value, (2) threaten the machine (a pocket dulls the blade, spiral grain destroys the rollers), (3) threaten the operator (spiral grain – the board spins on the outfeed), (4) are hard to spot without training (false heartwood in fresh beech looks like spilled coffee, a trainee sorts it into class A). Knots, cracks and blue stain are easier; they will return in lesson six.

**Where the lesson returns**: m2-w2-l5 assortments (a defect affects the assortment – a sawlog with rot does not go onto a veneer cant), m2-w2-l6 classes A/B/C/D (formalisation of thresholds), m2-w2-l7 saws (a pocket = shortened blade life), m2-w3-l4 cutting programme (a defect forces a different programme), m3-w1-l6 customer complaints.

**Pitfalls for migrant trainees**:
- "Heartwood = centre of the trunk" – **not always**. In oak and pine, yes; in beech and ash the heartwood should not be visible. For trainees from Ukraine (where beech dominates) this pitfall is more visible than for trainees from Poland (where oak dominates).
- Rot "because it is a bit soft" – **the boundary**: spike enters 2 cm without resistance = rot. A fingernail leaves a mark = only fresh, damp wood.
- Spiral grain "because the trunk twists on the bark" – **do not confuse**: spiral grain (a feature of the wood) versus crookedness of the trunk (a feature of the shape). A crooked trunk can have straight fibres, a straight trunk can have spiral ones.
- Pocket "because the bark stayed on the trunk" – **no**, a pocket is bark **inside** the wood, not outside.

**Case Emeka – African perspective**: Emeka came from a context of tropical timber (iroko, sapele, African mahogany). Those species have different dominant defects: "gum vein" (a resin strip), "brittle heart" (a brittle core after growth stress), "sapstreak" (bacterial discolouration). The defects of Polish species are new to Emeka. Show him photographs of defects from his native context, then overlay the Polish analogy. Beech false heartwood has a similar mechanism to "brittle heart" in iroko: both are reactions of the tree to stress.

**Why hammer tapping is a ritual, not a gimmick**: since the nineteenth century European classifiers have been tapping wood. The technique is simple, cheap, effective against 80% of rot cases. In the age of ultrasound and computed tomography of wood, tapping is still the first line of diagnosis on the log yard, because the master checks a sawlog in 30 seconds. Respect this ritual: you will not replace it with a phone app.

**Integration with T1 M2**: mechanical defects (spiral grain, pocket) can cause failures during cutting: blade jamming, a change of resistance, kickback. The 5-step protocol from m2-w1-l7 (STOP → WAIT → ANCHOR → REPORT → INVESTIGATE) applies when the operator senses such behaviour from the board. Defect recognition **ex ante** (this lesson) plus reaction to the defect **ex post** (l7 T1) together form a complete system.
