## Introduction

**Last lesson of Week 3**. Today we close the frame of the week: **the processes of a working day** — from entry to exit. We started with the lesson "who you are" (l1), went through transport (l2), the log yard (l3), the timber stack (l4), feeding the operator (l5), hand tools (l6), tidying (l7). **Today the final act of the working day**: **marking material** and **the report**.

Every log, every board, every pallet in the sawmill has **its own history**. It must. Because if it doesn't — **the sawmill falls into chaos within a week**. The customer ordered pine class A, got spruce class C. The warehouse keeper handed out "this many cubic metres" and doesn't know which boards came from which delivery. A complaint comes in after a month — **nobody knows** who cut, when, from which log. **The whole value of the sawmill** rests on a **paper trail** (or rather a **chalk and marker trail**).

As a helper **you are the first link** of this tracing chain. You mark **logs on the yard** (species, diameter, delivery number, date), **timber after cutting** (species, class, dimensions, operator, date), **stacks at the storage area** (order, customer, status). Five minutes a day spent on markings = **hours of work saved** for the warehouse keeper, the foreman, the manager.

That's why this lesson is **not just about "how to scribble chalk marks"**. It's about **the helper's role in the sawmill's information system**. And it's at **the end of the day** — because that's how the day closes: **marking + report to the foreman**.

The week's frame: today you'll see **the whole day from morning to evening** — how the individual T3 lessons make up a **coherent sequence**. You are a helper (l1) → you go to the station → you handle transport (l2), the yard (l3), the stack (l4), the line (l5), tools (l6), tidying (l7) → **you mark** and **report** (l8). And tomorrow **the same**.

## Objectives

After this lesson:

- You know the **four types of markings** in a sawmill: **logs** (species, diameter, date), **timber** (species, class, dimensions, operator), **storage stacks** (order, customer, status), **waste** (fraction, destination).
- You can use **coloured chalk** (species colour code), **waterproof marker** (permanent marker on wood), **attached label** (pallets with an order).
- You understand **where exactly** marking is done (on log ends, on the sides of boards, on pallet labels) and **why you must not rub off** someone else's markings.
- You know the **standard colour convention** (example): **red = oak**, **green = pine**, **blue = spruce**, **white = aspen/poplar**, **black = beech**, **yellow = larch** (but note: the convention depends on the plant, check with the foreman).
- You can **make a shift report** for the foreman: what you did, how much, what problems, workstation status.

## Content

### Why mark — the economics of information

Imagine a sawmill **without markings**. After the shift the warehouse keeper gets 50 boards **thrown onto a pallet**. He has to know: species? class? where from? for whom? He has to **guess** — or **check each separately** (20 minutes per pallet, 8 hours a day = **not a work day, just checking**).

**With markings**: on the side of each board there is **one chalk mark** ("green vertical = pine class B, from delivery 23.04, operator Kowalski"). The warehouse keeper sees **within 3 seconds** and moves on. **Savings**: 7 hours of work daily + **zero mistakes**.

**Economics**: a large sawmill (100 employees) handling **500 pallets a day**. Without markings = **3–4 checker positions** at 5000 zł/month = **20 000 zł/month** in costs. With markings = **0 positions**, because helpers mark in passing, during work. **Monthly saving**: 20 000 zł. **Annual**: 240 000 zł. **This is one lesson you learn in the sawmill — paid for by one OHS lesson of your hand.**

::: info
**In export sawmills** (e.g. oak boards to Germany, the Netherlands) marking is **legally required** — EU regulation 995/2010 on timber (EUTR), the CPR regulation for structural timber. Lack of identification = **pallet held at the border** + fine **10 000–50 000 zł**. Marking = **compliance**, not just tidiness.
:::

### Four types of markings — what to write where

**Type 1: Logs on the yard.**

On the **log end** (the round side, not along) you write:
- **Species** (abbreviation: **DB** oak, **SO** pine, **ŚW** spruce, **BK** beech, **BRZ** birch, **TOP** poplar, **OL** alder, **JS** ash, **MO** larch).
- **Diameter** (in cm, the largest): 45 cm → you write "45".
- **Delivery date** (dd.mm): 23.04.
- **Delivery number** (if any): "D23" (delivery 23).

**Example**: "**DB 45 23.04 D23**" on the end of an oak log 45 cm, delivered on 23 April, delivery no. 23.

Two tools: **chalk or oil paint stick**. White chalk is standard. **Coloured** chalk/stick is used for **species coding** (if the sawmill uses a colour system — see below).

**Type 2: Timber (boards, baulks, beams).**

On the **side of the board** (because the end may be hidden in the stack) you write:
- **Species** (abbreviation as above).
- **Quality class** (A/B/C or 0/I/II/III per PN-EN 1611-1).
- **Dimensions** ("32×150×3000" = thickness × width × length mm).
- **Operator** (initials: "JK" = Jan Kowalski).
- **Date**: dd.mm.

**Example**: "**SO B 32×150×3000 JK 23.04**" — pine class B, dimensions 32 × 150 × 3000 mm, Jan Kowalski cut on 23 April.

Tool: **wood marker** (e.g. Markal Paintstick — wax-oil paste, waterproof) or **chalk**. For EXPORT timber — **stencil + spray** (standard look).

**Type 3: Storage stacks.**

A stack is NOT a single board — it's a **group of hundreds**. The marking goes **on a pallet/stack label** (hangs on the side, a paper sheet in plastic):
- **Order number** (e.g. "ZL-2026-0187").
- **Customer** (company name).
- **Species + class + dimensions** (for a uniform stack).
- **Number of boards** (sometimes).
- **Date the stack was completed**.
- **Status** ("drying", "ready to ship", "complaint").

**Example label**:
```
ZL-2026-0187 / BUDMEX Sp. z o.o.
SO B 32×150×3000
quantity: 52 pcs. (ca. 0.75 m³)
Date: 23.04.2026
Status: DRYING (until 23.10.2026)
```

Material: **an A4 sheet**, in a **plastic sleeve**, **attached with a clip** to the side of the stack. Alternatively a **wooden tag** with burnt marks.

**Type 4: Waste.**

Seemingly "nothing" — but here too there's marking. On the **waste container** by the workstation, written on a plate:
- **Fraction** (sawdust/chips/large scrap/bark/metal).
- **Status** ("full — to be removed", "filling up").

You don't write on individual pieces of waste — it would waste time too quickly.

### Colour convention — an example

**It is not a universal standard** — **it differs between sawmills**. In your sawmill you will **ask the foreman** on the first day. The most common convention:

| Chalk/marker colour | Wood species |
|---|---|
| **Red** | Oak (DB) |
| **Green** | Pine (SO) |
| **Blue** | Spruce (ŚW) |
| **White** | Aspen, poplar (TOP, OL) |
| **Black** | Beech (BK) |
| **Yellow** | Larch (MO) |
| **Brown** | Birch (BRZ) |
| **Orange** | Ash (JS) |

**Why colours?** Because from a distance you **see** the species without needing to read the abbreviation. The warehouse keeper ten metres away looks at the stack "everything green" and knows: pine, all pine, no mistake.

**Additionally**: **quality classes** sometimes coded:
- **One line (|)** = class A / I (the best).
- **Two lines (||)** = class B / II (medium).
- **Three lines (|||)** = class C / III (structural, lowest quality).
- **Circle (O)** = reject, for re-cutting or waste.

::: example
**Scenario in the sawmill**: you enter the storage yard. Task: "bring a pallet of pine class B boards, order ZL-187". You see a **green stack, with two lines**, with the label "ZL-2026-0187". That's the stack. You **don't have to** read each board individually — the colour and the number of lines have already **told you**. **You save 10 minutes** on each order. **10 orders a day** = **100 minutes** = **1.5 hours a day** = **1/5 of a working day**. That's why colour conventions exist.
:::

### Marking technique — how, where, when

**Logs on the yard**:
- **When**: right after unloading, before they are thrown into a pyramidal stack.
- **Where on the log**: the **end** (round side closer to you), **one side** (on the side visible from the transport corridor).
- **With what**: oil chalk with a thick mark (writes on wet bark, doesn't wash off in rain for 2–4 weeks).
- **How large**: **letters 5–10 cm high** — visible from 10 m.

**Timber** (boards, baulks):
- **When**: right after cutting, on the receiving conveyor or during sorting.
- **Where on the board**: the **side** (not the end — the end will be hidden in the stack), **the end closer to the road** (visible from the aisle).
- **With what**: oil/wax marker Markal (or similar). Sometimes chalk.
- **How large**: **letters 2–3 cm high**.

**Stack labels**:
- **When**: after the stack is completed (the foreman or you, under his instructions).
- **Where**: on a **clip attached to the side of the stack**, **eye height** (1.5–1.7 m) — so it can be seen from afar.
- **With what**: a printed label in plastic (or written by hand with a thick marker).

::: tip
**The "one board, one label" rule** doesn't work in mass production. Instead, a **group marking** is used: a batch of boards cut from the same log gets **one mark of species + class + date**. Only the **first and last** boards in the stack are marked individually (so that in case of a complaint the origin of a particular board can be reconstructed).
:::

### What NOT to do regarding markings — work ethics

**Don't rub off another helper's or operator's markings.** Even if they look "unnecessary". That mark has an informative function for **someone else** in the chain. Rubbing off = **deliberate destruction** of data. In an export sawmill = **potentially a crime** (falsifying material information).

**Don't add made-up data.** If you don't know the species (a log looks like pine but you're not sure) — **ask the foreman**, don't write "SO" at random. Adding false information = **a customer complaint** + **loss of sawmill reputation**.

**Don't forget to mark.** An unmarked board ends up on a pallet labelled "unidentified", which in the worst case = **to be thrown out** (the warehouse keeper won't spend 30 min identifying it). **Rule: mark it right after cutting, not "in a minute"**.

**Don't waste chalk/markers.** One oil marker costs 15–30 zł, lasts for 300–500 boards. One chalk 5 zł, lasts for 50 boards. **Save**: draw **thinly**, **clearly**, **in the right place** — not "kilograms of chalk" but legible 3 cm letters.

### Shift report to the foreman — end of day

Before leaving the hall (typically 15:45–16:00, 15 minutes before the official end) — **a report to the foreman**. It is NOT "good evening, goodbye". It is a **short, technical report**.

**Standard 4 questions**:
1. **What did I do?** (The day's tasks, briefly: "I sorted pine timber, built 3 stacks, helped unload 2 log deliveries".)
2. **How much did I do?** (Numbers: "200 boards sorted, 3 stacks of about 60 boards each, 15 logs unloaded".)
3. **What problems were there?** (If any: "circular saw #3 was loud for an hour, the operator said it was a bearing", "there are no 22 mm stickers, I used replacement 18 mm for stack XY" — the foreman **must know**, to pass it on).
4. **Workstation status?** (Briefly: "swept, sawdust taken out, tools in the cupboard" — confirmation that everything was properly closed out).

The report **takes 2–3 minutes**. The foreman notes it in the **shift diary** (or an ERP system), uses it tomorrow for planning, passes it further in meetings with management.

::: warning
**Don't lie in the report.** If you saw a problem — say so. If you didn't finish a task — admit it. A lie in the report will be detected **the next day** (the foreman will check), and your reputation will suffer. A **honest report with a problem** ("I didn't finish the third stack, I have to complete it tomorrow") is better than **a dishonest confirmation** ("everything done").
:::

### The day's frame — from "entry" to "report"

Let's see how the **whole day of a helper** looks in the context of Week 3:

- **7:00** — arrival, PPE check (l1 set the role, you know who you are and what you do).
- **7:10–9:30** — **manual transport** (l2) of logs and materials between the yard and the station. **Log yard** (l3) — you wedge new deliveries, help unload.
- **9:30–9:45** — break.
- **9:45–12:30** — **feeding the operator** (l5) — you work in the production line. **Hand tools** (l6) — when something needs trimming. **Reception + sorting**.
- **12:30–13:00** — lunch.
- **13:00–15:30** — **building stacks** (l4) with stickers. Continued feeding. **Tidying** (l7) after every operation.
- **15:30–15:45** — **final tidying** (l7 moment 1) + **marking** (l8) of completed stacks + review of what was done.
- **15:45–16:00** — **shift report** (l8) to the foreman.
- **16:00** — exit.

**Each T3 lesson has its place** in this day. Tomorrow **the same**. This is your **helper's rhythm**. After 3 months of this routine — you have automatism, confidence, **you start to understand what is missing** (maybe a wedge has rotted, maybe stickers have run out, maybe the saw rattles differently) — and **you report to the foreman before he even asks**.

**This** is the professionalism of a helper. Not "fast cutting, 100 boards per hour", but **awareness of the whole day**, **your own place**, **the state of materials**, **the state of the workstation**, **the state of the report**.

## Key terms

- ✍️ **Oznaczanie drewna** (pl) — timber marking (en) — marcado de la madera (es) — маркування деревини (uk). Writing information (species, class, dimensions, date, operator) on logs and timber. A sawmill standard, an obligation for export (EUTR).

- 🎨 **Konwencja kolorów gatunków** (pl) — species colour convention (en) — convención de colores por especie (es) — кольорова конвенція порід (uk). Each species has a chalk/marker colour (for example: red = oak, green = pine). The convention is **plant-specific** — check with the foreman.

- 🏷️ **Etykieta stosu** (pl) — stack label (en) — etiqueta de la pila (es) — етикетка штабеля (uk). An A4 sheet in a plastic sleeve with order number, customer, species, dimensions, status. Clipped to the side of the stack at eye height.

- 📝 **Markal / pisak olejowy** (pl) — Markal / oil paint stick (en) — Markal / rotulador oleoso (es) — Markal / олійний маркер (uk). A wax-oil paste on a stick, **waterproof**, writes on any wood (wet, dry). A standard for timber marking.

- 🪵 **Skróty gatunków** (pl) — species abbreviations (en) — abreviaturas de especies (es) — скорочення порід (uk). DB oak, SO pine, ŚW spruce, BK beech, BRZ birch, TOP poplar, OL alder, JS ash, MO larch. Universal in the Polish industry.

- ⭐ **Klasy jakości tarcicy A/B/C (lub 0/I/II/III)** (pl) — timber quality classes A/B/C (en) — clases de calidad A/B/C (es) — класи якості A/B/C (uk). Quality markings per PN-EN 1611-1 (for softwoods) or PN-EN 975 (for hardwoods). A/I the best (no defects), C/III structural.

- 📋 **Raport zmiany** (pl) — shift report (en) — parte de turno (es) — звіт зміни (uk). The helper's closing report to the foreman: what I did, how much, problems, workstation status. 2–3 minutes before leaving. Key for planning tomorrow.

- 🌍 **EUTR (rozporządzenie UE 995/2010)** (pl) — EUTR (EU Timber Regulation) (en) — EUTR (Reglamento UE sobre la Madera) (es) — EUTR (Регламент ЄС з деревини) (uk). EU law requiring timber traceability on the market. No markings = export held up + fine.

- 🔖 **Identyfikowalność (traceability)** (pl) — traceability (en) — trazabilidad (es) — простежуваність (uk). The ability to reconstruct the history of every board (which log, who cut it, when, for whom). The basis of complaints, audits, export.

- 🎯 **Klamra dnia** (pl) — daily frame / day loop (en) — marco del día (es) — рамка дня (uk). The structure of a helper's full day: 7:00 arrival + PPE → work → 15:45 markings + report → 16:00 exit. **Repeats every day** — your working rhythm.

## Trainer's notes

**Pedagogical goal**: to **close Week 3** with a coherent "full day of a helper's work" narrative and convey that **marking + report** is **not an add-on** but **an integral part of the work**, equal with physical cutting/stacking. After this lesson the learner should **understand their role in the sawmill's information system** and **want** to mark well, report honestly.

**120-minute rhythm**:
- **0–10 min**: opening — "**the last lesson of Week 3**". Collect **what learners remember** from l1–l7 (each says one thing). Emphasise: **today we close the frame**. Show the day's timeline on the board (7:00–16:00, 8 hours).
- **10–30 min**: **four types of markings** — real examples. The trainer brings a piece of a log with marking, a piece of a board, a printed stack label. Learners look, touch, decipher: "what does DB 45 23.04 D23 mean?" Active quiz.
- **30–55 min**: **marking technique** — **practice**. Learners get scraps of wood + chalk + Markal + marker. Each writes a "sample label" of a board: SO B 32×150×3000 JK 23.04. The trainer corrects: "too small", "mark too thick", "can't be seen from 5 m". Repetitions.
- **55–75 min**: **colour convention** — show the table, explain that it **depends on the plant**. Exercise: 8 species of wood samples on the table. The learner matches the chalk colour to the species (per the example table). They learn to **see colour = species**.
- **75–100 min**: **day's frame** — **full breakdown** of a typical day in the sequence of T3 lessons. Each lesson (l1–l7) returns in **the context of time**. The learner **draws a timeline themselves** on a sheet. The trainer checks, asks: "At 10:00 where are you? What are you doing? Which lesson do you apply?"
- **100–115 min**: **shift report** — **role-played in pairs**. One = helper, the other = foreman. The helper gives a report (made up day), the foreman listens, asks follow-up questions. Swap roles. The trainer corrects: "too long", "you forgot the problem", "great that you mentioned the sticker shortage".
- **115–120 min**: **closing T3**. Key terms from l8. Emphasise: **T3 finished, T4 is next**. "You now have 75% of the picture of a helper: OHS (T1), material (T2), processes (T3). Tomorrow we start **T4: organisation** — paperwork, communication, planning. **T4 is the grown-up helper, not just skilled hands**."

**Frequent learner mistake**: **they are shy about the report** ("I didn't do anything special, what should I say?"). **Trainer's response**: "The report is NOT **praise of your person**. It is **information for the foreman**. Even 'today I only sorted and marked 200 boards' is **important information** — the foreman knows the work is progressing. A big report is when something went wrong — **those are the most valuable**. Don't be afraid to say 'today I damaged one wedge' — that is **pure gold** for the foreman."

**Practical recommendation**: introduce **a helper's notebook** — a small A6 pad that the learner carries in a pocket. Writes **during the day** observations and numbers (this will be the source of the evening report). 3–5 entries per day: "morning 50 pine boards class B", "saw #3 noisy from 10:30", "22 mm stickers ran out — used 18 mm". In the evening before the report — **reads the sheet**, **gives a structured report**. This practice turns into **a professional helper** in 2–3 months.

**Link to T4**: **Week 4: Organisation** — first day documents (OHS protocol, accident report, time sheet), then team communication (operator, warehouse keeper, foreman), then **weekly planning** (production rhythm, delivery dates, customer orders). **T4 complements T1–T3** with a "**grown-up work**" layer — a helper who **understands the organisation** is promoted faster than one who "only saws well".
