## Introduction

Monday, day six of week two. Week 1 was about **how to operate the machine safely**. Week 2 is about **what goes between the jaws of that machine**. Because the saw is the same, but green oak and seasoned oak behave completely differently: they crack differently, they pinch differently, they overheat the motor differently.

**The character of week 2 is Sofía Ramírez**, 28 years old, a Colombian from Medellín. She has been at the Fundacja EGIDA Sawmill at 12 Dębowa Street in Dębowa Góra for six months. She is the first woman operator in this hall. In M1 she was a helper on the log yard; now she is training as a junior operator. Last Friday Sofía received a delivery: twenty cubic metres of pine, brought out of the forest on Monday morning, seven days ago. Foreman Marek looked at the stack, shook his head and said: "Sofía, measure the moisture before this goes on the bandsaw. If it comes out above thirty-five, we are talking differently."

Sofía took the moisture meter, drove the electrodes into the end of the first log and read: **38%**. Then the second: 42%. The third: 36%. She sat with that number in her head and asked herself the question that is yours today: **what does this number actually mean, and why did the foreman react?**

## Objectives

After this lesson:

1. You understand what **absolute moisture content of wood** (MC, *moisture content*) is and why its value can exceed 100% – because you count the mass of water against the dry mass of wood, not against the wet mass.
2. You know the **three practical ranges** you meet in the sawmill: green wood 50–100%, air-dried wood 15–20%, export lumber 12–18%.
3. You understand the **fiber saturation point (FSP)** 28–30%: above this threshold a change in moisture does not change the dimensions of wood; below it, the dimensions change (wood shrinks and cracks).
4. You distinguish **free water** (in the cell cavities, evaporates first) from **bound water** (in the cell walls, evaporates slowly, below FSP).
5. You know how moisture affects **your work on the bandsaw**: cutting force, blade wear, natural lubrication, smoking, mould, distortion after sawing.
6. You know **why a fresh log must go onto the bandsaw or into the pond within 10–14 days** in summer, 21 days in winter, because after that blue stain and mould fungi set in, and the wood loses its grade.

## Content

### Why wood moisture can exceed 100%

Sofía hears "38 percent" and thinks about it like coffee: the more percent, the more of something in something else. With wood it is different. **Absolute moisture content** is the ratio of the mass of water to the **mass of completely dry wood** (dried in a laboratory oven to 0%, the so-called *oven-dry mass*). The formula is simple:

::: formula
**MC (%) = (wet mass – dry mass) / dry mass × 100%**
:::

If fresh beech weighs 1000 g, and after drying 500 g of wood remains, that means 500 g was water. 500 / 500 × 100% = **100% moisture**. Fresh pine can reach 120–140%, because the water in it weighs more than the wood substance itself. This sounds strange the first time, but it is enough to remember: **we do not count water against wet wood, we count water against dry wood**. This is the standard used by every norm (PN-EN 13183), every moisture meter and every commercial specification in Poland and in Europe.

Why this definition and not the other one? Because dry mass is a stable reference. If you measured moisture against wet mass, then a board at 50% moisture losing half its water would suddenly "be" at 33% (not 25%), and every calculation at the drying kiln would fall apart. Dry mass does not change as water leaves. The wet mass does. Engineers picked the stable reference, and after 150 years of industry practice the whole world has stuck with it.

### Free water and bound water – two waters in one board

Wood is not a sugar cube with water poured over the top. Water in wood sits in two places:

::: info
**Free water** fills the cell cavities (the lumens of vessels, tracheids, resin canals). It is "loose", held only by gravity and surface tension. It evaporates **first**, easily, without changing the dimensions of the wood.

**Bound water** sits in the cell walls, between cellulose and hemicellulose molecules. It is "hooked on" by hydrogen bonds. It evaporates **only after the free water is gone**, slowly, with a lot of resistance, and **as it leaves, the wood begins to shrink, crack, warp**.
:::

This sequence has a name and a number. The name is **fiber saturation point (FSP)**. The number in Polish species is **28–30%** moisture (more precisely: pine and spruce ~30%, oak and beech ~28%).

### Fiber saturation point 28–30% – why this is the most important number of the week

Imagine a sponge that you pull out of a bucket. First water drips out on its own: that is the free water. The sponge is still soft, full, wet, but drops no longer run. In wood this corresponds exactly to 28–30% moisture: **there is no free water left in the cell cavities, but the cell walls are saturated with bound water to the maximum**.

What does this mean in practice?

- **Above FSP (green wood 30–140%)**: the board loses water from the cavities, the weight drops, but **dimensions and shape do not change**. A green board at 80% and a green board at 40% have the same length, width, thickness; only the weight differs.
- **Below FSP (dry wood 28% → 8%)**: the board releases water from the cell walls, and **only now does it begin to shrink across the grain**. Oak shrinks 8–10% in width going from FSP to 8%. Pine 4–6%. Spruce 3–5%. **That number is the difference between a board that matches the order and a board that will not fit into a window frame tomorrow.**

That is why drying has two stages: fast (down to FSP, no risk of cracks) and slow (below FSP, because here every extra degree per hour means cracks in the board). There will be more on this in module 3.

One more practical note on FSP. Shrinkage is not uniform in all directions. Wood shrinks most in the tangential direction (along the growth rings), about half as much in the radial direction (across the rings), and almost not at all along the grain. That is why a flat-sawn board cups and a quarter-sawn board stays flatter. When Sofía plans a cut next week, this will matter: how the log is oriented on the carriage determines whether the boards come out tangential or radial, and therefore how much they will move as they dry.

### Three ranges you see every day

Wood moisture in the sawmill always falls into one of three practical ranges. Remember them like a ruler:

::: example
**GREEN WOOD: 50–100% (felled 1–14 days ago)**
Fresh pine in winter: 70–90%. Fresh pine in summer: 50–70%. Fresh oak: 60–80%. Fresh beech: 65–85%. Goes onto the bandsaw "straight from the forest" or into the water pond.

**AIR-DRIED WOOD: 15–20% (seasoned under a shed for 6–12 months)**
The standard condition for construction lumber in Poland: roof trusses, roof structure, sheathing. In summer, in the shade, wood drops to 15%; in winter it climbs back to 20–22%.

**EXPORT / FURNITURE LUMBER: 12–18% (kiln-dried 3–14 days)**
Required by customers in Germany, the UK, Scandinavia. Living-room furniture: 8–12%. Floorboards over underfloor heating: 7–9%. Exotic parquet: 6–8%.
:::

With her reading of 38%, Sofía landed **above FSP, in the "green" zone**. This means: the wood can be cut right away (many sawmills do this with pine), but it cannot be stored longer than 2 weeks without protection, because mould fungi and blue stain will appear. Foreman Marek had reason to worry: the delivery had already been waiting seven days, with 7 left until the fungus.

Notice what the three ranges do not include: anything between 20% and 30%. That gap is not an accident. It is the zone nobody wants to sit in: too dry to store safely as green, too wet to sell as dry, and right at the edge of FSP where any small change in conditions pushes wood into cracking. Sawmills move material through this zone as fast as possible, either by accelerating drying in a kiln or by keeping stock wet until cutting day. In practice, if a moisture meter shows you 25% on a stack that should be "dry", something has gone wrong: interrupted drying, a leaking shed, or mixed stock. Treat it as a warning, not a reading.

### How moisture changes your work on the bandsaw

For a junior operator this number is not academic. Five things change between 50% and 15%:

::: warning
**1. Cutting force (motor power)**. Wet wood (above FSP) requires **20–30% more power** than the same wood air-dried. A 15 kW bandsaw motor runs on fresh oak at the edge of overload, and on dry oak it runs easily. **If the motor smokes or slows down, check first whether the material is fresher than you expected.**

**2. Blade lubrication**. Green wood releases water and resin (in pine): this is natural lubricant. A blade in green pine wears **more slowly**, but picks up more resin (you have to clean it more often). Dry oak requires water-oil spray lubrication so it does not overheat.

**3. Pinching**. A green board, after being cut, **springs open under internal stress** and can pinch the blade. In dry oak this practically does not occur. In green pine, every tenth large log.

**4. Smoking and burning the kerf**. Wood below 15% (over-dried, rare in summer but it happens with old stock) **smokes from friction heating** even at normal feed rate. The kerf turns brown, then black; the blade glazes over and has to be replaced. The operator has to slow the feed by 30–40%.

**5. Mould and distortion after cutting**. A board cut at 40% moisture and stacked on a pile **will twist** (bow, cup, twist warp) if it does not have **stickers** (spacer strips every 1.5 m). In a week without stickers, a stack of green pine deforms so badly that 15% of the boards go to waste.
:::

### Why a green log has 10–14 days

The last thing Sofía had to understand: **why a delivery from a week ago is already a problem, not a reserve**. A fresh log (above 30% moisture) is the perfect environment for:

- **Mould fungi** (*Aspergillus*, *Penicillium*): 3–7 days from felling, especially in warmth above 15°C. Visually: white, grey, green bloom on the end grain.
- **Blue stain** (*Ceratocystis* fungi): 7–21 days, permanent discoloration of the wood structure. **It does not come off in cutting, it does not come off in planing, it does not come off in drying.** The whole log loses grade A and B, drops to C-D or to firewood.
- **Technical insects** (spruce bark beetle, pine sawyer beetle): lay eggs in the bark within 14 days of felling; larvae bore galleries in the sapwood.

::: tip
**The 14-day rule** (summer) **/ 21-day rule** (winter): from felling the tree in the forest to sawing on the bandsaw or submerging in the pond, no more time than this should pass. For pine and spruce in 30°C heat, **10 days** is the limit. For oak, which is more resistant, up to 28 days. After that time the wood either goes into the water pond (water displaces air, fungi do not grow without oxygen), or gets sprayed with borate.
:::

Sofía's delivery was already 7 days old. Another 7 until the limit. That is why the foreman said "we are talking differently": he had to rearrange the shift plan and move that pine to Monday morning, ahead of the oak that was originally in the schedule.

## Trainer notes

**Leading analogy**: a sponge taken out of a bucket (above FSP = drops run, sponge soft, dimensions constant) → sponge squeezed (FSP) → sponge drying out (below FSP, volume shrinks, hardens). It works visually on every trainee regardless of language.

**Why 28–30%, not a single number**: wood FSP depends on species, but the pine-oak difference is 2%, which in operator practice changes nothing. Teach "around 30%", do not count on precision. Precision you leave to the kiln master in module 3.

**Where this lesson comes back**: m2-w2-l2 measurement (practice), m2-w4-l1 cutting planning (effect of moisture on blade selection), m3-w1-l1 kiln drying (here full understanding of FSP is needed). If the trainee has not grasped FSP, do not go further without a review.

**Traps for migrant trainees**:
- "100% moisture means completely wet": **no**, 100% simply means "as much water as dry wood". Draw the formula on the board.
- Confusing relative humidity (of air) with absolute moisture (of wood). In weather, 80% air humidity means something different from 80% wood moisture. Mark this explicitly.
- "If wet cuts worse, then dry is always better": **no**, wood dried below 8% can burn from friction heat on a circular saw. Fresh pine, because of the resin, actually cuts more easily than seasoned. Each range has its advantages and its traps.

**Sofía case, to expand in discussion**: why did the foreman say "we are talking differently" and not "cut immediately"? Because he also had in the schedule fresh oak from yesterday (0 days, time up to 28), which could wait, and older pine from 7 days (time up to 7), which had to be saved. **Cutting priority is not delivery order: it is species × age order.** This rule comes back in m2-w4-l1 (cutting planning).

**Phonetic glossary EN**: "fiber saturation point" – full pronunciation in the Introduction at the start of the lesson, then the abbreviation FSP. A Ukrainian, Colombian, Venezuelan trainee pronounces "FSP" more easily than the full name. Likewise "MC": let them get used to it, because on the moisture meter only MC will appear.
