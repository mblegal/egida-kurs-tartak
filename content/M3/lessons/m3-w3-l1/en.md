### 7:30, feed rate and cutting pattern

**Feed rate** is the speed at which the saw head moves along the rail during cutting. Measured in feet per minute (imperial Wood-Mizer) or metres per minute. The LT70 panel at EGIDA shows feet/min, Rustam converts in his head to m/min (1 foot = 0.305 m, so 30 ft/min is about 9 m/min).

LT70 range: **0-80 ft/min** (0-24 m/min). The choice depends on four factors:

1. **Wood species**. Soft pine, fast (30-40 ft/min). Hard oak, slow (10-20 ft/min). Beech, medium (15-25 ft/min).
2. **Moisture**. Fresh wood, faster (lower cutting resistance). Dry, slower (higher resistance, more blade heating).
3. **Cut thickness**. Thick boards (50+ mm), slower (longer in the wood, more chips). Thin (below 25 mm), faster (less material removed).
4. **Log diameter**. Large log, slower on the first pass (cutting through the pith itself is the hardest). Small log, faster.

Today's conditions: fresh pine, 28 mm thickness, diameter 32 cm. Wood-Mizer's recommendation in the LT70 DTR, table 3.5: **30-35 ft/min** (9-10.5 m/min). Rustam picks **32 ft/min** as the start (centre of the range). A correction after the first cut is possible (kerf observation, board straightness, motor sound).

**Cutting pattern** (sawing pattern) is the order of cuts and the way the log is rotated. Two basic patterns for pine flooring boards:

**Pattern A: through-cutting the first cant (cant sawing).** The log lies flat on the machine bed. The first cut removes the **slab** (rounded edge with bark). 180-degree rotation, the second cut removes the second slab. 90-degree rotation, the third cut removes the third slab. 180-degree rotation, the fourth cut removes the fourth slab. Now we have a square block, the **cant**. From the cant we cut boards 28 mm thick in parallel. **Advantages**: simple, fast (the operator does not rotate the log often), good yield for medium and large logs (30 cm +). **Disadvantages**: every board contains the pith in the middle of its length, which lowers the strength class (C24 allows a maximum of 25% pith).

**Pattern B: quartering with rotation (quarter sawing).** The log is cut vertically through the pith into two halves. Each half is rotated 90 degrees, cut vertically through the pith axis into quarters. The quarters are cut in parallel into boards. **Advantages**: annual rings in the board perpendicular to the surface (higher mechanical strength, better dimensional stability, a more certain C24 class, sometimes C30). **Disadvantages**: more operations, slower, lower yield for narrow logs (below 30 cm the loss is large), requires log rotation.

Rustam thinks: *"The work order requires C24. A certain class, not C30 and not C16. Pattern A is faster and delivers C24 for Scots pine at 32% moisture without a problem. Pattern B would be better for C30 but the work order does not require that, the operator's workload would be twice as large, the yield 5% worse. **I choose A**, standard cant sawing."*

In KDP-001, the "Cutting parameters" section:
```
Feed rate: 32 ft/min (centre of the range 30-35 for fresh pine 28 mm)
Cutting pattern: A (cant sawing, 4 rotations, first cut removes the slab)
Board thickness: 28 mm (+3 mm allowance for drying and machining to 25 mm target)
Board width: 155 mm (+5 mm allowance to 150 mm target)
Board length: 4050 mm (+50 mm allowance for end trimming to 4000 mm)
Strip strategy: 4 slabs + 8-10 boards from the cant (planned 8 boards from P1 and P2, 9-10 each from P3 and P4)
```

## Content

### 1. Which parameters the operator selects and which are fixed

On the Wood-Mizer LT70 band sawmill (or on the Serra SM40, or on any other stationary band sawmill at EGIDA) not all parameters are a matter of choice. Some are set once at machine installation and never changed, some are forced by the machine's design (blade width, for example), and some are fixed for the work order (the target board dimension is given in the work order, it does not come from the operator's decision).

**Fixed construction parameters** (the operator does not change):
- Blade width: **38 mm** (LT70 uses this width, Serra as well, P1-P3 all 38 mm)
- Blade thickness: **1.07 mm** (Wood-Mizer standard, other thicknesses would require a change of the guide wheels)
- Motor power: **37 kW** electric for EGIDA, LT70 also has a 48 kW diesel version, but that is a purchase decision, not an operator's
- Feed-rate range: **0-80 ft/min** (physical limit of the vertical mast rail and the feed motor)
- Blade tension range: **2200-2400 PSI** safe (the gauge's range is 0-3000, but above 2400 PSI the risk of weld breakage rises exponentially)

**Parameters forced by the work order** (the operator executes, does not decide):
- Wood species (from the stores, selected by the foreman on purchase from the forestry district)
- Target strength class (C24, C30, C16, stated by the client)
- Target board dimension after drying and machining (client's work order)
- Number of boards (from the work order)

**Operator decision parameters**:
1. Choice of blade from the cabinet (steel type, tooth pitch, hook angle)
2. Blade tension within the 2200-2400 PSI range
3. Guide adjustment (3-4 mm gap)
4. Feed rate (10-40 ft/min typically)
5. Cutting pattern (A cant vs B quarter)
6. Order of logs within the work order (which log first, why)
7. Allowances for board thickness (plus 2-4 mm above the target)
8. Moment for parameter correction (after the first cut, after the first 10 cm, after the first board)

That is eight decisions an M3 operator takes every shift. An M2 operator (under supervision) executes parameters given by the foreman in the work order, an M1 operator (helper) carries out auxiliary tasks without parametric decisions. **Parametric independence** is the key difference of M3.

### 2. Blade selection by species and sortiment

From l6 M3 T2 we know: a band sawmill blade has four variables:

- **Steel type**: alloy (alloy steel) vs stellite (steel with a stellite layer on the teeth). Alloy is cheaper, easy to sharpen, dulls faster on hard wood. Stellite holds its edge 3-5 times longer, but costs 2.5 times more and does not tolerate pine resin.
- **Tooth pitch** (TPI, teeth per inch): 7 TPI = sparse (thick boards), 10 TPI = medium (thin boards), 22 TPI = very sparse (large diameters, frozen wood). The number of teeth per inch. A sparser pitch = larger chips = faster cutting, thicker boards. A finer pitch = smaller chips = smoother kerf, thin boards.
- **Hook angle**: 9, 10, 13 degrees. A smaller angle (9) = less aggressive bite, good for hard wood, less risk of tooth breakage. A larger angle (13) = more aggressive, good for soft or frozen wood, higher productivity but greater load on the blade.
- **Tooth sharpening type**: regular steel grinding, grinding plus a stellite tip, induction hardening. EGIDA rotates the first two types.

**Blade-selection table for typical EGIDA situations** (compilation of the Wood-Mizer DTR plus the foremen's experience):

| Species and state | Sortiment | Blade type | Pitch | Angle | Example label |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fresh pine 25-35% | Boards 22-32 mm | Alloy | 7/8 | 10 | ST-38-7/8-A12 |
| Fresh pine | Boards 15-20 mm (thin) | Alloy | 10 | 10 | ST-38-10-A12 |
| Dry pine < 20% | Boards 22-32 mm | Stellite | 9 | 9 | ST-38-9-S12 (gums up with resin more slowly) |
| Fresh oak 30-40% | Boards 22-32 mm | Stellite | 7/9 | 9 | ST-38-7/9-S12 (effective on fresh oak) |
| Dry oak 20-25% | Boards 22-32 mm | Stellite | 9 | 9 | ST-38-9-S12 |
| Beech / ash | Boards 22-32 mm | Stellite | 7/9 | 9 | ST-38-7/9-S12 |
| Frozen (December-February) | Any | Alloy | 22 | 13 | ST-38-22-A12 |
| Large diameter (45+ cm) | Thick boards 40+ mm | Alloy | 22 | 13 | ST-38-22-A12 |

For today's work order (fresh pine 32%, 28 mm boards) the first row: **alloy 7/8, angle 10**. Rustam selected ST-38-7/8-A12 from the stores. Correct.

### 3. Blade tension, a deep influence on cutting

Blade tension on a band sawmill is the force with which the wheels pull the blade vertically. The LT70 gauge shows PSI (pounds per square inch, 1 PSI is about 0.0069 MPa, 2200 PSI is about 15 MPa). Physically: the blade is a cutting cord stretched between two wheels, the tension keeps it straight in the cutting plane. Too little tension, the blade "breathes", flows in the cut, the kerf is wavy. Too much tension, the weld of the blade (the place where the blade was welded into a closed loop) is overloaded, the risk of breakage rises, the typical cost is 145-200 zl per blade plus 40 minutes for replacement.

**LT70 safe range**: **2200-2400 PSI**. The lower limit of 2200 is the minimum for an acceptable straight kerf. The upper limit of 2400 is the point above which the manufacturer (Wood-Mizer) does not guarantee weld durability. In EGIDA practice the choice within the range:

- **2200 PSI**: pine and spruce (soft, resinous), alloy blade, ordinary summer cutting.
- **2300 PSI**: mixed wood within a shift (half pine, half oak), a compromise.
- **2400 PSI**: oak, beech, ash (hard), stellite blade, or frozen wood. Maximum.

**What changes with every 100 PSI upward**:

- A straighter kerf (less "waviness"), especially on knots and internal defects
- The board comes out with more even thickness (less +/- 0.5 mm over 4 m of length)
- Blade vibrations smaller, cutting sound higher and more even
- Blade weld wear grows (average blade life 140-180 cuts at 2200 PSI, 110-140 cuts at 2400 PSI)
- Wheel bearing load grows (but small, below 1% shortening of life per 100 PSI)

For today's work order (fresh pine, alloy, comfortable in operation), **2200 PSI** is the standard choice. Rustam set it that way. For dry pine or a mix of pine with larch (which is harder) he would consider 2300.

**A common beginner's trap**: "just tension it more, the kerf will be better". Not always, because sometimes a better kerf comes from a feed-rate correction (slower), not from tension. And tension above 2400 PSI costs blades more often than it gains in kerf quality.

::: info
**The LT70 gauge does not directly measure PSI in the blade**, it measures hydraulic oil pressure in the tensioner cylinder. The scale is calibrated at machine manufacture (a conversion table, hydraulic pressure to blade PSI). The calibration is durable, but **the cylinder seal** (the same one as in l8), if it leaks, causes the gauge to show a value that is too low. That is why in KDP-001 we record the gauge value together with the date of the last calibration. Wood-Mizer Polska service checks the calibration once a year (inspection B from l8).
:::

### 4. Blade guides, small but critical

Blade guides are four rollers (lower and upper, left and right) through which the blade passes before the cut. Their role: **they hold the blade in a single plane**, they do not let it deflect laterally under the pressure of cutting into wood.

Each guide is adjusted with a screw. The distance of the roller from the blade is measured with a feeler gauge (a universal steel tool with a set of leaves of various thicknesses, at EGIDA from 0.05 to 1.00 mm). The standard gap on the LT70: **3-4 mm**.

- **3 mm** (lower limit): for hard wood, for a stellite blade, better guiding, a slight loss on guide wear (about 20% faster dulling)
- **4 mm** (upper limit): for soft wood, alloy, less friction, longer guide life
- **3.5 mm**: EGIDA standard for typical work, a compromise

**Four adjustment points**: upper left, upper right, lower left, lower right. Each adjusted separately. In theory all four should be identical. In practice the permissible deviation is **0.1 mm** (a 0.1 mm feeler gauge fits at one point, does not fit at the neighbouring one).

**Calibration as an operator procedure before every shift** (if not a blade change):
1. Pull out the feeler gauge at the recommended thickness (e.g. 3.5 mm).
2. Slide it between the guide roller and the blade. It should enter with slight resistance, but without forcing.
3. If it enters too easily (loose), turn the adjustment knob to the right by 1/8 of a turn, try again.
4. If it does not enter at all, turn to the left by 1/4 of a turn, try again.
5. Four points one by one, total time 3-5 minutes.

Lack of guide calibration is one of the three most common causes of a wavy kerf (the other two: too low blade tension, too high feed rate). M1 and M2 operators do not check the guides (the independent operator does), but they watch over the M3 operator's shoulder while he does it.

### 5. Feed rate, the most dynamic parameter

Feed rate is the only parameter that **changes during a shift**. The blade is the same, the tension is the same, the guides are the same. But the feed rate you **correct** based on observation of the cut.

**Start**: Wood-Mizer's DTR recommendation (LT70 table 3.5) or EGIDA's experience. For fresh pine 28 mm boards, as Rustam today: **32 ft/min** (centre of the recommended 30-35 range).

**Correction after the first cut** (the first slab removed, Rustam looks at the cutting surface):

- The surface is **smooth and straight** (the kerf like a sheet of paper, no waviness), the feed rate is OK, leave it at 32.
- The surface shows **gentle "scaling"** in the cutting direction (small peaks every 5-10 cm along the length), the feed rate is **too fast**, reduce to 28-30 ft/min.
- The surface is **wavy** (waves of 1-2 mm amplitude every 20-30 cm), the feed rate is **much too fast**, but more often the cause is a **dull blade** or an **incorrect tension**. Slow to 22-25 ft/min, but first check the blade.
- The board is **thicker at one end**, thinner at the other (wedge-shaped), the feed rate is not the cause, this is **blade alignment** (blade levelness relative to the bed), a service task, report to the foreman.
- The motor sound is **uneven**, "undulating", the feed rate is **too fast**, the motor is approaching overload, slow down by 3-5 ft/min.
- The motor sound is **very high, whistling**, the feed rate is **too slow** (the motor is running "loose", the blade is not biting into the wood, energy is wasted), increase by 3-5 ft/min.

Rustam teaches this correction to Wahan through **named examples**. Every next log is an opportunity to change the feed rate and Wahan learns to listen to the motor.

**Closing entry in KDP-001** (after the shift, in the evening), in the field "Parameters actually applied":
```

Log 1: 32 ft/min throughout, straight kerf, OK
```

### 6. Cutting pattern A (cant sawing), the standard

Pattern A, cant sawing (cutting to a square, then in parallel):

**Stage 1**. The log lies horizontally on the bed, the round face cross-section visible at the front of the machine. Rustam starts the feed, the head moves along the rail, the blade removes the **first slab** (the rounded bulge on the top side of the log, usually 3-5 cm thick at the middle, narrower at the ends). He estimates the slab thickness visually before the start by setting the head height. Result: a log with a flat top.

**Stage 2**. 180-degree rotation of the log (with the hydraulic log turner). The previous top is now the bottom, the previous bottom is the top. Rustam removes the **second slab**. Result: a log with two flat parallel sides, the two original side slabs on the left and right.

**Stage 3**. 90-degree rotation of the log (the same turner). One of the two side slabs is now on top. Rustam removes the **third slab**. Result: a log with three flat sides.

**Stage 4**. 180-degree rotation. The last slab (fourth) is on top. Remove it. Result: **a square cant**, dimensions close to a square. For a pine log of 32 cm diameter, the square comes out at about 23 x 23 cm (sides with some slippage, losses 20-25% in the 4 slabs).

**Stage 5 and onwards**. From the cant Rustam cuts in parallel boards 28 mm thick. Counting: 23 cm / 28 mm = 8.2, meaning **8 clean boards** plus a 9th that is a "half board" (a remnant of 15-20 mm), which goes to firewood or to machining as a strip. Eight boards of dimensions 28 x 155 mm (width limited by the cant cross-section) and length 4050 mm.

**Total time of pattern A for a single log 32 cm and 4 m**: 4 slabs at about 35 seconds = 2 min 20 s, plus 8 boards at about 30 seconds (the board is shorter than the log, equally long) = 4 min, plus 4 rotations at about 15 s = 1 min. Total **~7-8 minutes** from the first cut to the last board.

**Typical yield** from pattern A for pine of 32 cm diameter and 4 m: **8 boards x 28 x 155 x 4050 mm = 0.141 m3** from a log of volume **1.04 m3** (raw whole log cylinder pi x 0.16^2 x 4 = 0.322 m3, with a reserve for taper we count 1.04, though sometimes less precisely). Yield coefficient: 40% at low volume, 60% at full metric with the slab included. The formulas differ in the literature, EGIDA uses the simplified "yield = volume of rough lumber / volume of whole log" and for Scots pine 28 mm boards expects **55-65%**.

### 7. Cutting pattern B (quarter sawing), for a higher class

Pattern B, quarter sawing:

**Stage 1**. The log lies horizontally, Rustam sets the first cut so that the blade goes through the centre of the log, through the pith. The cut runs the whole length of the log. Result: **the log in two half-moons** (a D-shape, rich in silhouette).

**Stage 2**. One half-moon is set aside (the helper's trolley), the other is rotated 90 degrees from its original position (the flat side now down, the rounded side up). A cut through the middle of this half-moon, perpendicular to the original cut. Result: **a quarter of the log** (an irregular Y-shape).

**Stage 3**. Each quarter is cut in parallel into boards, starting from the flat inner side (where the pith was). The first two-three boards are the **best**: annual rings perpendicular to the board surface ("quarter-sawn boards"), maximum mechanical strength, highest dimensional stability under moisture changes. Boards from the quarter closer to the bark are weaker (diagonal rings), but still better than in pattern A.

**Stage 4**. Repeat stages 2-3 for the second half-moon.

**Time for a single log 32 cm and 4 m**: 1 dividing cut + 2 quartering cuts + 8-10 boards from 4 quarters = **~12-15 minutes** (about 2 times more than pattern A).

**Typical yield**: **45-55%** (worse than A because of larger geometric losses in the shape of the quarter).

**Strength class**: **C24 certain, C30 probable** (on 3-4 boards out of 10 from each log).

**When to choose B**:
- The work order requires C30 or C35 (client: "I need a certain C30 class for structural elements")
- The work order requires high dimensional stability (furniture, expensive parquet, window joinery)
- The sortiment price justifies the extra labour (oak parquet flooring at 180 zl/m2, worth it)

**When not**:
- A standard C24 work order (today's ZLE-077)
- Little blade life remaining (pattern B wears the blade faster, 15-20% life reduction per log)
- Time pressure (a work order for tomorrow, no 2x the time)

Rustam chose A today. Wahan wrote a question in his notebook: *"When B?"* for the after-shift conversation.

### 8. Documentation of the decision, the KDP-001 card

KDP-001 (Karta Doboru Parametrow, the Cutting Parameter Selection Card) is an A4 form introduced at EGIDA in March 2026 after the ISO 9001 quality audit in February (the auditor pointed out that **the absence of an evidentiary trail** of who set which parameters on a work order is a risk in case of a client complaint). Card structure:

**Header**: work order no., date, operator, machine, helper (if any).

**Section 1, Input raw material**:
- List of logs from the stores (stock no., dimensions, moisture, visible defects)
- Total estimated volume
- Comment on critical defects (if any, e.g. "P3 radial crack 8 cm, caution")

**Section 2, Blade selection**:
- Type, width, thickness, pitch, angle, stock no.
- Date of last sharpening
- Cut counter since sharpening (zero for fresh)
- Justification of the choice (1-2 sentences, why this blade)

**Section 3, Machine settings**:
- Blade tension (PSI, from the gauge)
- Guides (gap in mm, calibrated or not)
- Hall temperature (influences tension drift during the shift)

**Section 4, Cutting parameters**:
- Starting feed rate (ft/min)
- Cutting pattern (A or B)
- Board thickness (allowance stated)
- Width, length (allowances stated)
- Strategy (how many boards from the log are planned)

**Section 5, Parameters actually applied** (filled in at the end of the shift):
- Feed-rate change (from what to what, when, why)
- Tension change (if any)
- Blade replacement during the shift (if any, why)
- Observations of internal defects discovered

**Section 6, Signature**:
- Operator's name, date, end-of-shift time

**Retention**: **2 years on paper** in the sawmill office, **5 years as an electronic scan** in EGIDA OneDrive (work-order folder, access for the foreman and the manager). After 2 years the paper goes to the shredder, the scan stays.

**Document use**:
1. **Client complaint** (board too soft, too short, cracked after 6 months): the foreman opens the KDP, checks whether the operator applied good parameters. If yes, the fault is on the raw-material side (supplier Strzalowo Forest District) or drying. If no, the operator's fault (a corrective conversation, possibly training).
2. **FSC/PEFC audit** (m3-w4-l3): the auditor wants to see a quality trail of the work order, KDP-001 is the first document in the chain.
3. **Training a new operator**: the manager shows good KDPs ("this is how it is filled in"), bad KDPs ("this is how it is not filled in, the blade-choice justification is missing").
4. **Operator's self-assessment**: after 6 months the operator has a stack of 100-150 of his own KDPs, sees patterns in his decisions, learns from his own work.

::: warning
**A false entry in KDP-001** is treated as a false entry in a work journal. It falls back on **Art. 52 § 1 pt. 1 of the Labour Code** (a grave breach of duties), a basis for terminating the employment relationship without notice. EGIDA has had no such event, but the manager once a month spot-checks KDPs against reality (asks the operator about details of the work order, looks at the cutting result). The rule works both ways: an honest record is evidence for the operator that they worked well, a false record is evidence against.
:::

### 9. Common errors of beginning M3 operators

A compilation from EGIDA foreman reports for 2024-2025, parameter-selection errors in operators who moved up to M3 in the first half-year of independent work:

**Error 1. Choosing a blade from memory of the previous shift**. "Yesterday was oak, I took stellite 7/9. Today is pine, I take the same stellite, because it is ready to hand." Effect: stellite on pine gums up with resin in 40 cuts (instead of 140 for alloy), the blade goes for sharpening after one shift, 120 zl cost unnecessarily plus time. **Correct approach**: each shift is a new choice, not an automatic carry-over. KDP-001 forces this reflection.

**Error 2. Maximising tension "for safety"**. "I set 2400 always, because the kerf is the best." Effect: the blade lives 110-140 cuts instead of 140-180, blade costs in the annual budget 25% higher, the weld breaks more often in the cut (interrupted cut, recovery for 145-200 zl extra). **Correct approach**: tension matched to species and blade type, optimal range 2200-2300 PSI for most work orders.

**Error 3. Feed rate "straight to fast"**. "Faster means I get more done, 40 ft/min from the start." Effect: the first board slightly wavy (correction to 32 after the first log is a loss of 20 minutes and 2 boards down to C16 instead of C24), blade wear faster by 15%, motor heating above norm. **Correct approach**: start with the Wood-Mizer DTR recommendation, correct only after the first cut and only based on observation.

**Error 4. Pattern B "just in case"**. "C24 is OK, but I will do B, because higher classes are better, the client will be pleased." Effect: work twice as long, yield 10% worse, the client pays for C24 anyway, not for C30 (because they did not order C30), shift economics 30% worse. **Correct approach**: exactly what the client ordered, not more. The foreman may authorise pattern B only in pre-agreed cases.

**Error 5. No KDP-001 entry "because no time"**. "I will fill it in in the evening." Effect: in the evening the operator remembers about half, the blade and guide details blur with the previous shift, the KDP is imprecise. An ISO auditor will flag it as a non-conformity. **Correct approach**: the KDP is filled in **before the first cut** (sections 1-4), section 5 after the shift.

**Error 6. Copying a colleague's solution without understanding**. "Damian set 30 ft/min yesterday, I take the same." Effect: Damian had dry pine at 18% (different species-state and moisture), 30 ft/min was too slow for fresh pine at 32%, the blade slides, a sub-optimal result. **Correct approach**: every work order has its own parameters, the comparative reference is a previous KDP **from similar conditions**, not the most recently recorded one.

### 10. When the parametric decision is beyond the operator

Not every work order is within the M3 operator's comfort zone. There are situations where the operator should **ask the foreman** before deciding. Three scenarios:

**Scenario A, unknown species**. A work order for "exotic wood" (jatoba, meranti, iroko), which the operator is seeing for the first time. The cutting characteristic, resistance, resins, and final moisture are unknown from experience. **Procedure**: the operator asks the foreman, who consults Wood-Mizer service (Polish hotline, response time 15-30 minutes), obtains a blade and tension recommendation, the decision is written into the KDP with a note "based on consultation with WM Polska, 2026-05-..., ... hours".

**Scenario B, log diameter outside the LT70 range**. The LT70 has a maximum cutting diameter of **67 cm**, minimum **10 cm**. A log of 72 cm (rare, but it has happened that the forest district sets aside a large pine) is outside the range, the operator routes it to another station (Serra SM40 has a max of 80 cm) or the log goes for sectioning (hand cutting with a chainsaw into smaller pieces before the LT70). He does not try to "jam it in", because this risks damage to the machine, blade breakage, and even injury.

**Scenario C, extreme log moisture**. A log "dry as a bone" (below 15%, rare in EGIDA's stores, but a forgotten shelf from 2024 did happen once) or "after a flood" (45%+, swollen, weighing twice what it should). Normal cutting parameters are uncalibrated, the Wood-Mizer DTR recommendation does not cover this. **Procedure**: the operator asks, the foreman decides, the documents are recorded.

**EGIDA rule**: in non-standard cases the independent operator **can always call the foreman before start-up**. The foreman is not annoyed by the questions, indeed he prefers them to accidents and complaints. KDP-001 has a field "Foreman consultation (yes/no, date, time, person)", so we expressly record that the operator asked.

## Key Terms

**Cutting parameter selection** (*dobór parametrów cięcia*, EN *cutting parameter selection*, ES *selección de parámetros de corte*, UK *підбір параметрів різання*): the decision process of the independent operator before starting a work order, covering the selection of blade, tension, guides, feed rate and cutting pattern based on the raw material and the target sortiment.

**KDP-001 card** (*karta doboru parametrów*, EN *cutting parameter selection card*, ES *ficha de selección de parámetros de corte*, UK *картка підбору параметрів різання*): an EGIDA A4 form filled in by the operator before the first cut of a work order, documenting all decision parameters plus the parameters actually applied after the shift.

**Feed rate** (*prędkość posuwu*, EN *feed rate*, ES *velocidad de avance*, UK *швидкість подачі*): the speed at which the saw head moves along the rail during cutting, measured in feet per minute (Wood-Mizer DTR) or metres per minute, LT70 range 0-80 ft/min.

**Blade tension** (*napięcie taśmy*, EN *blade tension*, ES *tensión de la cinta*, UK *натяг стрічки*): the force with which the wheels of the sawmill stretch the blade vertically, measured in PSI (pounds per square inch), LT70 safe range 2200-2400 PSI.

**Blade guides** (*prowadniki taśmy*, EN *blade guides*, ES *guías de la cinta*, UK *напрямні стрічки*): four rollers (lower and upper, left and right) that hold the blade vertical during cutting, adjusted with a feeler gauge in the 3-4 mm range.

**Cutting pattern A cant sawing** (*schemat cięcia do kwadratu*, EN *cant sawing pattern*, ES *corte por pieza cuadrada*, UK *схема різання до квадрата*): the log rotated 4 times, 4 slabs removed to a square cant, boards cut in parallel from the cant. Fast, universal, good yield.

**Cutting pattern B quarter sawing** (*schemat czwartowania*, EN *quarter sawing pattern*, ES *corte por cuarterones*, UK *схема четвертного різання*): the log split through the pith into halves, each into quarters, boards cut from the quarters. Slower, better strength class (C30+) and stability.

**Tooth pitch TPI** (*podziałka zębów*, EN *tooth pitch TPI teeth per inch*, ES *paso de los dientes TPI*, UK *крок зубів TPI*): the number of blade teeth per inch, typically 7/8 TPI for pine, 7/9 TPI for oak, 10 TPI for thin boards, 22 TPI for frozen wood or large diameters.

**Hook angle** (*kąt natarcia*, EN *hook angle*, ES *ángulo de ataque*, UK *передній кут*): the angle at which the blade tooth "enters" the wood, typically 9 degrees for hard wood, 10 degrees for the standard, 13 degrees for frozen.

**Stellite vs alloy steel** (*stal stellitowa vs stopowa*, EN *stellite vs alloy steel*, ES *acero estelita vs aleación*, UK *стелітова сталь vs сплавна*): two types of blade sharpening, stellite holds its edge 3-5 times longer, but costs 2.5 times more and does not tolerate pine resins.

**Production work order** (*dyspozycja produkcyjna*, EN *production work order*, ES *orden de producción*, UK *виробничий наряд*): an A5 document issued by the foreman, containing the raw material, target sortiment, quantity and execution date, without cutting parameters (those are chosen by the operator).

**Allowance** (*naddatek*, EN *allowance / oversize*, ES *sobredimensión / margen*, UK *припуск*): the difference between the raw cutting dimension and the target dimension after drying and machining, typically 3 mm in thickness, 5 mm in width, 50 mm in length for flooring boards 25x150x4000.

**Yield** (*uzysk / wydajność surowca*, EN *yield / lumber recovery*, ES *rendimiento / aprovechamiento*, UK *вихід / коефіцієнт виходу*): the ratio of rough-lumber volume to whole-log volume, for Scots pine 28 mm boards typically 55-65%.

**Independent operator M3** (*operator samodzielny*, EN *independent operator*, ES *operador independiente*, UK *самостійний оператор*): an operator who has completed module 3 of the EGIDA course, takes parametric decisions on a work order without direct supervision by the foreman.

**Cant** (*blat prostokątny*, EN *cant*, ES *pieza cuadrada*, UK *брус / чотиригранний блок*): a rectangular or square block of wood formed after removing 4 slabs in pattern A, from which boards are cut in parallel.
