## Introduction

Tuesday, the seventh day of week 1. Yesterday (lesson 5) Oleh read Rozp. MG 2000 and refused to work on a circular saw without a riving knife. Today the person stepping up to the four-sided planer is **Andrei, a Moldovan from Chișinău, 34 years old**. Andrei already knows this machine: for half a year he was a helper to the same operator, feeding him boards, receiving them on the other side, sweeping chips (you remember him from M1 week 4 lesson 5, when he worked at a saw with a cracked guard? Now he faces a sneakier challenge).

Andrei starts the planer, sets the thickness to 27 mm, feeds in an oak board. The machine cuts. After fifteen boards Andrei tastes something sweet in his mouth. After thirty he notices that the dust bin behind the planer **is not filling up as fast as it should**. He pushes the next board through and looks under the machine. **Chips are falling on the floor. The extraction is not working.**

Andrei hits STOP (left hand by reflex, the way Andrij did in lesson 3). He stops the planer and goes to check. The filter in the cyclone behind the building is **completely clogged**. Two months without a change, differential pressure 2400 Pa instead of 800 Pa (replacement limit is 1200 Pa). The extraction produces 5 m/s instead of 22 m/s. Chips fall on the floor, and **fine dust hangs in the air and enters Andrei's lungs**.

This is a lesson about the dust you can see, and the dust you cannot. Coarser chips are not dangerous: you sweep them up with a shovel. **Fine dust from oak and beech is a Group 1 carcinogen according to IARC**, as certain a cause of cancer as asbestos or tobacco smoke. And it is **explosive**: 30-50 g/m³ in the air plus a spark from a bearing equals a dust fire that engulfs the whole hall in five seconds.

Today you will learn three protection strategies (in order from most effective), you will learn the 10-second test that tells you whether the extraction works, and you will see when you are required to change the filter and when to report a failure to the foreman.

## Objectives

After this lesson:

1. You understand why dust from oak, beech, elm, mahogany, and walnut is classified by IARC as a **Group 1 carcinogen** and why it cannot be "aired out" or "swept up after the shift".
2. You know the three protection strategies in order of effectiveness: **workstation extraction** (source), **central extraction** (zone), **FFP3 mask** (body), and you know why the mask is third, not first.
3. You can perform the **10-second test** that checks whether the extraction at your machine works, and you recognise three signs of failure (loss of suction, change of tone, chips on the floor).
4. You know when to change the filter (pressure difference >1200 Pa, mass saturation, visible clogging) and who is authorised to do it (operator for the bag, service technician for the cyclone filter).
5. You understand why oak and beech dust is also explosive: you know the MIE (minimum ignition energy 10-100 mJ) and the minimum explosive concentration (30-50 g/m³), and you know which three sources of sparks must never be tolerated in the zone.

## Content

### Why wood dust kills twice

You work eight hours a day, five days a week, for twenty years around oak dust. The sum: 40,000 hours of breathing fine dust. **One in fifty to one in a hundred operators will develop nasal sinus cancer** (adenocarcinoma nasal). Italian statistics 1995-2015 for furniture joiners show a 50-fold higher risk than in the general population.

This is because **IARC (International Agency for Research on Cancer)** in 1995 classified **hardwood dust (oak, beech, elm, mahogany, walnut)** as **Group 1 carcinogens**, the highest category, alongside asbestos, benzene, plutonium, and tobacco smoke. "Group 1" means: **confirmed human carcinogen, based on epidemiology, not only on laboratory studies**. You cannot dismiss it with the argument "nobody in my family ever had it". In Poland the **NDS (najwyższe dopuszczalne stężenie, the Polish occupational exposure limit) of 2 mg/m³ for oak and beech dust** applies (Rozp. MPiPS 2018, Polish Ministry of Labour Regulation 2018).

The second danger is faster. Fine dust (below 500 micrometres) at a concentration of **30-50 g/m³ in the air** forms an **explosive mixture**. A spark from a bearing (minimum ignition energy, **MIE 10-100 mJ**, small) ignites the cloud. The shock wave lifts the dust settled on the ceiling beams: a second explosion, larger. Sawmills in the USA report on average 30 dust fires per year (USCSB 2006-2020), 3-5 of them fatal.

::: warning
**Two faces of the killer**: coarse dust (visible, on the floor) you sweep with a broom, divide into sections, and clear away. Fine dust (invisible, suspended in the air) is what kills you. **Working too long without extraction means your lungs and bronchi take on a dose that at age 55 will give you a diagnosis**. The immediate risk is fire; the long-term risk is cancer. Both are removed by **workstation extraction** and an **FFP3 mask**. Without them you have no right to work.
:::

### Three protection strategies in order of effectiveness

The occupational-health hierarchy says: **source > pathway > person**. Dust must be caught where it is created, not only at your face.

**Strategy 1: workstation extraction (at the source)**

The extraction pipe ends in a **nozzle** next to the cutting tool. For a circular saw the nozzle is under the table and above the blade. For a four-sided planer there are 4 nozzles, one above each knife, plus 2 under the rollers. Air velocity at the nozzle: **20-25 m/s** (this is transport speed: fast enough to lift oak dust, not only pine chips). At the very inlet of the nozzle the velocity drops to **1-3 m/s**, and that is the moment when the dust is either drawn in or escapes.

**Strategy 2: central extraction (zonal)**

All the machines in the hall are connected by a pipe network to a cyclone outside. The cyclone separates coarse chips (they drop into a bin), while fine dust flies on to a bag filter. Cleaned air returns to the hall or is vented outside. **Pressure difference on the filter**: new filter = 200-400 Pa, dirty filter = 1200 Pa (limit), fully clogged = 2500+ Pa. Above 1200 Pa the transport velocity drops, dust settles in the pipes, and there it waits for a spark.

**Strategy 3: FFP3 mask (on you)**

Back to lesson 2: **FFP2 filters 94% of the aerosol, FFP3 filters 99%**. For oak and beech dust: **FFP3 is mandatory** (Rozp. MZ 2019, Polish Ministry of Health Regulation 2019, on work with carcinogens, § 12). Not FFP2, not an ordinary half-mask, not a handkerchief. FFP3 with an exhalation valve, fitted to the face (palm-seal test: on inhalation the mask must suck tight to the face).

::: info
**Why the mask is third, not first**: 8 hours in an FFP3 mask is 8 hours of increased breathing resistance (+10-15% more chest-muscle work). Can you endure that every day? Fatigue adds up. A working extraction means the mask is only needed during failures and in transition zones. A broken extraction means the mask is on for 8 hours a day, and you change jobs within 2 years.
:::

### The 10-second test: is the extraction working?

Before starting the machine (this is an extension of point 3 of the checklist from lesson 4):

1. **Stand 30 cm from the extraction nozzle** at the cutting tool.
2. **Turn on the extraction** (the button is usually red on the control cabinet).
3. **Hold a strip of A4 paper (2 × 15 cm)** at the height of the nozzle.
4. **Count: "one thousand one, one thousand two… one thousand ten"**.
5. The strip must be **drawn toward the nozzle** and stay there without your hand.

If the strip barely tilts or falls down: extraction weak, STOP. If the strip flies fast and is sucked into the pipe: OK, extraction working. Ten seconds that can save your lungs.

**Three signs of extraction failure during work**:

- **Chips fall on the floor** instead of disappearing (instead of going into the pipe).
- **The tone of the fan motor changes** (higher when clogged, lower when the drive is loose).
- **Dust visible in the air** while the machine is running (in a normally operating sawmill the air is clear).

::: tip
**Pocket rule of dust**: "You see dust in the air, you have a problem. You see chips on the floor, you also have a problem. You see neither dust nor chips: take out the paper strip and check." Fine dust at a concentration of 2 mg/m³ is **invisible to the naked eye**. The only method for the operator: flow test plus smell-fatigue test (after 2 hours of work you no longer smell the wood = probably the extraction is weak, you are sitting in a high concentration, and the receptors in your nose have shut down).
:::

### When to change the filter

**Workstation extraction bag (at the machine)**: change when **3/4 of the volume** is full or **the weight exceeds 15-20 kg** (the limit for an operator without help). Rozp. MPiPS 2017 on manual transport: man 30 kg continuously, 50 kg occasionally. A 25 kg bag is already at the edge of a spinal injury. Do not wait until it is full.

**Central cyclone filter**: replaced by the service technician when the **pressure difference exceeds 1200 Pa** (the manometer is on the cabinet). The operator **does not replace** the cyclone filter on their own: it requires disconnecting the installation, opening the chamber, vacuuming the dust in a P3 mask, fitting a new bag, and sealing. The operator **reports to the foreman** when the manometer exceeds 1000 Pa (margin); do not wait for 1200 Pa.

::: example
**Case Andrei, Monday morning**: Andrei notices chips on the floor after 30 boards. He hits STOP. He walks out to the cyclone, looks at the manometer: 2400 Pa. Filter overloaded. Two options:

- **The bad one**: "I'll push through these 200 boards, the shift ends at 15:00 anyway." Result: 2 mg/m³ × 8 h = a weekly dose in one morning. Plus the risk of a dust fire.
- **The good one**: Andrei goes to the foreman: "§ 5 of Rozp. MZ 2019, NDS 2 mg/m³, filter at 2400 Pa. I am not starting." The foreman calls the service company. Filter replaced in 90 minutes. Andrei works from 10:30, without exposure. The foreman notes in the log: "extraction hall 2 – filter replaced after operator report."

Andrei chooses the good option. In the evening the foreman tells him: "Good decision. Next time report at 1000 Pa, not at 2400." Andrei learns another thing: **preventive reporting** gets ahead of the failure.
:::

## Check yourself

**Question 1**: You start the planer and you see that chips are falling on the floor instead of going into the extraction pipe. What do you do?

*Good answer*: STOP, you do not keep working. You walk out to the cyclone and check the manometer. If >1200 Pa, you report to the foreman "filter to replace". If <1200 Pa, you check the pipe under the machine (whether it has come loose), and report the problem.

**Question 2**: A colleague says: "Only oak is carcinogenic, pine is not. You don't need a mask for pine." What do you answer?

*Good answer*: not true. Hardwood dust (oak, beech, elm, mahogany, walnut) is IARC Group 1; softwood dust (pine, spruce, poplar) is Group 3 (not classified, but irritating). FFP3 mask is mandatory for all hardwood species. For pine FFP2 is acceptable, but only if NDS 3 mg/m³ is kept. In practice you wear FFP3 always, because both species are in the hall.

**Question 3**: What is the "10-second test" and why do you do it?

*Good answer*: an extraction flow test before starting the machine. You hold an A4 strip at the nozzle, turn on the extraction, count to 10. The strip must be drawn in. If not: extraction weak, you report. You do this every day, as an extension of point 3 of the checklist from lesson 4.

**Question 4**: Why is the FFP3 mask the third strategy, not the first?

*Good answer*: occupational-health hierarchy: source > pathway > person. The mask loads the respiratory muscles 10-15% for 8 hours. The extraction removes the dust at the source. The mask is the last line of defence, not the first. A well-working extraction means the mask is used only during failures and in transition zones. A bad extraction means the mask is on for 8 hours, and you burn out in 2 years.

Tomorrow morning:

2. During work: every 30 minutes look under the machine – **chips on the floor or in the pipe?**

## Trainer notes

The dust topic is pedagogically hard: **the consequences are delayed by 15-25 years** (nasal sinus cancer develops 20-30 years after exposure), so migrant trainees aged 25-40 think "this doesn't concern me". A strong visual stimulus is needed. During this lesson I recommend showing a photograph of **the nose of a patient with adenocarcinoma** (CIOP-PIB, the Polish Central Institute for Labour Protection, has training materials; ethically, use them only after warning the group). The second stimulus: **filter weight**. Show that an empty filter weighs 2 kg, a clogged one 25 kg. Those 23 kg are the dust that **was going to the lungs**.

The Andrei case can be played as a short demonstration: the trainer shows the cyclone manometer in the hall (it is always near the door), reads the current value, explains what it means. Five minutes. It stays with the trainees forever.

The 10-second test should be **physically practised** by every trainee. An A4 strip cut out. Extraction on. The trainee holds the strip themselves and sees the result themselves. Knowledge gained through this experience has a different durability than knowledge from a lecture.

Week 1 ritual now extended: **check yourself (l2) → check STOP (l3) → check machine 5 points (l4) → check compliance with the law (l5) → check extraction (l6)**. These are the five layers of the "check" ritual before every start-up. Not all in full resolution every day: PPE and STOP are a daily ritual, the 5-point checklist is the daily paperwork, Rozp. MG 2000 is "once per machine" (checked when you receive a new machine or after an overhaul), and extraction is the daily paper-strip test.
