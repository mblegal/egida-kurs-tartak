## Introduction

This is the **last lesson of Week 2**. Today we close the topic of **"the material you work with"** with one of the most important concepts in a sawmill: **the difference between wet wood and dry wood**.

Sounds trivial. Everyone knows that wet wood is heavier than dry. But that is **only one third** of the truth. Three things change fundamentally when wood goes from **fresh state** to **dried state**:

1. **Weight**: a wet board can weigh **twice as much** as the same board after drying.
2. **Behaviour during transport**: a wet board is **flexible**, a dry one is **stiff and brittle**.
3. **Way of stacking**: a wet board must have **ventilation** to dry evenly. A dry one can be **packed tight** without risk.

A helper who **doesn't understand this difference** easily makes mistakes. He picks up a dry board "any old way" and breaks it. He builds a wet stack "like always", and after a week the boards are warped. He tries to **lift a wet oak log alone**: ends up in hospital with a back injury.

In this lesson you'll learn about the **weight difference** (and how to feel it with your hand), the **technique of building a wet stack** (with stickers), and the **transport safety** of wet material.

After this lesson **we close Week 2**. The next two weeks are **Processes** (W3) and **Organization** (W4), that is, **what you do** and **how you do it** during a working day. But the foundation is what we built in W1 (safety) and W2 (material). **Without it, you won't understand the rest.**

## Goals

After this lesson:

- You understand the difference between **fresh (wet) wood** and **kiln-dried wood**: in three dimensions: **weight**, **behaviour**, **stacking**.
- You know that **wet wood weighs 1.5–2 times more** than dry, and you can **feel** it with your hand after one day of work.
- You understand why a **wet lumber stack** must be built with **stickers**: and why a dry one can go without them.
- You know that wood **continues to "work"** after drying, and how that affects stacking and storage.

## Content

### Why wood is wet when it arrives at the sawmill

A tree is alive. The trunk of a tree is **a giant vessel** in which **water flows from the roots to the leaves**. When you fell the tree, **the water doesn't disappear**: it stays in the wood.

How much? **A lot.** **Fresh, just-felled wood** contains **30–60% water** of total mass. Specifically:

- **Fresh pine**: about 40–55% water.
- **Fresh spruce**: about 35–50% water.
- **Fresh oak**: about 30–45% water (less, because denser).
- **Fresh beech**: about 35–45% water.
- **Fresh birch**: about 40–60% water (a lot, because it's a "watery" tree).

That means **almost half the mass** of a fresh board **is water**. After **drying**, wood has only **8–18% water** (depending on species and use).

::: info
**Furniture wood** is dried to **8–10% moisture**. **Construction wood** (rafters, trusses), to **12–18%**. **Pallet wood**: **20%** is enough. The drying target always comes from the **intended use**. So in one sawmill you have **different drying levels**: and as a helper **you learn to tell** which stack is which.
:::

### The weight difference: your back will tell you

The simplest and quickest noticeable effect of wet wood: **weight**.

**Specific numbers** (a 4 m × 25 cm × 4 cm board = about 0.04 m³):

| Species | Wet | Dry | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pine | 22–28 kg | 14–18 kg | ×1.5 |
| Spruce | 20–25 kg | 13–16 kg | ×1.5 |
| Oak | 45–55 kg | 30–38 kg | ×1.5 |
| Beech | 40–50 kg | 28–35 kg | ×1.5 |
| Birch | 28–35 kg | 18–24 kg | ×1.5 |

**On average**: wet wood weighs **1.5–2 times more** than dry. That means:

- **A wet pine board** (4 m, fresh), weighs as much as **a dry oak board** of the same size.
- **A wet oak board** (4 m, fresh), weighs **almost 50 kg**. **The limit for one man** in a single lift.

::: warning
**Lifting limits** in Polish labour law (Regulation of the Minister of Labour, 2017):
- **Man**: 30 kg in a single lift (repeatedly), 50 kg in a single lift (occasionally).
- **Woman**: 12 kg in a single lift (repeatedly), 20 kg in a single lift (occasionally).

**A wet oak board 4 m = 50 kg = the absolute limit for one man**, and only occasionally. Repeatedly, **not allowed**. You always work **in pairs** with wet oak and beech lumber.
:::

### How to recognize wet wood by sight?

A helper who sees hundreds of boards a day **must learn to tell** wet from dry **without weighing**. A few signs:

**1. Colour.** Fresh, wet wood has a **brighter colour** and a **more vivid tone**. After drying, the colour **dulls**, becomes **more matt** and slightly darker.

**2. Surface.** A wet board has a surface that is **slightly slippery**, **a bit cool to the touch**. A dry one, **a dry, warm touch**, sometimes you see **small surface cracks**.

**3. Board ends.** Wet ends are **smooth and even**. Dry ends often have **small end splits** (from wood shrinkage during drying).

**4. Smell.** Wet wood **smells stronger** of resin (pine), tannins (oak). Dry wood smells **weaker**, more "neutral".

**5. Weight in the hand.** After **two weeks of work** you'll tell wet from dry **without looking**: by weight alone. This is the most reliable method.

::: tip
First week of work: **lift each board slowly**. Pay attention to the **weight in your arms**. After one day you'll notice that some stacks "**pull down harder**" than others. Those are the wet lumber stacks. After two weeks the **weight tells you** whether you have fresh pine or dried pine in front of you, **without looking at the colour**.
:::

### Wet lumber stack: stickers are the key

Wood **must dry evenly**. If you stack fresh lumber **tight without gaps**, the bottom boards will stay wet forever and the top ones will dry first. Result: **the bottom ones mould**, **the top ones warp**, **the whole stack is a loss**.

That's why wet lumber is stacked with **stickers** (Polish: **przekładki**, in Polish jargon also "**listewki**"). A sticker is **a small wooden lath** with a cross-section of about **2×4 cm** or **3×5 cm**, laid **perpendicularly** between layers of boards.

**Construction of a wet lumber stack:**

1. **Bottom supports**: beam, concrete, pallets. The stack **doesn't stand on the ground** (water from the soil would destroy the bottom boards).
2. **First layer of boards**: laid evenly, edge to edge (but without overlap).
3. **Stickers**: **every 60–80 cm** along the length of the stack, **perpendicular** to the boards. Standard: **5–7 stickers** for a 4-metre board.
4. **Second layer of boards**: onto the stickers.
5. **Repeat**: boards → stickers → boards → stickers…
6. **Roof** on top of the stack, to **protect from rain**.

**Why stickers work:**

- They create **air channels** between layers of boards.
- **Wind** blows through the stack. Moisture **evaporates evenly** from all sides.
- Wood dries **gradually**, without **internal tension** → fewer warps and splits.

::: example
In a typical sawmill, a wet lumber stack measures **2 m × 1 m × 1.5 m tall** (about 30–50 boards). Stickers are **6–8 laths per layer**, **15–25 layers**. That means **one stack contains 100–200 stickers**. The helper who builds a stack **must have them prepared in advance**: they lie next to the stack, sorted.
:::

### Dry lumber stack: without stickers, but with other rules

Dry wood **no longer needs** ventilation. On the contrary, you want to **protect it from moisture** (rain, soil moisture). So dry lumber is:

- Stacked **tight**, board on board, without stickers.
- In a closed warehouse or under a **good roof**.
- **Off the ground** (pallets, support beams).

But careful, **dry lumber is more brittle**. If you drop a wet board from 1 m, the worst that happens is it **bends**. If you drop a dry one, **it can crack**.

::: warning
**During transport of dry lumber** be **doubly** careful with impacts. A dry board hitting a hard edge (forklift, concrete) **cracks at the end by 10–20 cm**. That cracked piece is **a loss**: the board drops from class I to class III. A helper who **regularly damages** dry boards during transport **gets a comment from the foreman after two weeks**.
:::

### Wood "works" all its life

Even **after drying**, wood **still reacts** to its environment:

- **Humid air** (rain, fog, autumn), wood **absorbs moisture**, **expands**.
- **Dry air** (frost, a stove at home), wood **loses moisture**, **shrinks**.

These movements are **small** (1–3% of dimension), but **real**. Hence:

- **Wood is slightly larger in summer** than in winter.
- **Doors sometimes stick** on a rainy day.
- **Stacks "creak"** with temperature change, the wood is "working".

In a sawmill this means that **stacks shift** at night, **especially in frost**. Recall the rule from lesson 7: **in the morning check the stack from 5 metres away** before walking up.

## Practice link

In practice you'll see **three kinds of stacks** in a sawmill:

1. **Stacks of wet, freshly cut lumber**: with **many stickers**, outside under roofs. This is "**air drying**", takes months.
2. **Stacks in the kiln**: in a closed room with controlled temperature and humidity (pine 35–70°C, oak and beech 30–60°C, about 2 weeks).
3. **Stacks of dry lumber**: in a covered warehouse, tight, ready for sale.

Your task in the first week: **walk** with the foreman around the yard and **recognize** which stack is which. The foreman will test you: "**What kind of stack is that?**", answer: "**fresh, still drying**" or "**dry, ready**". After two weeks **looking from 10 m** you know whether what's in front of you is **fresh pine** (lighter, stickers visible) or **dried pine** (matt, packed tight).

After a month the foreman will give you a **real task**: building a wet lumber stack together with a colleague. Then **you'll bring the stickers yourself** and **count** how many you need. That means **you've learned the material**.

## Trainer notes

**Pedagogical aim:** **to close Week 2**, linking the previous topics (anatomy → identification → terminology → defects → tools → machines → **state of the material**) into **a single thread of thought**: wood is a **living material** that **changes over time** (wet → dry), and your work as a helper **must adapt** to that state.

**120-minute rhythm:**
- 10 min, short recap of L7 (support machines, forklifts).
- 25 min, group reading of the content with a tutor, with special emphasis on the **weight difference** and **stack construction with stickers**.
- 25 min, **the weight exercise**: two identical boards (one wet, one dry, can be arranged from the plant warehouse). Each participant lifts both and **says out loud** "which is wet, which is dry". A strong "aha" moment.
- 15 min, "Key terms" section.
- 25 min, "Check yourself" + **summary of the whole Week 2** (a quick run-through of the 8 lessons: anatomy → species → terminology → defects → tools → saws → support machines → drying). Show the **narrative arc**: "from the tree in the forest to finished lumber in the stack".
- 20 min, buffer for questions.

**Helpful materials:**
- **Two identical boards** (one wet, one dry), essential for the weight exercise.
- **Stickers (5–10 pieces)**: to show what they look like and what size they are.
- Photos of stacks (a wet one with stickers, a dry one without), from the internet or from your own plant.
- **A table of lifting limits** (Regulation 2017), printed, brought in, discussed.

**A common trainee mistake: underestimating the weight limits** ("I'm strong"). Explain: limits are **the law** (Regulation of the Minister of Labour 2017), not a suggestion. A worker who **regularly lifts above the limit** has an **increased risk** of a permanent back injury after **3–5 years**. Disability at age 35. The PIP statistics are **brutal**.

**Trainee questions:**
- "How long does a board take to air-dry?", Depends on thickness, species, weather. Pine 4 cm, **3–6 months**. Oak 4 cm, **6–12 months**. Oak plank 10 cm, **2–3 years**. That's why **kilns** shorten this to **2 weeks**.
- "Can a wet board rot in the stack?", Yes. **Without stickers**: certain. **With stickers and a roof**: minimal risk. That's why stack construction is so important.

**Practical recommendation:** plan **a tour of the kiln** (if the plant has one). Doors open, you can feel the warmth, you can see the boards inside. This is **a strong sensory impression**: the trainee understands **what is happening** during drying.
