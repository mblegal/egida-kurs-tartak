## Introduction

In l1 you saw the **whole day** at the sawmill. In l2 you saw **your workstation**. In l3 we learn **something in between**: **how to communicate with people** when you cannot speak.

Because the sawmill is a **loud place**. When the band saw runs, noise at the workstation is **95–105 dB** (the level of an aircraft taking off at 100 m). When a rip-cut circular saw runs, it is **100–110 dB**. A planer is **95–100 dB**. A dust extractor is **85 dB**. When everything runs together, the hall is **louder than a boxing match ringside**.

At that noise level, **words are inaudible**. You can shout and no one will hear you. You have hearing protection in; you will hear the others even less. And the work requires **synchronization**: the operator has to tell you "pass", "wait", "stop", "step aside, kickback". How?

**Through gestures.**

A sawmill (like any loud factory) has a **standard gesture set** that every worker knows. Some are codified under **ISO 7010** (the international standard for safety signs) or under the **Polish sawmill standard** (a tradition that grew at Polish wood-industry plants from the 1960s). Others are **local**: every crew has a few of its own, exchanged at the briefing on the first day.

In this lesson you will learn **five basic gestures** you **MUST** know right after you enter the hall: **STOP, PASS, WAIT, STEP ASIDE, STEP BACK**. Plus a few extra ones (attention, acknowledged, I need help) that will be useful.

But gestures are only half of it. The other half is **eye contact**. Without eye contact, gestures are useless: you can wave your arms like a windmill, but if the operator looks at the saw, **he will see nothing**. So we will also learn **how to establish eye contact in a loud hall** before you release any gesture.

## Objectives

After this lesson:

- You understand that in a sawmill **words are inaudible while the machines run**, and so **gestures are the primary communication language**.
- You know the **five basic gestures**: STOP, PASS, WAIT, STEP ASIDE, STEP BACK. You can perform them and recognize them in others.
- You know that **eye contact** is the precondition of every gesture-based message: without it the gesture is useless.
- You know three **extra gestures** (ATTENTION, OK/UNDERSTOOD, I NEED HELP) and a few **local ones** that the crew will introduce.
- You know how to react when the operator gives an **urgent** gesture (immediate STOP, DANGER ATTENTION): what you do without thinking.
- You understand that **a whistle with the foreman** complements gestures: it **draws attention** at distance before you perform the actual gesture.

## Content

### Why gestures rather than words

Walk into the sawmill hall during production. The band saw puts out 100 dB. The dust extractor adds another 85 dB. A forklift runs down the aisle: another 90 dB at the horn. Pieces of wood hit a metal return conveyor: 95 dB. In total: **ambient sound at 100–105 dB**.

What does that mean? **Conversation does not work.** Two people 2 metres apart have to shout, and they still do not hear each other clearly. On top of that you wear **hearing protection** (mandatory above 85 dB under the Polish Minister of Labour and Social Policy Ordinance of 2007), which **further dampens** the surroundings by 20–30 dB. Your voice reaches the operator's ear **distorted, unclear**.

Second thing: **the operator looks at the saw**. Not at you. If you speak, he will not even **notice** that you are speaking, because his eyes and attention are 100% on the blade / disc.

That is why all of wood industry (and every loud industry: metallurgy, mining, aviation on the apron) has developed a **gesture system**. A gesture is **visible from 10 m away**. A gesture is **unambiguous**. A gesture **does not require hearing**. And, most importantly, **in case of sudden danger** the STOP gesture is understood instantly by anyone who can see.

::: info
**Sawmill noise is a serious matter on its own.** Working above 85 dB without hearing protection for 8 hours a day over 5 years leads to **permanent hearing loss** (PIP, 2019 report: 37% of wood-industry workers have damaged hearing after 15 years). Therefore (1) always wear hearing protection, (2) communicate with gestures, not shouts (shouting adds stress and strains your throat; it does not help). This is not "just noise": it is an **occupational factor** on par with dust and vibration.
:::

### Eye contact: the precondition

Before you release any gesture, **you must have eye contact with the person the gesture is for**. Otherwise your gesture is **useless**.

How do you establish eye contact with an operator who is looking at the machine?

**Method 1: stand in his field of view**. If the operator looks forward (at the saw blade), stand **2 metres in front of him, to one side**, so he catches you out of the corner of his eye. Do not enter his working zone: **to the side**, **behind the safety line**. Operators are trained to glance every few seconds at the area around the machine, looking for the "shadow" of the helper. He will see you.

**Method 2: a wave in the peripheral field**. If you cannot enter his field of view, wave **clearly** (one long wave of the hand upward, **not** short shaking). Motion in the operator's peripheral vision grabs his attention.

**Method 3: touch (only from the SIDE or from BEHIND, never from the front at a running machine)**. If you stand next to the operator at a running machine, do **not** touch him from the front or on the arm. A startled operator may make an uncontrolled move and put a hand into the rotating blade. Touch **only from behind, on the shoulder blade**, unambiguously. That is the "see me" sign.

**Method 4: a whistle**. Some crews issue a **short whistle** to the foreman / operators / helpers. One short whistle = "look at me". Two short whistles = "alarm, stop". You will be given a whistle at the first briefing if your sawmill uses them.

::: warning
**Never touch the operator from the front or on the arm while his hands are at the machine.** The operator may turn and **put a hand into the blade**. This rule is in the Minister of Economy Ordinance of 2008 and in every sawmill safety training. Communication is done by standing in the field of view **behind the safety line**, not by direct touch.
:::

**Once eye contact is made**, you release the gesture. The operator understands, nods (that is the "acknowledged/confirmed" gesture, see below), and you know the message got through.

### The five basic gestures

Below is the set you **MUST** know immediately. Learn it **in pairs** with a colleague: one performs, the other names; rotate. After ten minutes it should be in your head.

**Gesture 1: STOP – "stop now, immediately"**

- **Shape**: arm raised above the head, open palm, fingers extended, **facing the person you are signalling**. Like a traffic officer at a junction.
- **Meaning**: "stop immediately what you are doing". At the saw operator: the operator stops the blade (hits the operator's stop) and waits for an explanation.
- **When you use it**: you see a danger (something is falling, someone steps into the zone, material is mis-stacked and can jam, kickback is brewing). Any time you think "oh no, something is wrong".
- **Variants**: both arms above the head, STOP crossed = "general stop, breakdown" (that is already an alarm gesture, used by the foreman or the operator in serious danger).

**Gesture 2: PASS (HAND OVER) – "give me the material"**

- **Shape**: arm extended toward you (the helper), open palm, fingers up, then **pull the arm to self** twice. Like "come here".
- **Meaning**: the operator is ready for the next piece of material, hand it over.
- **When you react**: immediately. You pick up the next board / log / piece as agreed and hand it to the operator **from the safe side** (behind the working-zone line, as taught in W3 l5).
- **Note**: the PASS gesture is **not** a command to a sudden move. If a log is sliding off the stack or your grip is uncertain, **wait 2 seconds**, secure the grip, only then move. The operator will wait. Better a delay than an accident.

**Gesture 3: WAIT (STAND BY) – "do not pass, wait"**

- **Shape**: arm extended forward, open palm facing up, **toward you**. Like "stop there" but without raising above the head. May be completed by a light "push" of the hand.
- **Meaning**: the operator needs a moment, do not pass yet.
- **When you react**: you stand calmly with the material in your hands, watching the operator. When he shows PASS, you pass. Do not comment, do not ask "why", do not put the material down (if you hold a board, hold it: the operator will call you in a moment).
- **Note**: WAIT may last 5 seconds, 30 seconds, **2 minutes**. The operator decides. You wait.

**Gesture 4: STEP ASIDE (MOVE OUT, DO NOT STAND THERE) – "leave the zone"**

- **Shape**: arm extended **to the side away from the operator** (often with a repeated "pushing" motion), open palm, showing the direction you are to move. May be completed with a pointed finger **to where** ("there").
- **Meaning**: "leave where you stand, move 2–3 metres in the direction I am showing".
- **When you react**: **immediately**, without thinking. The operator sees something you do not: a log about to split, a tensioning blade, a forklift behind you. **First you move, then you ask "what was it?"**.
- **Note**: STEP ASIDE is an **urgent signal**. If you receive it, you react within **1–2 seconds**. Any delay is potentially fatal (literally: this is the gesture that saves from kickback, from a falling log, from the forklift).

**Gesture 5: STEP BACK (BACK UP A BIT) – "take 2–3 steps backward"**

- **Shape**: arm extended toward you, fingers bent up, then **fingers waving toward self** (that "curling" motion toward the chest, as if beckoning someone, plus a "backward" hint). At some sawmills: **open palm** and a wave **up and back**.
- **Meaning**: "step back 2–3 steps, you are too close to the machine or too close to a danger zone, but you do not need to move aside completely".
- **When you react**: you step back 2–3 paces while still watching the operator. You do not turn around (because behind you there may be another danger zone or someone with a forklift). **Step by step backward, with eye contact.**

::: tip
**How to remember the five gestures**: put them in a **sequence**. Production runs. (1) **STOP**: break it. (2) **PASS**: move forward. (3) **WAIT**: hold. (4) **STEP ASIDE**: flee immediately. (5) **STEP BACK**: retreat a bit. In this order, from the most stopping (STOP) through continuation (PASS/WAIT) to safety movement (STEP ASIDE/STEP BACK). **You will learn it in 10 minutes.**
:::

### Extra gestures: ATTENTION, OK, I NEED HELP

On top of the five basics, a few helpers:

**ATTENTION (warning)**: index finger raised, circling in the air or pointing in the direction of danger. After sending this gesture, you scan with your eyes in the direction of the finger. The operator is showing you "look there": "look, a forklift is coming", "the stack is splitting there", "a board is flying". **Reaction**: move at once to a safe side or give way.

**OK / UNDERSTOOD**: thumb up (the classic "OK") or a head nod. After the operator sends you a gesture, you **always** reply with this, to signal "got it, I know, doing it". Without your confirmation, the operator does not know the two of you are aligned.

**I NEED HELP**: both arms extended forward, open palms, together as if holding something, then you spread the palms to the sides with a slight "shake". Meaning: "I cannot handle this alone, call someone over". You use it, for instance: (1) a log is jammed and you cannot move it on your own, (2) you notice equipment is missing, (3) you feel unsure in a task and want someone to advise you. **No shame in it**: it is a professional signal.

### Local crew gestures

Every sawmill has a few **of its own gestures**, exchanged at the first-day briefing:

- **FASTER**: finger on the wrist (pointing at the watch) plus a finger circling forward. "Pick up the pace."
- **SLOWER**: index finger vertical, then a slow lowering of the arm. "Not so fast."
- **KICK (IT INTO PLACE)**: foot striking an invisible object plus a direction. "Kick that wedge/board the way I am showing."
- **ONE WHISTLE**: "notice me".
- **TWO WHISTLES**: "general STOP / alarm / evacuation".

Do not learn these in advance. The foreman will show you on the first day. If you forget, you ask a colleague during the break: "what does it mean when Maciej wiggles his finger like that?". It is never shameful to ask.

::: example
**From real life**: a helper from Georgia, a sawmill near Lublin, day two. The circular-saw operator gives him a STEP ASIDE with a repeated motion to the left. The Georgian does not understand: **in Georgian culture, a waving hand to the side sometimes means "everything is fine"**. He stays put. The operator shouts (through the ear protection the Georgian does not hear), then **hits the emergency stop himself**. It turned out the saw had a loose disc, and had it started in the next second, the disc could have torn off and struck the Georgian. **Lesson**: gestures are not universal across cultures. If you see something and do not understand, react cautiously (always step aside, ask later). Over-reaction is better than an ignored gesture.
:::

### Urgent gestures: a 2-second decision

Out of all the gestures, **two are urgent**:

1. **STOP** (arm above head, open palm) = something is wrong, stop.
2. **STEP ASIDE** (direction + pushing motion) = get out of here now.

**Rule**: when you see an urgent gesture, you **react within 1–2 seconds**. No "why" questions. No attempts to comment. No "wait, let me just set this down".

The operator does not give an urgent gesture without reason. **If he gives one, it means he sees something you do not.** Your safety depends on whether you **trust the operator** and **react at once**.

**After reacting**, once you are safe (you moved aside, you stopped moving), you may ask with the I NEED HELP gesture or with words (if the machine is stopped): "what was it?". The operator explains. That is when you learn: "ah, the stack was starting to lean; that is why he told me to step aside".

This is the **2-second decision** rule. A migrant who "hesitates" or "wants to finish the task before stepping aside" is **five times more likely** to have an accident than a migrant who reacts automatically. PIP 2017–2022 data: in 23 serious accidents involving helpers, in **19 cases** the helper did not react to the operator's warning gesture within 3 seconds.

### How to learn gestures in the first week

**Day 1**: the foreman shows you five basics plus three local ones. You repeat each gesture twice. **Ask if anything is unclear.** Do not pretend to understand when you do not: this is an investment in your life.

**Days 2–5**: during breaks you practise with a helper colleague. One performs, the other names. Rotate. Five minutes a day. **After four days**, it is in your head.

**Week 2**: the gestures are already automatic. You pay attention to **local gestures**: maybe someone does something the foreman did not show on day one. You ask during the break.

**Month 1**: you start to **use** gestures yourself, not just react to them. You send OK/UNDERSTOOD after every PASS. You send STOP if you see something worrying. You become a **member of the communication**, not just a receiver.

## Summary

Today you learned that in the sawmill **words are inaudible** when machines run (95–110 dB), and therefore **the primary communication language is gestures**.

**Precondition**: **eye contact**. Without eye contact a gesture is useless. Ways to establish it: stand in the field of view, wave in the peripheral field, touch from behind (never from the front at a machine), whistle.

**The five basic gestures**:

1. **STOP** – arm above head, open palm. Reaction: stop everything.
2. **PASS** – arm pulling toward self. Reaction: hand the next piece of material.
3. **WAIT** – arm forward, palm to you. Reaction: stand calmly with the material.
4. **STEP ASIDE** – arm pointing sideways + "push" motion. Reaction: **immediately** 2–3 metres aside.
5. **STEP BACK** – fingers waving to self + backward. Reaction: 2–3 steps back with eye contact.

**Extra gestures**: ATTENTION (vertical finger), OK/UNDERSTOOD (thumb up; you **always** confirm), I NEED HELP (spread palms).

**Urgent gestures** (STOP, STEP ASIDE) = **2-second decision**. You react at once, ask afterwards. PIP statistic: 19/23 serious helper accidents = no reaction within 3 seconds.

**Local gestures**: the foreman shows them on the first day. If you forget, ask at break. Never pretend to understand when you do not.

## Notes for the Trainer

**Pedagogical aim**: build an **automatic reaction** in the learner to the five basic gestures plus the ability to **perform** them. The lesson is mostly **practical**: 70% of the time is paired practice, 30% theory. A room with 5×5 m of space is ideal (so pairs can have distance).

**120-minute rhythm**:

- **0–15 min**: opening. Show a video clip (if you have one) from a loud sawmill hall: half a minute with captions "noise level 100 dB". Question: "how do you communicate in this noise?". Introduction: with gestures. A short history (ISO 7010, Polish sawmill tradition).
- **15–30 min**: eye contact. **Paired exercise**: one learner is the "operator" and looks at the wall (simulating focus on the machine); the other must establish eye contact. Four methods (standing in the field of view / peripheral wave / touch from behind / whistle). Swap after each method. **Conclusion**: gesture without eye contact = 0.
- **30–70 min**: **five basic gestures**. The trainer shows the first (STOP), learners repeat. Paired rotation: one performs, the other names; one names, the other performs. **Each gesture × 5 repetitions × 2 directions**. After the five: a test – the trainer shows a random gesture, learners name it in chorus (whoever is wrong, we shout "attention!" as in a game). Lively, loud, memorable.
- **70–85 min**: **extra gestures** (ATTENTION, OK, I NEED HELP). Paired exercise: the "operator" gives a three-gesture sequence, the "helper" reacts in turn. E.g. PASS → (helper simulates the handover + sends OK) → WAIT → STOP. We teach the **rhythm** of communication.
- **85–105 min**: **urgent gestures**. The trainer suddenly gives a STOP or STEP ASIDE; learners must react in 2 seconds (physically move aside, or freeze). Time it. Repeat ten times. **Goal**: automatic, not deliberate reaction.
- **105–120 min**: the Georgian case (from the lesson): discussion "why gestures are not universal", how to ask the foreman on day one, conclusions. Key terms. Preview of l4 (Polish words).

**Common mistake**: learners perform gestures **unclearly** (small amplitude, unfinished motion). From 10 m away such a gesture is invisible. **Correction**: "**clearly, like on stage**". Arm up = **straight**, not bent at the elbow. Open palm = **all fingers visible**, not clenched. The trainer should correct form at least twice before a learner is "passed".

**Second common mistake**: the learner **forgets to send OK/UNDERSTOOD** after receiving a gesture. The operator gives PASS; the helper starts handing over but **does not confirm**. The operator does not know if he was seen, looks around in uncertainty, the rhythm of work breaks. **Correction**: an automatic reflex – every received gesture → immediate OK (thumb, head nod).

**Practical recommendation**: hand out an A5 card with pictograms of eight gestures (five basics plus three extras). Name in Polish plus translation in the learner's language. Laminated, in the PPE pocket. **Use for two weeks.**

**Link to the next lesson**: l4 "Key Polish Terms and Commands". When machines stop (briefing, break, report), communication returns to words. Thirty Polish sawmill words that the learner must **understand by ear** (passive comprehension) and **pronounce** (active production). Commands like "uwaga" (attention), "odsuń się" (step aside), "zablokuj" (block it), "puść" (let go), "trzymaj" (hold) – the same meanings as the gestures but **in conditions of quiet**.
