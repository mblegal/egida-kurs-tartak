## Introduction

You finished the first week. You already know **why** a sawmill is different and **how** not to get killed in one. Starting today, for the next several days, we'll be talking about something else: the **material** you work with every day.

Notice one thing. Everything that happens in a sawmill, the saws, the transport, the stacking, the noise, the dust, all of it happens **for the wood**. Wood is the reason this plant exists. And wood is what you'll pick up today.

But **wood is not a uniform block**. What looks like an ordinary board has, on the inside, its own structure, its own zones, its own weak and strong spots. The same log you'll see in the yard in a moment contains **several different "kinds of wood"**: lighter and darker, soft and hard, good and worse.

In this lesson you'll learn to **look at a log and see structure** in it, not just a brown cylinder. Once you understand that structure, it will be easier to understand everything that comes later: **why some boards are more expensive than others**, **why wet wood weighs differently than dry wood**, **why the same board can have two colors**.

## Goals

After this lesson:

- You recognize **sapwood** (Polish: **biel**), **heartwood** (Polish: **twardziel**), the **pith** (Polish: **rdzeń**), the **annual rings** (Polish: **słoje roczne**), and the **bark** (Polish: **kora**) on a log cross-section.
- You understand why the same log has zones of **different quality** and **different uses**.
- You know that a helper who knows the structure of wood **understands the foreman's sorting instructions better**.
- You remember that **bark is waste**: but **controlled waste**, with its own place in the yard.

## Content

### What you see when you look at a log cross-section

Imagine taking a round disc that someone has cut off the end of a log. You look at it from above. You see a circle. But that circle is **not uniform**: it has several layers, one inside the next, like the layers of an onion. From the outside in:

1. **Bark** (Polish: **kora**), rough, dark brown or grey, sometimes scaly. The "skin" of the tree.
2. **Inner bark** (Polish: **łyko**), a thin layer under the bark, light, soft. Often comes off together with the bark.
3. **Sapwood** (Polish: **biel**), a wide ring of light wood, usually 2–8 cm thick.
4. **Heartwood** (Polish: **twardziel**), the darker central part, the main mass of the log.
5. **Pith** (Polish: **rdzeń**), the very middle, a small spot (sometimes dark, sometimes light), a remnant of the young shoot the tree once was.

Through all the sapwood and heartwood layers run the **annual rings** (Polish: **słoje roczne**), circular lines, one next to the other, each one representing a single year of the tree's life.

::: info
The tree from which a typical Polish pine log is made **is 80–120 years old**. An oak tree, often **150–200 years old**. That means the board you're holding in your hand was growing **before your grandparents were born**. So wasting wood isn't only a money loss, it's the loss of many decades.
:::

### Sapwood and heartwood: why the same log has two "kinds of wood"

This is the most important difference you have to remember. Looking at a fresh cross-section of pine, oak, or larch, **you'll see two colors right away**:

**Sapwood** (Polish: **biel**), the lighter ring near the outside of the log. This is "young" wood, through which **the tree still moves sap**. Sapwood is **soft**, **moist** (more water inside), **less durable**, and **more easily damaged** by fungi and insects.

**Heartwood** (Polish: **twardziel**), the darker centre. This is "old" wood, no longer carrying sap. The tree has enriched this part with resins and natural preservative substances. That's why heartwood is **harder**, **drier**, **more durable**, and **more resistant** to pests.

In Polish sawmills, three species have **the most visible** difference:

- **Pine** (Polish: **sosna**): light yellow sapwood, reddish-brown heartwood.
- **Oak** (Polish: **dąb**): cream-coloured sapwood, dark brown heartwood, sometimes almost black in the centre of old trees.
- **Larch** (Polish: **modrzew**): light sapwood, intensely red heartwood.

In other species (spruce, fir, birch, beech) the difference is **almost invisible**: the whole wood looks uniform. That doesn't mean there is no sapwood and heartwood, they are simply the same colour.

::: example
In a Polish sawmill yard you'll often see two stacks of oak boards. One, only dark boards, **with no white edges**. The other, boards with a clearly **lighter strip on the edge**. The first stack is oak "**from the heartwood only**", more expensive, going to furniture and floors. The second is oak "**with sapwood**", cheaper, going to construction and pallets. The foreman doesn't explain this every time, after a year on the job he expects a helper to **see the difference himself** and put boards on the right stack.
:::

### Annual rings: the tree's history written in the wood

Every year of a tree's life leaves **one ring**: the circular bands you see on the cross-section.

In our climate the tree grows differently in spring and summer than in autumn. So each ring has **two parts**:

- **Earlywood** (spring wood), light, wide, soft. The tree was growing fast, using the spring rains.
- **Latewood** (summer-autumn wood), darker, narrow, harder. The tree was growing slowly, finishing the season.

Together these two bands form **one year**. You can **count the rings** and tell how old the tree was when it was felled.

The rings **are not all the same width**. A tree that grew in good conditions (warmth, rain, plenty of light) has **wide rings**. A tree from a difficult, dry site has **narrow, dense rings**. And here is the paradox you have to remember:

**The narrower the rings, the harder and the better the wood.**

Pine with narrow rings (from mountain regions of Poland) is significantly harder and more expensive than pine with wide rings (which grew faster but more "loosely"). This matters for construction, for furniture, even for musical instruments.

::: tip
When you help the foreman sort boards, look **closely** at the rings. If you count **more than 6 rings per centimetre of board width**: that's "**dense**" wood of a high class. If the rings are so wide that only 2–3 fit in a centimetre, it's a lower class. The foreman will notice that you see this and will start giving you harder tasks.
:::

### Bark: waste, but not always

**Bark** (Polish: **kora**) is the first thing **taken off the wood in a sawmill**. A machine called a **debarker** (Polish: **korowarka**) does this. After **debarking** (stripping the bark) the log only then goes under the saw.

Why does the bark have to be taken off?

- **Bark destroys saw blades**: bark often contains pebbles, sand, grit. They hit the blade and dull it.
- **Bark is a habitat for insects and fungi**: if you leave bark on a board, the larvae will spread through the wood.
- **Bark holds moisture**: it makes lumber drying harder.

But bark **doesn't go to the rubbish bin**. In a modern sawmill bark is **fuel**:

- Dry bark goes to the plant's **boiler room**: it is burned to heat the **wood drying kilns**.
- Some bark goes to **gardening** (mulch under shrubs, mulching).
- Bark scraps may go to **briquettes** or **pellet**.

For a helper this means one thing: **bark has its own place in the yard**. Never throw bark into the sawdust container or the metal one. The foreman asks, he asks because these are two different **waste fractions** that will go to different places.

::: warning
Bark **freshly taken off a log is wet and heavy**. A single old "shell" of oak bark can weigh 5–10 kg. Never try by yourself to throw a large piece of bark onto a heap, it is easy to slip on a wet piece or strain your back. **You call a second person** or use a cart. This applies to **any heavy waste** in the sawmill, not just bark.
:::

### Why does a helper need this knowledge?

A helper **doesn't sort** wood into quality classes himself. That's done by the foreman, the quality control technician, or the operator. But a helper:

- **Carries boards to the right stack**: when the foreman says "those with sapwood on the left stack, those without sapwood on the right", you have to **know what sapwood is**.
- **Recognizes whether a log is "from the pith side" or "from the sapwood side"**: this affects how to grip and pass it.
- **Sees that a board has "healthy" wood or "diseased" wood** (sapwood attacked by fungi), this matters for cleanup and reporting.
- **Understands why bark is separate**: and doesn't throw it where it shouldn't go.

In short: **knowing the structure of wood turns you from a "pair of hands" into a helper who thinks**. That's a difference which over time means a higher pay rate and better tasks.

   - A) Sapwood is darker and harder than heartwood.
   - B) Sapwood is lighter and softer; heartwood is darker and harder.
   - C) These are two different wood species, sapwood and heartwood come from different trees.

   - A) How old the tree was and whether it grew fast or slowly.

   → **B**

## Practice link

In the second week of the course **you'll go out into the yard with the foreman**. The foreman will take a fresh pine or oak log and show you "**what it looks like inside**". He may use a short hand saw to cut off a thin disc from the end of the log. Together **you'll count the rings** and find where the sapwood ends and the heartwood begins.

Your task: at the end of the exercise **point with your finger** at those three zones (bark, sapwood, heartwood) and name them **in Polish**. The foreman doesn't expect perfect pronunciation, he expects you to **understand** what you're pointing at.

The next day, when you help shift boards in the yard, you'll see the same two colours, sapwood and heartwood, already on **finished lumber**. That's when you'll understand why boards are sorted onto different stacks.

## Trainer notes

**Pedagogical aim:** to build the **first layer of professional looking** at wood. For most trainees wood has so far been simply "brown". After this lesson they should be able to point at a fresh cross-section and name 3 zones (bark / sapwood / heartwood) **in Polish**. This is **perceptual knowledge**, not encyclopaedic, it is checked with the eyes, not the memory.

**120-minute rhythm:**
- 10 min, short recap of W1: "We talked about safety. Today we begin to talk about **what** we're working with. Without this you won't understand the rest of the course."
- 35 min, group reading of the lesson with a tutor (you can divide it: one student reads a section, the group comments).
- 25 min, **visual experiment** (if available): a fresh disc of pine and a disc of oak. Each student steps up, touches it, looks at it. Points at sapwood, heartwood, bark. Counts the rings out loud.
- 15 min, "Key terms" section, saying the Polish names out loud, each student in their own order.
- 20 min, "Check yourself" + a discussion of why this matters for a helper (closing section of the content).
- 15 min, buffer, questions, summary.

**Helpful materials, if available:**
- A pine cross-section disc and an oak cross-section disc (a **fresh** cross-section, not dried, the colours are sharper). You can also bring an old board with visible sapwood at the edge.
- A magnifying glass (×4), to show the rings up close.
- Photos from the PIP PDF "Manual woodworking" (pages 4–8, typical schemes of tree structure).

**A common trainee mistake:** confusing **sapwood (biel)** (a part of the log) with **bleaching** (the process of removing colour from wood). Explain that these are two different concepts, in our course we only use "biel = the lighter outer part of the log".

**A common doubt:** "And spruce and fir, do they have sapwood and heartwood?" Yes, they do, but **of almost the same colour**. That's why in those species a helper does not sort "by sight" into sapwood/heartwood, that's done by a specialist or by a moisture measurement.

**Practical recommendation:** if at all possible, hold this lesson **in the yard, not in the classroom**. 30 minutes with a fresh log gives more than 60 minutes with a slide deck. You can return to the classroom for the "Key terms" and "Check yourself" sections.

Tomorrow we'll learn to recognize the **species** of tree, that is, whether it is pine, spruce, oak, beech, or birch. Because each of these species has its own place in the sawmill and its own use."
