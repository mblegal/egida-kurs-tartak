### 10:02, measuring and cutting the first blade

The blade length for an LT70 sawmill follows from **the circumference of the two tensioning wheels**. For an LT70 (wheels 483 mm diameter each, axles 1610 mm apart) the blade circumference is about **5 m 380 mm** (for a working tension of 2200 PSI). Wood-Mizer norm: **5.40 m +/-10 mm** (a centimetre either way is acceptable). A shorter blade will not seat on the wheels at full tension, a longer one will not reach 2200 PSI tension (the tensioner carriage has a limited stroke).

Rustam takes the **alloy 38/7-8 rake 10 degrees** (the first, today we are making typical blades for pine) from the roll. He measures out on the table:

1. Hooks the beginning of the blade on the left holder of the table (a magnetic catch, the steel blade sticks to it).
2. Unwinds the roll to the right, by hand. The blade is wound in the roll under tension, it jumps out sharply, Rustam holds it in thick leather gloves (OHS, the blade has teeth, it cuts a finger if you are careless).
3. Measures with a 5-metre steel tape (two pieces, overlapping), marks the cut point with a white marker at **5.40 m**.
4. Plus an extra control length of **+20 mm** for cutting (because the circular saw takes 2-3 mm, plus a margin for end cleanliness).
5. Cut with the HSS circular saw: the blade lying on the table, saw in hand, cut perpendicular at 90 degrees (important so that the ends are parallel to each other after welding).

Time: 3 minutes measuring, 1 minute cutting. **Blade no. 1 ready**, 5.40 m +20 mm, lying on the table.

### 10:08, preparing the ends, degreasing

The ends of the blade are factory-sharp (teeth and edge), but not clean. Before welding you have to:

1. **Steel brush** (*szczotka stalowa*), fine strokes within about 3 cm of the end on both sides, removing any dust, corrosion, or residual oil from roll production. Goal: a metallically clean surface, **on which the weld is to be made**.
2. **Acetone on a cotton swab**, wiping the same 3 cm section. It removes grease that the brush did not pick up. Acetone evaporates in 20-30 seconds with no residue. **OHS note**: acetone in an open container only briefly, nitrile gloves (not latex, acetone dissolves latex), the hall has ventilation plus **ATEX Zone 22**, so an open flame (e.g. a cigarette) on the same shift would be forbidden before the bottle is closed.
3. **Drying for 30 seconds** in the air, so that acetone residues evaporate before the blade goes into the welder.

Rustam does this with methodical mindfulness. For many years he has learned that **the quality of a weld starts on the steel brush**. The sawmill in Romania where he worked before Poland had a rule: "the brush goes on as long as you see bare rough metallic surface with no matt film", and this rule came with Rustam.

- **Welding pressure indicator** (kg-force, typically 50-80)
- **Cycle start button** (a large red one in the middle of the panel)
- **Green LED** (ready, ready to weld)
- **Red LED** (error, interrupted cycle, transformer overheating)

Rustam sets:
- **Current: 9 kA** (middle of the 8-10 range for 38 x 1.07 alloy blades)
- **Time: 1.8 s** (Wood-Mizer standard for this thickness)
- **Pressure: 70 kg** (set by a mechanical lever, verified with the EGIDA spring scale, reading 68-72 kg, acceptable)

Positioning the ends of the blade in the jaws:
1. Left end: the blade slides into the left jaw with teeth down, longer to the rear, the end of the blade sticking out **0.5-1 mm past the edge of the jaw** (very important, too much overhang = an uneven weld, too little = incomplete fusion).
2. The left jaw clamping lever goes down, the blade is immobilised.
3. Right end: **parallel** to the left, teeth in the same orientation (because the blade after welding must be monotonically oriented, a mix of tooth directions = destruction on the first shift of cutting).
4. Check the contact of the ends: it should be **metal to metal with no gap, no overlap**. If there is a 0.1 mm gap, there will be a hole in the weld (it will tear on the first tension). If there is overlap, right over left, the weld will be thicker at that spot (it will need heavier grinding). Optimum: the ends touch plane to plane, visibly perpendicular.
5. The right jaw lever goes down.

Rustam checks visually. The contact is beautifully perpendicular, the gap is invisible. **OK**.

### 10:15, welding cycle

Rustam steps back from the welder's arm (OHS, the weld is very bright, DIN 5 or DIN 6 safety goggles are required, Rustam has DIN 5 on his forehead, lowered at the moment of start).

- **Goggles down** (green lenses, brightness filter).
- **Hand on the cycle start button.**
- **Press.**

Automatic cycle:
- **0.0 s**: start pressed. The transformer charges the capacitor.
- **0.3 s**: contactors closed, 9 kA current flows through the contact of the blade ends (contact resistance produces enormous heat at the point of contact, the metal melts locally).
- **0.3-2.1 s**: 1.8 second heating cycle at 70 kg pressure. The ends of the blade plastically fuse (not literally "poured metal", more "welded" = pressed together in a soft state).
- **2.1 s**: current off, pressure still held.
- **2.1-4.0 s**: 2 seconds of cooling under pressure (important, under pressure the metal solidifies evenly, without pores).
- **4.0 s**: pressure released, cycle complete, the green LED blinks.

Through his goggles Rustam sees a bright white flash (short, 1-2 s), the fused contact turns red, then dark red, then black metallic. The welder hums at the typical low 50 Hz tone from the welding current. Wahan, from the other side of the door, sees the flash through the shield and squints slightly (Wahan has put on safety goggles as well, OHS in the workshop for an observer behind a glass door is not required but EGIDA requires it for familiarisation with the procedure).

### 10:16, natural cooling

After the weld the blade stays in the jaws, **you must not take it out immediately**. The material in the weld is still hot (60-80 degrees C on the surface, up to 300 degrees C inside), rapid cooling (contact with a cold glove, dropping onto the concrete floor) **produces thermal stresses** and microcracks. The weld looks good outwardly, but will crack after 20 cuts.

EGIDA procedure: **wait 60-90 seconds with the blade in the jaws** (*chłodzenie naturalne*, natural cooling), let the temperature equalise with the surroundings. Then release the jaws carefully, pull the blade out to the next stage.

### 10:18, grinding the weld

After the welder the weld is not smooth. At the contact of the end surfaces a **"weld bead"** (*grat zgrzewu*) forms (slightly bulging material, typically 0.5-1.0 mm above the blade thickness), on both flat sides of the blade. If such a blade with a bead were mounted on a sawmill, **the bead would touch the guides** (BG-UHMW-70 blocks from l8 M3 T2) and quickly wear them out, and the bead itself would be blunted.

Rustam takes the **two-stage grinding stone** (*brusek dwustopniowy*):

**Stage 1, coarse P80 stone** (grinding stone of 80 grit, handheld, about 10 cm plate, 2-3 cm thick, held in the hand):
1. Place the blade on the table, spine up, weld under the stone.
2. Smooth back-and-forth motion (20-30 passes), grinding the bead off one side. Observe visually: the bead visible at first as a slight bulge, after grinding the surface looks flat with grinding marks (white streaks on the metal).
3. Turn the blade over, bead on the other side, the same.
4. Time: 2-3 minutes for each side.

**Stage 2, finishing P240 stone** (finer stone, half-smooth surface):
1. The same motion, now finer, 10-15 passes per side.
2. The goal is to **remove the P80 scratches** by replacing them with a smoother P240 surface (not invisible, but fine enough not to "catch" in the cut).
3. Turn over, the second side.
4. Time: 1-2 minutes each side.

**Stage 3, micrometer measurement**:
Rustam measures the weld (middle of the blade width, holding the micrometer perpendicular). Measurement 1: **1.08 mm**. Measurement 2, 2 mm away from the first: **1.07 mm**. Measurement 3: **1.06 mm**. Mean **1.07 mm**, which is the factory blade thickness. **OK**, the weld has been ground evenly.

Wahan asks through the door: *"Why are you measuring three times?"* Rustam: *"Because over the 5 cm of weld width the thickness can vary. If one side of the weld is 1.10 mm (too thick) and the other 1.05 mm (too thin), a 1.07 mean looks OK, but in reality I have an uneven weld that will shear off in cutting. I have to see all three points together, not just the mean."*

EGIDA tolerance for a weld (*tolerancja grubości zgrzewu*, weld thickness tolerance): **1.07 +/-0.03 mm** at each of the three points. For today's weld all three are within tolerance (1.06 to 1.08), **OK**.

### 10:26, visual and geometric tests

After grinding Rustam:

1. **Lays the blade flat on the table**, looks from a side perspective. The weld should be **invisible from a flat perspective** (that is, no "hump" or "dip" in the plane of the blade).
2. **Looks from above** at the weld, looking for microcracks (most often in the centre of the width, clear dark hairline lines). For a good weld: uniform matt metallic grey with fine P240 grinding streaks. For a bad one: visible radial or axial cracks. Today's weld: **uniform, OK**.
3. **Bends the weld lightly with his fingers** (the blade is elastic, you can bend it easily by 10-20 degrees in an arc). The weld in this test should bend **just like the rest of the blade**, no "cracking", no "clicking" (*test gnięcia*, bend test). Today's: bends smoothly, **OK**.
4. **Final geometric test**: the blade lying flat. The weld should form a straight line between the teeth on the left and right side. If the weld has introduced a **"bend"** in the tooth belt (that is, the teeth on the left are at one angle and on the right at another), the blade once mounted on the sawmill will "drift" sideways. Today's weld: teeth on both sides in a single straight line, visually **OK**.

If any of the tests is **not OK**, the weld goes to the bin (cut out and weld again) or **the blade goes to Wood-Mizer Polska** for diagnosis (if the operator does not know why the weld failed). EGIDA keeps statistics: in 2025 there were 47 welds, of which 4 were rejected by operators at this stage (8.5 percent rejection, acceptable).

## Content

### 1. The role of the M3 operator in blade welding

Welding of saw blades is a task that sits **on the border between the competence of the M3 operator and that of a specialist**. In many sawmills larger than EGIDA there is a dedicated "blade man" (employed full time, working in the blade workshop) who performs all welding and sharpening. In smaller sawmills (such as EGIDA, 12-15 production employees) the task is distributed:

- **Welding at EGIDA's own workshop**: 30-40 percent of blades (mainly emergency replenishment, single pieces). Performed by **M3 operators**.
- **Welding at Wood-Mizer Polska** in Kolonia Koszajec: 60-70 percent of blades (bulk lots, quarterly). Performed by the plant specialists.
- **Sharpening**: 100 percent at Wood-Mizer Polska (a sharpener with a diamond wheel, infrastructure not met by EGIDA).

The decision on "where to weld" belongs to the **foreman**. The M3 operator executes when told. But the M3 operator **understands the criteria** (batch size, blade type, urgency), because on a Saturday evening when a blade breaks and the foreman is not available, an autonomous operator has to assess for themselves: "weld now myself or wait until Monday for Wood-Mizer". Examples of decisions:

- **Monday morning, 8 h of production planned, broken blade at 7:50**: the M3 operator welds by themselves, 30 min of downtime, the plan holds.
- **Saturday evening 5:00 p.m. after shift, the store has 0 spare blades, Monday start at 6:00 a.m.**: the M3 operator stays 45 minutes longer, welds 2-3 blades, Monday's plan is saved.
- **Thursday, a production shift plan, the store has 2 spare blades, the client is not urgent**: the M3 operator does not weld by themselves, asks the foreman to schedule a shipment to Wood-Mizer (cheaper).

### 2. The Wood-Mizer BMS-250 welder, process technique

The BMS-250 is a **resistance welder** (*zgrzewarka oporowa*) with **clamping on both sides** and a **pulsed-current capacitor**. It is not an arc welder (then you would see an electric arc), it is not a laser welder (more expensive, only for the hardest blades). The resistance technique has been the **standard of the sawmill industry** since the 1980s, not fundamentally changed over decades.

**How the current works**:
1. The two ends of the blade are pressed in the jaws under mechanical pressure (60-80 kg).
2. Through the contact flows a huge current (8-10 kA, which is a thousand times more than in a 230 V 10 A socket).
3. **The contact has electrical resistance** (microscopic surface roughness), this resistance generates heat (Joule's law P = I squared times R). For a contact with 0.0001 ohm resistance and 9000 A current, the power generated at the contact is 9000 squared times 0.0001 = **8100 W = 8.1 kW** at a contact point of 1 mm squared. That is like 8 electric hobs on a poppy seed. The metal melts in milliseconds.
4. When the metal has softened (plastic, orange-yellow), **the mechanical pressure squeezes the ends** one into the other. The material merges directly.
5. Current off, pressure held for 2 seconds (cooling under pressure). The metal solidifies pore-free.
6. Pressure released. The weld is ready (raw, ready for grinding).

**Why pressure is critical**: without pressure the melting metal would flow out of the joint (gravity plus current force), the contact would break, the current would be interrupted, **the weld would be porous**. 70 kg of pressure mechanically pushes the ends together and keeps contact until solidification.

**Why time is critical**: too short (e.g. 1.0 s), the metal will not reach full plastic temperature, the weld is not full in cross section. Too long (e.g. 2.5 s), the metal overheats, crystalline grains grow large, the weld is brittle.

**Why current is critical**: Joule's law is quadratic. Going from 8 to 10 kA is not 25 percent more heat, it is **156 percent more** (10 squared / 8 squared = 1.56). A small drift of the parameter = a big change in the result. That is why the welder potentiometer has precise markings and the operator checks the setting **before every start**, does not rely on "like yesterday".

### 3. Critical geometric parameters that the operator cannot "push through"

There are three things that the operator **cannot regulate**, even if it would be tempting:

**A. Perpendicularity of the blade ends**. The ends must be cut at 90 degrees to the blade axis. If they are at 89 degrees (1 degree deviation, almost invisible to the naked eye), the weld at the joint has **an angle instead of a straight line**, stresses accumulate there, it will crack after 30-50 cuts. **Correction**: if the HSS circular saw cut crookedly, **cut it off and cut a new end at 90 degrees**, do not try to "straighten" with a file (it will not work).

**B. Parallelism of teeth on both sides of the weld**. If in the jaws you placed the ends so that the left teeth are "at 20 degrees" and the right teeth "at 22 degrees", the weld itself will be a straight line, but **the line of teeth** is kinked. A band sawmill with such a blade will cut in waves (the blade "drifts" sideways), the guides will wear quickly. **Correction**: a visual check in the jaws, if the teeth are not perfectly parallel, **readjust the position of one end** (release the jaw, set it correctly, clamp) before you start the cycle.

**C. Tooth direction** (*kierunek zębów*, tooth direction). Every blade has a **"running" direction**, the teeth are pointed one way. If you put one end of the blade in the jaws "teeth forward" and the other "teeth backward", after welding you have a blade with teeth in two opposite directions, **absolutely useless**. **Correction**: impossible, the blade to the bin, a second attempt with a new piece from the roll. Awareness of direction before placing in the jaws is key. EGIDA has a sticker on the welder: "Check TOOTH DIRECTION!" in three languages.

### 4. Weld life test, EGIDA statistics

A good weld lets the blade work through **a full life cycle**: 140-180 cuts before sharpening for alloy on pine, 180-250 for stellite on oak. After welding the blade goes to the Wood-Mizer Polska sharpening shop, from there it comes back sharpened, another 140-180 cuts, and another sharpening. A blade takes **3-5 sharpening cycles** (the teeth get gradually shorter, the steel becomes harder from post-sharpening hardening), then it **goes to scrap**.

A weak weld (badly made) shortens the blade's life. EGIDA 2025 statistics (47 local welds):

- **43 welds (91.5 percent) reached a full cycle** of 140+ cuts without cracking. Standard.
- **4 welds (8.5 percent) cracked before a full cycle**:
  - 2 cracked within 20-50 cuts (the weld clearly weak, thermal microcracks from bad cooling, the operator got feedback and corrected the procedure)
  - 1 cracked at 80 cuts (a borderline weld, hard to pinpoint the cause, Wood-Mizer diagnosis did not detect it)
  - 1 cracked at 130 cuts (acceptable, close to the expected life, this is not a "weak weld")

EGIDA acceptance threshold: **max 10 percent rejection in the first line** (the operator themselves rejects a badly made weld at the test stage). If an operator has a rejection statistic of 15 percent+ in a quarter, the foreman invites Rustam (or Damian) to Wood-Mizer Polska for a training day (EGIDA procedure from 2024).

For comparison, welds **from Wood-Mizer Polska** have a statistic of **more than 95 percent of full cycles** (about 5 percent cracks). EGIDA with its 91.5 percent is lower but acceptably so, the difference coming from experience and the plant's machine park (EGIDA's BMS-250 vs WM Polska's plant machines).

### 5. Blade replacement on the sawmill, repetition from l6 plus M3 context

Blade replacement (old to new) is a procedure known from l6 M3 T2 (inspection) and l7 (blunt blade). In l2 we repeat it in the context of **M3 autonomy**, where the operator replaces **without waiting for the foreman**, on the basis of their own decision.

Pointers for the replacement (from l6, here in short as a reminder):

1. **STOP, LOTO** (main switch plus padlock plus tag, from l4 M3 T1).
2. **Release tension** (tensioner lever to SLACK, pressure gauge to 0).
3. **Remove the cover** (2 bolts, 13 spanner).
4. **Remove the old blade** (by hand, leather gloves, careful with the teeth), hang on the used-blade rack.
5. **Clean the band wheels** (cloth, oil traces removed), **not acetone** (the wheels are rubber, acetone damages them), **only WD-40** or **naphtha** in small amounts.
6. **Mount the new blade** (from the store), teeth "forward" relative to the direction of rotation (arrow on the wheel).
7. **Tension** (tensioner lever UP, turn the knob to 2200-2400 PSI on the gauge, from l1 the choice depends on the species).
8. **Adjust the guides** (feeler gauge, 3-4 mm at four points, from l1).
9. **Cover** (back on, 2 bolts).
10. **Test** (LOTO off, switch ON, low revs, 10 seconds of observation, whether the blade runs smoothly, does not slip on the wheels, does not jump off).
11. **Entry in KKS-001** (the maintenance card from l7) in the "Inspections and replacements" section: date, time, operator, old blade no., new blade no., reason for replacement.

Replacement time for an experienced M3 operator: **20-25 minutes** (M2 operator with supervision: 30-35; M1 operator: does not replace by themselves).

### 6. Typical mistakes of M3 beginners in welding

A compilation from EGIDA foremen's reports and from conversations with Wood-Mizer Polska technicians:

**Mistake 1. Skipping acetone**. "The steel brush is enough, the blade looks clean". The weld has oil residues at the contact and cracks within 20-40 cuts. **Correct**: acetone is the second, non-optional step, not an alternative to the brush.

**Mistake 2. Running the welding cycle twice "just in case"**. The operator does one cycle, it seems "weak", presses start a second time. The first cycle had the optimal parameters, the second welds the already-welded material, **overprocessing**: the material overheats, grains grow, the weld is brittle. Cracks at 30-60 cuts. **Correct**: one cycle, one weld. If it seems "weak", **check the parameters** and do another one with a fresh piece, do not double-click start.

**Mistake 3. Cooling under a hall fan "for speed"**. The operator opens the jaws after 20 seconds, lays the blade in a draft, thinks "longer is saving time for me". Result: microcracks within the first 15 cuts, the weld in the bin. **Correct**: 60-90 seconds in the jaws, natural cooling, a patient blade.

**Mistake 4. Grinding only with P80**. "I see no difference between P80 and P240, P80 is enough". The weld is rough, it will bite into the BG-UHMW blocks within the first cuts and wear them fast. **Correct**: P80 gives the coarse shape, P240 gives a smooth surface that does not catch on the guides.

**Mistake 5. Micrometer measurement at one spot**. "1.07 mm, done". The weld is asymmetric (1.10 - 1.07 - 1.04), the mean is 1.07, but the sides are at the edges of the error band (+/-0.03 means 1.04-1.10). It shears on the first cut into a knot. **Correct**: **three measurements** (middle +/- 2 mm), all within 1.07 +/-0.03 individually.

**Mistake 6. No label on a new blade**. "I know I made it today". A week later the foreman finds 3 unlabelled blades in the store, does not know which is which. **Correct**: an A6 label always, type, no., date, operator, sharpener counter.

**Mistake 7. Welding rolls without distinction**. The operator takes a "stellite 7/9" roll thinking it is alloy 7/8. Welds a very hard blade with alloy parameters (9 kA, 1.8 s, 70 kg). Stellite requires **stronger parameters** (10 kA, 2.0 s, 75 kg) for full fusion, a weaker cycle gives a porous weld. **Correct**: **check the roll type** (label on the face of the roll), **verify in the Wood-Mizer instruction** the welding parameters for this type.

### 7. When the weld goes "at an angle", diagnostics

If after welding and grinding the operator sees that the weld is not straight, the teeth on both sides are not collinear, or the bend test has the weld "clicking" unclearly, **three-point diagnostics**:

**Point 1. Welder parameters**. Were the current, time, pressure within range? Are the potentiometers clean (a dirty one can give an unstable reading)? Is the transformer not overheating (red LED)? If any parameter is out of range, the first weld in this cycle to the bin, correct parameters, a new attempt.

**Point 2. End geometry**. Did the HSS circular saw have a sharp blade? A blade after 300+ cuts goes blunt, does not cut perpendicular, gives a "toothy" edge. If so, replace the saw blade (a spare in the workshop cabinet).

**Point 3. End cleanliness**. Is the steel brush not "ruined" (thrown into the dusty workshop, clogged with dust, no longer cleaning)? Is the acetone not stale (bottle open for 6 months, top-grade acetone siphoned off, leaves traces)? Replace the worn items.

**Point 4. Is the welder "on schedule"** (time since the last Wood-Mizer service). BMS-250 service is every **2500 welds or yearly**, which for EGIDA is every **about 4 years**. EGIDA's last service was **2023-05**, the next is planned for **2027-05**. If after 2500 welds since the service the welds start to fail atypically, the operator's diagnosis is "service must be called", not "I guessed the parameters".

If none of points 1-4 explains the problem, **escalate to the foreman**. The foreman will call Wood-Mizer Polska, the service technician will analyse over the phone or via phone photos, and will plan a service visit (one to two weeks of waiting).

::: warning
**Absolute prohibition**: trying to "force a weld by strength". An operator who, after three failed attempts on the same blade, raises the current to 12 kA (outside the 8-10 range) risks burning out the transformer (replacement cost 8,000-12,000 zł, 6 weeks of waiting), withdrawal of the BMS-250 for a full service. **The welder is a device with a precise range**, which is defined in view of: 1) blade thickness, 2) transformer condition, 3) OHS (current above 15 kA generates visible sparking, burn hazard). **If three attempts did not give a good-quality weld, stop, diagnose, escalate.**
:::

### 8. Entry in KDP-001 when welding during a shift

If during a production shift the operator has to weld a blade (an atypical situation, for example a blade breaks on an order, the store has no matching one, welding is needed), the entry in **KDP-001, section "Actual parameters applied"**:

```
Log X, time HH:MM: blade broke after N cuts,
visual diagnostic: [e.g. "weld seam split, weld made 2026-05-15"]
Action: replacement with spare blade [store no.],
     plus welding a new blade from roll [type] in the workshop,
     parameters: 9 kA / 1.8 s / 70 kg, cooling 80 s, grinding P80+P240, measurement 1.07/1.07/1.08, tests OK.
Downtime: 45 min.
KKS-001 entry: done.
```

This is **an evidentiary trail** for the foreman and the ISO auditor: the operator did not just "replace the blade", they also **welded a new one**, from specific parameters, with a measurement. In case of a customer complaint (a board from log X has a mark of non-uniform texture in that spot), the cause and time of the event can be traced in KDP-001.

### 9. Economics of local welding vs Wood-Mizer Polska

Why does EGIDA weld 30-40 percent of blades in its workshop, even though the quality at Wood-Mizer is about 4-5 percent higher? **Economics and urgency**.

**Local cost**: a raw alloy stock roll 38 x 1.07 x 45 m costs **about 890 zł net** (supplier Wood-Mizer Polska), gives 8 blades, so the raw-material cost per blade is **about 111 zł**. Plus welder power (about 3 zł/weld), plus 20 min of operator time (EGIDA labour rate 45 zł gross per hour, 15 zł for 20 min). **Total about 129 zł per blade**.

**Cost at Wood-Mizer Polska**: a ready welded blade, without sharpening, alloy 38 x 1.07 x 5.40 m: **145 zł net** (catalogue price 2026). Plus transport (a package of 10 blades is about 50 zł, so 5 zł per blade). **Total about 150 zł per blade**.

**Difference: 21 zł per blade in favour of local**. For 47 blades per year that is **a saving of about 1,000 zł/year**, not large, but **reaction time** matters more:

- **Failure on Friday evening, needed Monday morning**: Wood-Mizer ships on Monday after 10:00, delivery Tuesday. One day of shift downtime = loss of revenue **about 1,500 zł/day** (EGIDA averages 1,000-1,500 zł of margin per shift). Local welding saves that day.
- **Planned bulk order at the end of the quarter**: 10-20 blades at once, transport economics better, WM Polska quality higher. This goes to WM.

Conclusions: the M3 operator should understand **both paths**, know when it pays to weld, when to hand it over. The goal is not to weld everything yourself, nor to do nothing yourself.

### 10. OHS protocol of the blade workshop

The blade workshop has higher OHS requirements than the sawmill hall, because:

- **Welding current of 8-10 kA** (very dangerous on contact, although short-lived in the cycle)
- **Metal sparks** during welding (can hit an eye)
- **Acetone** (flammable, narcotic fumes)
- **Metallic dust** during grinding (can cause "iron dust lung", siderosis, in long exposures)

EGIDA requirements:

1. **DIN 5 or DIN 6 safety goggles** during the welding cycle (brightness filter, protecting against flash and sparks).
2. **Thicker leather gloves** than in the sawmill hall (the blade has teeth, the current is dangerous).
3. **Cotton overall** (cotton fire self-extinguishes, synthetics drip molten onto the skin).
4. **Forced ventilation** switched on during work with acetone (a wall fan 400 cubic metres per hour, switch by the door).
5. **Acetone cabinet locked** (key with the foreman, the M3 operator borrows it for 30 min).
6. **CO2 fire extinguisher** by the workshop door (CO2 is best at quenching acetone flames, not water, not foam).
7. **First-aid kit** with burn supplies (gel, hydrocolloid dressings) plus an eyewash (500 ml saline bottle).
8. **Working alone in the workshop? Tell someone.** EGIDA rule: the operator in the workshop informs the foreman by phone or radio, the foreman checks every 30 minutes (a glance through the window, the question "OK?"). For a 30-minute weld this check is once.
9. **After work**: clean the table, ventilation off, acetone in the cabinet, welder main switch OFF, workshop door locked.

Breaking any of these requirements is a matter of **Art. 211 pt. 2 of the Labour Code** (the employee uses personal protective equipment) plus **Art. 211 pt. 4** (uses machines in accordance with their intended use). At EGIDA a workshop breach means a conversation with the manager plus **suspension of the workshop authorisation for 3 months** (the operator continues to work as M3 on sawmills, but does not enter the blade workshop until reinstatement).

## Key Terms

**blade workshop** (*warsztat taśm*, EN *blade workshop*, ES *taller de cintas*, UK *майстерня стрічок*): a dedicated room in the sawmill (6 x 4 m at EGIDA), equipped with a resistance welder, a grinding stone, a micrometer, raw blade stock rolls, accessible only to M3 operators, the classifier and the foreman.

**resistance blade welder** (*zgrzewarka oporowa*, EN *resistance welder*, ES *soldadora por resistencia de cintas*, UK *контактний зварювальний апарат стрічок*): an electrical device (typically Wood-Mizer BMS-250, 400 V three-phase, 15 kVA) that joins two ends of a blade by a resistance current cycle of 8-10 kA / 1.5-2.0 s / 60-80 kg of pressure.

**raw blade stock roll** (*rolka taśmy surowej*, EN *raw blade stock roll*, ES *rollo de cinta en bruto*, UK *рулон стрічки в сирому вигляді*): a coil of steel blade 45 m long (LT70) in a paper cover, from which the operator measures and cuts a blade of 5.40 m for a specific sawmill.

**weld bead** (*grat zgrzewu*, EN *weld bead*, ES *cordón de soldadura*, UK *зварювальний валик*): the bulging material that forms at the joint of welded ends under pressure, typically 0.5-1.0 mm above blade thickness, removed with the grinding stone.

**two-stage grinding stone** (*brusek dwustopniowy*, EN *two-stage grinding stone*, ES *piedra de afilar de dos etapas*, UK *двоступеневий точильний камінь*): a hand tool with two grinding surfaces (coarse P80 plus finishing P240), used to grind the weld after it is taken out of the welder jaws.

**welding cycle** (*cykl zgrzewania*, EN *welding cycle*, ES *ciclo de soldadura*, UK *цикл зварювання*): the automatic welder sequence covering the closing of contactors, current flow, and cooling under pressure, typically 4.0 seconds from start to finish for 38 x 1.07 mm blades.

**natural cooling** (*chłodzenie naturalne*, EN *natural cooling*, ES *enfriamiento natural*, UK *природне охолодження*): a period of 60-90 seconds after the welding cycle, during which the blade remains in the jaws for pore-free solidification without thermal microcracks.

**weld thickness tolerance** (*tolerancja grubości zgrzewu*, EN *weld thickness tolerance*, ES *tolerancia de espesor de soldadura*, UK *допуск товщини зварного шва*): the permissible thickness range of the welded blade of +/-0.03 mm from the factory thickness (1.07 mm for LT70), measured at three points (middle and +/-2 mm).

**bend test** (*test gnięcia*, EN *bend test*, ES *prueba de flexión*, UK *тест на згинання*): a manual visual test in which the weld, bent with the fingers, should behave like the rest of the blade, without audible "clicking" or "cracking", confirming full microstructural coherence.

**tooth direction** (*kierunek zębów*, EN *tooth direction*, ES *dirección de los dientes*, UK *напрямок зубів*): the orientation of the blade teeth relative to the direction of motion on the sawmill, critical in welding (both ends must have teeth in the same orientation), marked with an arrow on the raw roll.

**BMS-250** (*Wood-Mizer BMS-250*, EN *Wood-Mizer BMS-250*, ES *Wood-Mizer BMS-250*, UK *Wood-Mizer BMS-250*): the Wood-Mizer welder model for sawmill blades, EGIDA standard, distributed by Wood-Mizer Polska, blade range 25-50 mm wide, 2026 price about 38,000 zł net.

**ATEX Zone 22** (*strefa ATEX 22*, EN *ATEX Zone 22*, ES *zona ATEX 22*, UK *зона ATEX 22*): a classification of a space with a combustible-dust explosion hazard (wood dust), required by Directive 2014/34/EU; the EGIDA sawmill hall is Zone 22, the blade workshop inside the hall inherits the classification.

**blade workshop log** (*dziennik warsztatu*, EN *workshop log*, ES *diario del taller de cintas*, UK *журнал майстерні стрічок*): a register of all welds performed in the EGIDA workshop (date, no., type, operator, welding parameters, measurement results), retention 5 years paper plus OneDrive scan.
