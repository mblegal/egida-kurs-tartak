## Introduction

Fifth lesson of Week 3. In the fifth lesson of Week 2 we talked about the **golden rule of feeding** — "never a hand past the working-zone line, never without the operator's signal". Today we develop this theme into **the full mechanics**.

Feeding material to the operator is **the most important activity in the production line**. And **the most dangerous for the helper**. Why?

- Because it happens **next to a running machine**. A band saw cuts at a speed of **30–40 m/s** at the edge of the blade. A circular saw — the disc rotates **2500–3000 rpm**. If the helper's hand enters the working zone — **there's no time to react**. Amputation takes 0.01 seconds.

- Because it demands **synchronisation with the operator**. The operator cuts in a rhythm — a log goes in, cutting takes 20–40 seconds, a finished board comes out the back. The helper **hands in the next log** exactly at the moment when the operator is **ready**. Too early = collision. Too late = operator waits, production drops.

- Because the operator **doesn't always see the helper**. The saw noise **drowns out speech**. Dust blocks visibility. Gloves and safety glasses **limit the operator's peripheral vision**. If the helper acts **without a visual signal**, the operator **doesn't know whether the helper is ready**.

That's why feeding has **signalling systems** — not ordinary conversation, but **standard gestures** understood industry-wide. **Stop. Go. Wait. Back up.** You'll learn them today.

You'll also learn **where the working-zone line is** (that yellow stripe on the floor is **not decoration**), **how to grip a board coming out** of the mill (never with both hands directly on it), **what to do when the operator switches off for a minute** (you don't enter the zone until the **padlock** hangs on the switch — LOTO — but that's an M2 topic), and **five emergency situations** (saw "gules", board cracks, kickback, jam, smoke).

## Objectives

After this lesson:

- You know the **five basic gestures** of operator↔helper communication: **STOP (vertical fist)**, **GO (thumb up)**, **WAIT (open hand, sideways)**, **BACK UP (thumb down)**, **HELP (crossed hands overhead)**.
- You understand where the **working-zone line** is (a yellow stripe 1–1.5 m from the machine) and that **NEVER** do you cross it without an explicit operator signal.
- You know how to **grip a board coming out** of the receiving conveyor: **only after it has stopped** or **at a distance from the disc/band** (minimum 1 m sideways), with **anti-slip gloves**, **without twisting the torso**.
- You know the **3-2-1 startup rhythm** (confirmation of gestures before the first cut) and the closing rhythm (who switches off, who tidies up, who moves to the next task).
- You can point out **five emergency situations** and the first action in each.

## Content

### Geography of the production line — who stands where

Before we discuss gestures — **who stands where**?

**Before the saw (the "feed" side)**: the log enters **from the operator's side**. The operator is **behind the control panel**, sees the log, controls the feed. The helper who **feeds logs** stands **to the side** (1.5–2 m) or **behind the operator** (if feeding with a cart). **Never directly in front of the log** — because if it "shot" backwards (rare, but possible), the helper is in the line of fire.

**Behind the saw (the "receiving" side)**: finished boards come out onto a **receiving conveyor** or a **drop-off table**. A second helper stands **at the end of the conveyor/table** (2–3 m from the saw), receives boards, stacks them on a cart or pre-sorts them. **Never close to the disc/band**, even if the machine looks "about to stop".

**Working-zone line**: a yellow stripe on the floor, 1–1.5 m from the edge of the machine. **Behind the yellow line** — the operator's working zone. **In front of the yellow line** — the helper's zone. Crossing the yellow line **only on an explicit operator gesture** (e.g. "HELP" = I really need help, come in) and **only after the machine has stopped**.

::: info
**Why 1–1.5 m?** Because this is the **minimum safe distance** for **wood chips** flying off the disc. A splinter travelling 20 m/s at 1.5 m distance — injures, but **doesn't kill** (usually). Less than 1 m — can knock out an eye, touch an artery. The yellow line is a **compromise** between ergonomics (easy access) and safety.
:::

### Five gestures — an international language

Gestures in the sawmill are **standardised** — understood everywhere, regardless of language. This is NOT a Polish sawmill custom — this is **ISO 7010** and **EU industry standards**. Every operator and every helper must know them. **You too.**

**1. STOP — vertical fist, raised above the head.** Means: **stop all movement immediately**. The operator notices the gesture, **does not finish the cut**, but brakes the band/disc. You use it when: you notice a person in the danger zone, you see fire/smoke, a log breaks unexpectedly, a board jams the band.

**2. GO — thumb up, arm straight.** Means: **ready, hand over the next**. The operator sees the gesture, **resumes the feed**. You use it when: you've taken the previous board, got into position, ready to take the next one.

**3. WAIT — open hand, vertical, to the side.** Means: **pause, do nothing, don't feed**. The operator stops the movement, but **doesn't switch the machine off**. You use it when: you need 10–30 seconds (to fix something, call another person, set something aside), but it's not an emergency.

**4. BACK UP — thumb down, retreating motion.** Means: **withdraw, pull back the log/board**. You use it when: you see a log defect while feeding (a huge knot, embedded stone, metal element — invisible earlier).

**5. HELP — both hands crossed above the head (X).** Means: **I need a second person, now**. Not a danger alarm (that's STOP), but a request for help. You use it when: one person can't manage to lift a board, something has jammed that needs a second person to unfasten, you need a visual confirmation ("do you see what I see?").

::: warning
**NEVER shout at the operator with words** while the machine is running. Saw noise = 90–105 dB. Your voice = 60–70 dB. The operator **doesn't hear**. Use **gestures**. If the operator has hearing protectors (often) — he doesn't hear even shouting. **Only sight.**
:::

### 3-2-1 rhythm — starting the cut

Before the first cut of a new log, the **3-2-1** ritual:

**3. Visual check.** The helper looks at the log: are there protruding wires, metal, stones. The operator looks at the machine: band sharpened, guards closed, dust extraction on. They both look **at each other**, confirm with their eyes.

**2. Ready gesture.** The helper shows **GO** (thumb up). The operator answers **GO** (thumb up). This is confirmation that **both sides are ready**.

**1. Start.** The operator starts the feed, the log enters. The helper **steps back** one pace (to a safe distance), **watches**. Doesn't walk away — waits, because the first board will come out any second.

This ritual takes **5–10 seconds**, but prevents 80% of accidents with unexpected start-up. **Helpers who skip 3-2-1 "because we're working fast" — lose fingers.**

### Log-feeding technique

What does the **physical feeding of a log** into a band saw (the most common type of mill in small/medium sawmills) look like?

**1. Log on a cart (sliding roller / loading cart).** The log lies **on the cart**, you **push/pull the cart**. **You don't lift by hand** — you're **on the side** of the cart as it rolls into the saw.

**2. Last 50 cm — you release the cart.** At 50 cm from the edge of the machine **you stop pushing**, the cart stops (there's a catch or a lock). You **step out of the cart area** — don't stand **behind the cart**, because at the moment of start-up the operator feeds the log into the band and **you must be out of the line**.

**3. GO gesture to the operator.** Only now do you show thumb up. The operator starts the feed. You **watch** the log entering the band, **ready to STOP** in case of the unexpected.

**4. The cut takes 20–40 seconds.** In this time: **you don't enter the working zone** (not once). You can use the moment to **prepare the next log** on the side. But **you must watch** — if something goes wrong, **STOP immediately**.

**5. End of the cut.** The operator gives a "done" signal (GO down or stops the machine). Only then **you enter** (past the yellow line, but **only** on an explicit gesture) to receive the next operation.

### Technique of receiving a board from the mill

On the other side — receiving the board coming out. Here **different technique**:

**1. The board comes onto the receiving conveyor.** The receiving conveyor is 3–5 m long. The first 2 m are **in the operator's working zone** (you don't touch). The last 2–3 m — **in your zone**.

**2. You don't grab the board "on the move".** You wait until it reaches the end of the conveyor, **stops**. Only then **you grip**. The reason for the rule: the board on the conveyor is for a moment **under drive**, it may **jerk**. Once stopped — it's safe.

**3. Two-handed grip, anti-slip gloves.** One hand in the middle of the board (for weight control), the other closer to the end (for direction). **Don't grip the very end** — the board has **fresh splinters** after cutting, you'll cut yourself.

**4. You set down on a cart/stack/sorting table.** You use the **5-step technique** from lesson 2 (bent knees, straight back). **Don't twist the torso** — if the board needs to go from left to right, **turn with your feet**.

**5. Ready gesture (GO) to the operator — after setting down.** The operator sees that you are **ready for the next one**.

::: example
**Rhythm 50 boards/hour.** Correct feeding = **about 50 boards per hour** (one every 70 seconds). That's **not too fast** by the look of it, but **physically healthy** (allows the 5-step technique) and **safe**. A helper who "does 100 boards/hour" = skips technique, shortens gestures, **ends up injured** in 3–6 months.
:::

### Five emergency situations

You must know **what to do** when something goes wrong. Here are **five situations**:

**1. The saw "gules" — a change in sound.** A normal saw sounds "ssh-ssh-ssh". If it starts to sound "rrrr-rrrr" or "clunk-clunk" — **something is jammed**. **STOP** immediately (gesture). The operator will hear it himself, but **confirm with the gesture**.

**2. A board cracks during cutting.** You see a crack along the board, pieces flying. **STOP** immediately. Step 3 m back from the conveyor — there may be **kickback**. The operator switches off the machine.

**3. Kickback.** Rare, but it happens: the log "jumps" back from the band. If you are **in the kickback line** — 480 kg of wood flies at you at 2–3 m/s. That's why **you NEVER stand straight behind the log**. Always to the **side**. If you see kickback signs (a crack, a jerk) — **jump to the side**, not backwards.

**4. A board jammed in the band.** The band has stopped (emergency), the board hangs between the guides. **DO NOT help pull it out.** That's done by **the operator with power off** (LOTO — locked out). If the operator asks you: "hand me the wrench", you hand the wrench. **A hand in the machine = amputation, even if it looks switched off.**

**5. Smoke / sparks / burning smell.** The disc or band may overheat — friction. Smoke is a signal: **the disc is overheating, it will crack soon**. **STOP** immediately, plus call the operator with the HELP gesture. The operator switches the machine off, cools the disc, assesses the damage.

::: warning
**Kickback is the most frequent saw death in Poland.** PIP reports 2020–2024 show: **70% of kills at the saw are kickback**, not blade cuts. Kickback kills helpers who **stand behind the log in the feed line**. **Always to the side.** No discussion, no "but it's faster for me". **To the side.**
:::

### Verbal communication — only when the machine is stopped

Gestures are **during machine operation**. When the machine **stops** (break, log change, end of shift) — **you can talk**. Then you:

- Verbally confirm what the gestures said ("OK, moving on")
- Report observations ("on that log I saw a 15 cm knot, a problem may emerge")
- Ask questions ("what do I do with this scrap at the front?")
- Coordinate the next tasks ("after this log we go for a break")

During machine operation **you are silent**. The operator won't hear, and you **lose concentration** by speaking and watching at the same time. **Eyes on the machine, hands ready, mouth shut.**

### What to do when the operator switches the machine off

Scenario: the operator switches the saw off, says "I have to change the band, 10 minutes". **What do you do?**

**DO NOT enter the working zone**, even if the machine looks off. Because:

- The operator may **restart it** unexpectedly (changed his mind, came back because he forgot something)
- Inertia of the band/disc after switch-off = **5–10 seconds** of further movement
- Hidden energy: spring, hydraulic system, dust under electrostatic charge

**The LOTO rule** (Lock Out / Tag Out) — that's the topic of **M2 Week 2 Lesson 5** ("Blocking machines before work"). For now remember: **"switched off" does not mean "safe"**. Safe = **you see the padlock on the main switch** and **feel absence of vibration** (minimum 30 seconds after switch-off).

So when the operator says "10 minutes" — you **sweep scrap at your station**, **prepare the next log** on the cart (outside the working zone), or **go for water/toilet**. You DO NOT approach the machine.

## Summary

Today you learned **the language of the production line**:

- **Five gestures** — STOP (fist), GO (thumb up), WAIT (open hand), BACK UP (thumb down), HELP (crossed hands). An international language, ISO 7010.
- **Working-zone line** — yellow stripe 1–1.5 m from the machine. Behind the line = operator. In front of the line = helper. **Never cross** without an explicit operator gesture and the machine stopped.
- **3-2-1 rhythm** — before the first cut: visual check (3), ready gesture (2), start (1). A 5–10-second ritual saves fingers.
- **Log-feeding technique** — push the cart, stop 50 cm from the machine, step out of the line, GO, watch.
- **Board-receiving technique** — wait for the stop on the receiving conveyor, two-handed grip, gloves, 5-step technique (l2).
- **Five emergency situations** — saw "gules", board cracks, kickback, jamming, smoke/sparks. Reaction: **STOP** with gesture, never a hand in the machine.
- **Kickback** is the biggest killer in the sawmill. **Always to the side of the log**, never directly behind it.
- **Silence, eyes on the machine** — talk **only** during breaks and changes. Voice won't cut through the saw.

The production line is **like a tango** — two people, **one rhythm**, **sure steps**, **flawless visual communication**. If you master this dance — you are a **safe and effective** helper. If you don't — you are **a danger to yourself and others**.

## Key terms

- ✋ **Gest STOP** (pl) — STOP signal (en) — gesto de STOP (es) — жест СТОП (uk). Vertical fist raised above the head. Means: stop immediately. Used in every emergency — you see danger, you react with the gesture.

- 👍 **Gest DAWAJ** (pl) — GO / GIVE signal (en) — gesto de "adelante" (es) — жест ДАВАЙ (uk). Thumb up, arm straight. Means: ready, hand over the next one. Standard communication between feedings.

- 📏 **Linia strefy roboczej** (pl) — working zone line (en) — línea de la zona de trabajo (es) — лінія робочої зони (uk). Yellow stripe on the floor 1–1.5 m from the machine. Boundary between the operator's zone and the helper's zone.

- 🎬 **Rytm 3-2-1** (pl) — 3-2-1 startup ritual (en) — ritmo 3-2-1 de arranque (es) — ритм 3-2-1 запуску (uk). Sequence before the first cut: visual check → ready gesture → start. 5–10 seconds, prevents 80% of unexpected-start-up accidents.

- 🔨 **Odrzut (kickback)** (pl) — kickback (en) — rechazo / retroceso (es) — віддача (uk). The log/board shooting back from the band/disc. Most common saw death in Poland (70% of kills). **Always to the side, never behind the log.**

- 🧵 **Taśma odbiorcza** (pl) — receiving conveyor (en) — cinta de recepción (es) — приймальний конвеєр (uk). Conveyor behind the saw on which the finished board exits. First 2 m in the operator's zone, last 2–3 m in the helper's zone.

- 🔒 **LOTO (Lock Out / Tag Out)** (pl) — LOTO blocking procedure (en) — procedimiento LOTO (es) — процедура LOTO (uk). Blocking machines before work (padlock on the switch). "Switched off" does not mean "safe" — safe = padlock. Details in M2-W2-L5.

- 🎭 **Synchronizacja operator-pomocnik** (pl) — operator-helper synchronisation (en) — sincronización operario-ayudante (es) — синхронізація оператор-помічник (uk). Rhythm of joint work: hand over → cut → receive → ready gesture → hand over. Like tango — one leader (operator), one follower (helper).

- 🤐 **Milczenie podczas pracy** (pl) — silence during operation (en) — silencio durante la operación (es) — мовчання під час роботи (uk). No talking during saw operation. Noise 90–105 dB drowns out the voice. Only gestures + hearing protectors.

- 👁️ **Obserwacja ciągła** (pl) — continuous observation (en) — observación continua (es) — безперервне спостереження (uk). Eyes on the machine the whole time it's running. Ready for STOP every second. This is NOT "staring" — this is active readiness.

## Trainer's notes

**Pedagogical goal**: ingrain **the five gestures** so deeply that the learner **reflexively** uses STOP at the sight of danger — without thinking. This is **motor memory**, not verbal. Whoever thinks "I have to raise my fist to give STOP" — **is already too late**.

**120-minute rhythm**:
- **0–15 min**: opening — recap from l5 W2 (golden rule of feeding, working-zone line). Ask the group: "Who knows the STOP gesture?" — almost always someone does. Ask: "Where does this gesture come from?" — few know it's ISO. State: gestures are an **EU standard**, not "a sawmill custom".
- **15–35 min**: **five gestures, demonstration and practice**. Trainer shows each gesture. Each learner **repeats**. Then a test: "show STOP", "show GO"... in random order. Fast, like military commands. Repeat until reflex.
- **35–55 min**: production-line geography — a drawing on the board (saw, receiving conveyor, yellow line, positions of helper-1 (feeder) and helper-2 (receiver)). Exercise: learner takes the "feeder" position. "Where exactly? Behind the yellow line, at what distance from the machine?" Measuring with tape.
- **55–75 min**: **3-2-1 rhythm** — role-played in pairs. One = operator (trainer demonstrates). Other = helper. Full sequence: check, ready gesture, start. Trainer corrects: "too fast", "you forgot to look at the log". Repetitions.
- **75–95 min**: **five emergency situations** — trainer describes each, group discusses the reaction, one learner shows the gesture (STOP). **Kickback as a special case** — side view of the kickback line drawing. "Where do you NOT stand? Here and here." (point).
- **95–115 min**: feeding and receiving technique. If there's a shop floor and a stopped machine (without power!) — physical practice. If not — model role-play in the group.
- **115–120 min**: key terms, oral quiz (five gestures in random order), announcement of l6 (hand tools — **you use tools alone for the first time**, not on a machine).

**Frequent mistake**: learners "improvise" gestures. They give "GO" with two thumbs, "STOP" with crossed hands (confusing with HELP). **Trainer's response**: "Gestures are a **standard**. If you improvise — the operator **won't understand**. An operator in Poland, in Germany, in Spain — all understand the same gestures because they are in ISO 7010. Your improvisation = your accident."

**Practical recommendation**: during the first 2 weeks of work the learner **says the gesture's name out loud** while showing it. "STOP!" + fist. "GO!" + thumb. Saying it out loud reinforces motor memory. After 2 weeks the voice goes, the movement stays.

**Link to the next lesson**: l6 is **hand tools — hand saw, plane, measuring rule**. The first time **you use tools alone** (you're not on the machine line). That means different technique, different OHS. But you still work **next to** the machine, so the gestures from l5 stay in use.
