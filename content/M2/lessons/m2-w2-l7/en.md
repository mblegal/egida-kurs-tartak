## Introduction

Monday, 6:45 a.m. Maricel Santos walked into the hall first among the operators, even before foreman Marek. The weekend is over, but the smell of machine oil and sawdust hangs in the air like a Friday evening. Marek shows up two minutes later with a notebook in the pocket of his overalls and a piece of white industrial chalk in his hand. "Come on, today we're doing the saw walk. All four types, all stations. You'll see what wore out during a week of cutting oak."

This lesson introduces **the four types of saws used in a typical sawmill**: band, circular, frame, and multi-rip. For each one you learn the **anatomy** (tooth, tip, set, tension) and the **five main signs of wear** that a junior operator must recognise and report. The lesson closes the 80/20 rule from the previous one (lumber grading): this time it is not about sorting boards, but about **sorting tools into two piles: fit for work and to be replaced**. The decision to replace is made by the foreman or senior operator, but **recognising and reporting is already the junior operator's duty**.

A saw is not an object that "either cuts or doesn't cut". It is a **consumable** that loses its working properties gradually over dozens of hours of cutting. An operator who doesn't see this process is an invisible hazard for the sawmill: he keeps cutting with a saw that in an hour will break apart and send a fragment flying through the hall at 200 km/h.

## Objectives

After this lesson:

1. You can tell apart **four types of saws** by appearance and use: band (band saw, "trak"), circular (circular saw), frame (gang saw, "gatter", vertical frame saw), multi-rip (multi-rip saw with 2–12 blades on a common shaft).
2. You know the **anatomy of a saw tooth**: tooth (the cut-out), tip (the cutting edge), gullet (the bottom between teeth), set (lateral deflection), tension (longitudinal tension of 2200–2400 PSI for a band).
3. You recognise the **five main signs of wear**: crack at the tooth base (gullet), tip dulling, loss of set, wavy kerf, blue overheating patches.
4. You know the **approximate service life of a band**: 20–40 hours cutting dry pine, 6–12 hours cutting dry oak, 4–8 hours cutting fresh oak. Change daily when cutting oak, weekly when cutting pine.
5. You apply the **wear reporting protocol**: EVERY crack, EVERY blue patch, EVERY wavy kerf goes to the foreman without discussion. Tip dulling and loss of set – you report at the first doubt (you are a junior, you don't have to be certain).
6. You understand the limit of your competence: **you recognise and report, you do not replace on your own**. Changing the band is a separate lesson (l8) and always happens under supervision.
7. You know the risk statistic: **11% of sawmill accidents in the PIP 2019–2023 data are band or blade breakage**, of which **60% resulted from an unrecognised crack at the tooth base**. The tool walk after the weekend and before the shift is not a ritual, it is the mechanics of survival.

## Content

### Four types of saws – what works where

A typical Polish sawmill runs **four types of saws**, each in a different link of the breakdown chain. Memorise the order the way a board travels from log to package:

**1. Band saw** (*band saw*, "trak"). The heart of the sawmill. An endless band (a loop of 4–6 m, width 38–75 mm, thickness 1.07 mm) tensioned between two wheels of 900–1400 mm. First breakdown of the log into thick boards or a cant. You recognise it by the two large wheel housings (top and bottom). **The dominant sawmill saw in Poland**.

**2. Circular saw** (*circular saw*). A round blade of 300–800 mm, 24–80 teeth, often with tungsten carbide (HM) inserts. The edger and the cross-cut saw – second and third links in the chain. You recognise it by the round blade and fast rotation (2000–4000 rpm).

**3. Frame saw** (*frame saw*, "gatter", gang saw). A frame with 4–20 straight vertical blades; the whole frame moves up and down. Cuts the log into lumber in a single pass (all boards at once). An older technology, being pushed out by the band saw, but still dominant at older sawmills. Slower (0.5–2 m/min vs 5–20 m/min for the band).

**4. Multi-rip saw** (*multi-rip saw*). 2–12 blades on a common shaft, parallel cutting. Converts a cant into boards in a single pass. Used mainly in the second stage after the band saw.

::: info
The EGIDA Foundation sawmill uses three types: two Wood-Mizer LT40 band saws (positions #1 and #2 in the hall), one edger (circular, with 4 edging blades), one multi-rip saw (6 blades, for cutting cants). There is no frame saw – but at the neighbouring sawmill in Parczew there stands a gatter from the 1970s, once operated by the Polish state forestry mills.
:::

### Anatomy of a band saw tooth

Before you recognise wear, you have to see what actually wears out in a tooth. Let's take the band saw as the example (the anatomy of a circular blade is similar, it only differs in the shape of the body):

- **Tooth** – the cut-out in the toothed edge of the band, repeating every 20–30 mm (tooth pitch). A tooth has **three working surfaces**: tip, front face (cuts the fibres), back face (evacuates the chip).
- **Tip / point** – the cutting edge of the tooth, the most heavily loaded spot. On better-grade saws it is reinforced with **stellite** (cobalt alloy, hardness 50–55 HRC) or **tungsten carbide**. The tip makes the first cut into the wood fibre.
- **Gullet** – the rounded bottom between teeth. It has two functions: it collects the chip during cutting and distributes bending stresses. **Fatigue cracks are born right here** (smallest material thickness + greatest bending).
- **Set** (or swage) – lateral deflection of every second tooth to the left and right by **0.3–0.6 mm** (depending on the wood species). Thanks to the set the **kerf** (cut width) is wider than the band itself – the body of the band does not rub against the walls of the kerf. Pine needs a wider set than oak (softwood is more elastic, it "squeezes" the kerf more).
- **Band tension** – longitudinal tension of 2200–2400 PSI (152–166 MPa, corresponding to a tensile force of 25–40 kN across the band's cross-section). Tension is a **professional number**: the foreman checks it during replacement with the tensioner's gauge, the junior operator must know the range. Too low – the band wobbles, the kerf runs crooked. Too high – the band breaks at the tooth base or at the weld.

::: tip
The trainer will show you: "A tooth with no set is like a shovel with no edge – it cuts by force, not geometry. A tooth with a dulled tip is a shovel with a square edge. A tooth with a gullet crack is a shovel that is about to snap off and hit you. You look at these three things: a sharp tip, visible set, a smooth gullet with no mark."
:::

### Five main signs of wear – what the operator has to see

The saw walk after the weekend and before every shift is **five questions** asked of every saw in the hall. Three seconds of looking at each, a finger passing along the edge (never with the machine running!), the eye searching for colour.

**1. Crack at the tooth base (gullet).** The most dangerous defect. A fatigue microcrack is born at the bottom of the gullet and grows with every cycle of the band bending over the saw wheels. From the appearance of the crack to full breakage passes 10⁴–10⁵ cycles, **which is from 10 minutes to two hours of running**. A visible crack is already the last warning bell.

**Appearance:** a thin dark line 1–5 mm running from the gullet towards the back of the band. Often only visible at an angle, under good light. **Junior rule: ANY line in the gullet = STOP, report, replace.** You don't measure, you don't interpret – you see and you speak.

::: warning
A broken band saw flies off at a circumferential speed of **50–70 m/s** (180–250 km/h). Typical fragment range: **5–8 metres** from the saw. The Regulation of the Ministry of Economy of 2000, § 10, requires band guards (upper and lower), but the guard stops only 70–80% of the fragment's energy. The rest flies around the hall. In the PIP 2019–2023 data, **11% of all sawmill accidents** are band or blade breakage – of which **60% resulted from an unrecognised crack at the tooth base**, which would have shown up at the morning walk.
:::

**2. Tip dulling.** The tip goes from sharp (radius 50–100 µm) to rounded (300–500 µm) after 6–20 hours of cutting. A 30–50% rise in feed force, burning of the kerf, the smell of scorching.

**Appearance:** a shiny tip instead of a matte sharp one. Dark patches in the kerf on the board, especially with oak (tannins burn brown at 250–300 °C).

**3. Loss of set.** The teeth level out under load (0.3–0.6 mm → 0.1–0.2 mm after 30–50 hours). The kerf narrows, the band rubs against the kerf walls, it heats up.

**Appearance:** teeth "even" from the side instead of the visible zig-zag of the set. The kerf pinches the band at the end of the cut (the characteristic "howl"), the board may come out wedge-shaped.

**4. Wavy kerf.** The surface of the kerf is rippled like corrugated sheet. Usually a combined effect: dulling + loss of set + a drop in tension.

**Appearance:** the board coming off the saw shows 1–3 mm amplitude waves on the cut surface. After the machine is stopped, a finger should feel no wave.

**5. Blue overheating patches.** Steel above 300 °C changes colour: straw yellow (200 °C) → brown (250 °C) → steel blue (300–350 °C). **A change of colour = permanent loss of elasticity.** The band does not return to its original properties after cooling down.

**Appearance:** a blue band 5–30 mm along the saw, most often between the teeth and the tensioner wheel. Unambiguously blue, impossible to mistake.

::: example
Maricel, Monday 6:55, inspection of saw #2. She looks at the band with a head torch (the hall is still half-dark before the main lights come on). Three teeth away from the lower guide she sees: **a thin dark line, 3 mm, running from the gullet towards the back**. She points it out to Marek. Marek brings his face to within 10 cm, nods: "Good eye. Fatigue crack, third day on this band cutting oak. Stop the saw, I'll get a spare. At 7:20 today we're doing the change – you stay, you watch, you assist. This band we don't turn on again." The break did NOT happen – because on the walk Maricel saw exactly what § 13 of the Regulation of the Ministry of Economy of 2000 requires to be checked: the state of the guards and the state of the working tool before every start-up.
:::

### Approximate band service life by species

The band saw is a **consumable**. The sawmill's tool budget assumes replacement after a defined cutting time – but the time depends on the species:

| Species | Wood condition | Band service life | Change every |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pine, spruce | dry (MC 15–20%) | 20–40 h | weekly |
| Pine, spruce | fresh (MC >30%) | 12–20 h | every 3–4 days |
| Oak, beech | dry | 6–12 h | daily |
| Oak, beech | fresh | 4–8 h | daily (sometimes 2× a day) |
| Larch, ash | dry | 10–18 h | every 2–3 days |

Wear on oak is **3–4× faster** than on pine for two reasons: the **tannins** in oak act corrosively on steel, and oak's **density** (650–720 kg/m³) requires 2× the feed force of pine (400–500 kg/m³). Fresh wood wears the band 2× faster than dry wood – free water cools the tool, but **tannins in solution** are more aggressive than in crystalline form.

::: info
Weekly cutting plan at the sawmill: Monday oak (new band), Tuesday oak (end of band, replacement), Wednesday beech (new band), Thursday beech (end, replacement), Friday pine (new band). The Friday pine band lasts the whole following week. That is the foreman's planning, but the junior has to understand the logic – so he doesn't wonder "we just changed it yesterday, and now again?".
:::

### Wear reporting protocol – the 80/20 rule applied to tools

From lesson 6 you remember the 80/20 rule in lumber grading: after 3 months of experience you grade 80% on your own, 20% of unclear cases go to the master. **For saws the ratio is different – and more conservative.** The risk of error is disproportionately greater: a wrong grade on a board costs a few dozen zlotys, a broken band costs an amputation or a life.

**Reporting rule for the junior operator (0–6 months):**
- **EVERY gullet crack** – immediate report, machine stopped, no discussion.
- **EVERY blue patch** – same.
- **EVERY wave visible on the board** – same (waviness is a composite signal; behind it stands one of three faults you cannot yet tell apart).
- **Tip dulling** – report at the first doubt. If your finger senses that the tip "is round", or the kerf burns dark patches – you speak up.
- **Loss of set** – same. You hear the "howl" at the end of the cut or see the board coming out wedge-shaped – you speak up.

**The key phrase** you use: "**Foreman, please take a look – I'm not sure whether this is already wear or not yet.**" You do not say "this is worn out" (that is the foreman's decision), you do not say "nothing has happened" (that is an interpretation outside your scope). You say "please take a look" – and you point with your finger.

**Statistics**: in 2019–2023 PIP analysed 156 band breakage accidents in Polish sawmills. 94 (60%) had a prior **visible gullet crack**, detectable during a routine walk. In 67 of them the walk **did take place, but the junior did not report** ("I wasn't sure", "I thought it was supposed to be that way", "I didn't want to bother the foreman"). That is exactly the pattern this lesson cuts out: **you don't have to be sure, you just have to point with your finger.**

## Link to practice

**Exercise 1 (morning walk, 15 min):** In the first week after this lesson you accompany the foreman during the morning saw walk. Your task: **say nothing**, just watch where the foreman looks and where he stops longer. After three days you do the walk yourself, the foreman goes behind you. After a week you do the walk alone, the foreman checks your report (a list of the saws with "OK" or "check" notes).

**Exercise 2 (learning the look, 30 min, one-off):** The foreman shows you three bands for comparison: (1) a brand-new band straight from the box, (2) a band after 15 hours of cutting pine, (3) a band after 10 hours of cutting oak, withdrawn for replacement. You look, you run your finger along the tip (machine off, band removed), you listen to the foreman's commentary. **That one comparative hour gives you more than the whole manual.** You will come back to it in memory for months afterwards.

**Exercise 3 (reporting phrase):** Rehearse the sentence in your head or out loud: "Foreman, please take a look – I'm not sure whether this is already wear or not yet." Repeat it ten times so it becomes a habit. Under stress you only use sentences that sit in memory without thinking.

## Trainer notes

**Pedagogical goal**: moving the learner from observer of the material (l1–l6) to observer of the tool. First contact with the logic of the machine – not "how to use it", but "what is happening to it over time". The notion of a consumable is introduced implicitly through the service-life table and the replacement cycle.

**Week 2 arc**: Maricel returns after her debut in Block 1 T2 (l1 moisture). The arc = observation of the material → observation of the tool → assisting at the machine (l8). The learner ends week 2 with the full context that in week 3 will let him begin performing operations.

**Teaching pitfalls**:
1. No metallurgy of steel. The junior does not need martensite – he needs to recognise a blue patch and know that this is the end of the band.
2. Don't skip the cutting-time table. Without it the junior does not know whether a band that has been cutting oak for 4 hours is "old" or "fresh". The range of hours is a professional number just like 2200–2400 PSI.
3. The "please take a look" phrase is the foundation – learners from hierarchical cultures (Vietnam, the Philippines, Central Asia) tend to stay silent so as not to bother the foreman. This phrase opens a way without violating hierarchy.

**Integration with previous lessons**: l4 (wood defects) → l7 (tool defects) – same eyes, different criterion. l6 80/20 rule → l7 sharpened to "at any doubt, speak up" because of the risk. l5 machine chain → l7 four saw types correspond to the links of the chain.

**Formative test (5 min)**: lay out 3 bands (new, lightly worn, withdrawn) + 2 circular blades (new, dulled). Sort them into two piles, "fit for work" vs "to be replaced", plus a one-sentence justification. The justification matters more than the sort.

**Integration with M3**: in M3 week 2 the learner learns to replace the band on his own (in M2 he only assists). The saw walk is a ritual that runs through all 3 modules. In M3, welding a cracked band and straightening axial bending are added.
