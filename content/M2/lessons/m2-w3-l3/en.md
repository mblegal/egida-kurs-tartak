## Introduction

Wednesday, 7:00, band saw Wood-Mizer LT40 number two. On Monday Oleksii started it up on his own, on Tuesday he adjusted the guide rollers (four rollers in the 3.2–3.8 mm range, difference 0.4 mm – Marek wrote "OK" without comment). Today is the **first production cut** – not a test kerf on a scrap board, but a 3.5-metre oak log, 42 cm in diameter, moisture content 28 percent (measured yesterday evening with a resistance moisture meter, lesson 2 of week 2). The log comes from a three-year batch of the Dębowa Góra Forest District, class A after the top was trimmed off, intended use: edged sawn timber 25 × 150 × 3000 mm for a local furniture joiner. Marek puts a sheet with a feed-rate table on the console: "Oak, moisture 25–30 percent, thickness 50 mm, Silvertip band: feed rate 18–22 millimetres per second". Oleksii looks at the table, then at the saw, then at Marek: "Is this the fourth number of the trade?". Marek nods: "Yes, but the first one that is not fixed. Feed rate **depends on the material** – one oak is not like another oak, dry is not like fresh, 30 mm thickness is not like 70. A trade number that varies, not one that is rigid."

This lesson introduces the **fourth professional parameter** of a band-saw operator alongside the three fixed numbers from lesson 8 of week 2 (tension 2200–2400 PSI, rollers 3–4 mm, idle run 30 s). Feed rate is the **first variable parameter** – you will not find a single value for your entire career as an operator. You will find a **band of 10–80 mm/s** (for a band saw cutting hardwood and softwood) and **four variables** that decide where within that band you sit for a given cut: wood species, moisture, board thickness, band type.

Setting feed rate is the **first situation in Module 2 in which you match the machine to the material, not only to yourself**. So far you have learned fixed procedures (PPE, STOP, checklist, start-up, guide adjustment) – now you are learning to **read signals from the machine** (engine sound, chip colour, smoke) and to decide: "I speed up, I slow down, I stop and change the band". This lesson closes the triad of Block 1 of week 3: **switch on (l1) – adjust (l2) – cut (l3)**.

## Objectives

After this lesson:

1. You understand that **feed rate is a variable parameter**, dependent on four variables: wood species (pine 40–80 mm/s, oak 15–30 mm/s, beech 12–25 mm/s – more than twofold difference), moisture (a moisture content of 30 percent reduces feed rate by 20–30 percent relative to air-dry wood), board thickness (a 70 mm board requires a feed rate of 60–70 percent of the feed rate for a 25 mm board), band type (Silvertip allows 20 percent higher feed rate than the standard Wood-Mizer DoubleHard).
2. You can **read the feed-rate table** prepared for the Fundacja EGIDA sawmill: four species (pine, spruce, oak, beech) × three moisture classes (air-dry up to 20 percent, half-dry 20–35 percent, fresh above 35 percent) × three thickness classes (thin up to 30 mm, medium 30–60 mm, thick above 60 mm). The table gives you a starting range – the final feed rate is set by observing the machine.
3. You know the **three signs of feed rate too high**, requiring immediate reduction or STOP: (a) smoke from the kerf (band overheating above 200 °C), (b) kerf waviness visible to the eye (the band bends under load), (c) motor overload on the ammeter (current above the nominal nameplate value, usually 15–18 A for the LT40). Each of the three signs = left hand on STOP from lesson 3 of week 1, reporting to Marek.
4. You know the **three signs of feed rate too low**, requiring an increase or a band change: (a) chips fine as dust instead of even filings 2–5 mm (the band is grinding instead of cutting), (b) dark, burnt kerf smelling of scorching (the band is heating up instead of shearing), (c) the band "jumping" in the gullet with no forward travel (worn tooth edge, band replacement – back to lessons 7–8 of week 2).
5. You apply the rule **"start at the lower limit of the range, accelerate only on the foreman's hand signal"**: oak 50 mm moisture 28 percent = range 18–22 mm/s, you start at 18 mm/s (lower limit). If the machine runs calmly (motor 13–14 A, even chips, no smoke), after 30 seconds Marek raises his hand = you may accelerate to 20 mm/s. Further only on another signal. You never start at the upper limit of the range – never.

### Four variables – why feed rate is not fixed

**Wood species** decides first. Pine (density 450–550 kg/m³, soft, fibrous) yields to the cut easily – feed rate 40–80 mm/s for dry pine 50 mm. Oak (density 650–720 kg/m³, hard, 50 percent denser than pine, plus tannins that abrade the band teeth) requires a feed rate of 15–30 mm/s – more than twice as slow. Beech is even more difficult, not because of hardness, but because of **false heartwood** (lesson 4 of week 2) – zones of harder wood in random places within the log. When cutting beech, the feed rate may suddenly become too high when passing through false heartwood, even though a dozen or so centimetres earlier it was fine.

**Moisture** has the second effect. Fresh wood (moisture 40–70 percent, lesson 1 of week 2) **is not easier to cut**, it only seems that way at first impression: the chips are wet, the kerf looks smooth. The problem: water in the wood pulls heat away from the band more quickly (the heat capacity of water is 4.2 kJ/kg·K, of wood 1.5–2.5 kJ/kg·K), the band **heats up more on the edge**, the teeth lose hardness. That is why the feed rate for fresh wood = 60–70 percent of the feed rate for air-dry wood. Trainees often get this wrong at this stage: "fresh wood cuts more easily, so I will speed up" – no, you cut more slowly, because you are protecting the band, not because it is harder.

**Board thickness** has the third effect. A thin board (25–30 mm) resists the cut for a shorter time – less material to evacuate, less heat to dissipate. A thick board (70+ mm) requires more time for every millimetre of forward travel. Practical rule: feed rate for a 70 mm board = 60–70 percent of the feed rate for a 25 mm board of the same species and moisture. This is not a linear relationship – it is a curve with a steeper drop for very thick boards (above 100 mm the feed rate falls sharply because of the risk of the band deflecting under load).

**Band type** has the fourth effect, but cannot be ignored. The **Wood-Mizer Silvertip** band (hard-faced edge, chromium-cobalt coating) withstands an edge temperature up to 280 °C and allows 20 percent higher feed rate than the standard **Wood-Mizer DoubleHard** (hardened edge, no overlay). A band past 50 percent of its service life (lesson 7 of week 2 – service-life table pine 20–40 h, oak 6–12 h) should have its feed rate reduced by 15–20 percent – worn teeth need more force, so less speed.

### Three signs of feed rate too high – STOP or reduction

**Sign 1: Smoke from the kerf.** A light, grey smoke rises from the cut. Reason: the band edge temperature has exceeded 200 °C, the wood is starting to carbonise (pyrolysis – thermal decomposition of cellulose). Smoke means that the band **has already been damaged** – it has lost edge hardness, the teeth are dulled for good. Reaction: STOP immediately, report to Marek, probable band replacement (lesson 8 of week 2). You do not just reduce the feed rate and continue – a band that has smoked once will not return to full performance.

**Sign 2: Kerf waviness.** The kerf surface is visibly wavy – lines with a wavelength of 10–30 mm and an amplitude of 0.5–2 mm. Reason: the band bends under the material's pressing force, returns to the vertical position, bends again – rhythmic oscillation. Waviness means that either the feed rate is too high, or the band tension is too low (below 2200 PSI, back to lesson 8 of week 2), or the guide rollers are set incorrectly (above 4 mm, back to lesson 2 of this week). Reaction: reduce the feed rate by 30 percent, if the waviness does not disappear – STOP, check tension and rollers.

**Sign 3: Motor overload.** The LT40 saw has an ammeter on the main motor (60 mm dial, scale 0–30 A, green zone 10–15 A = normal operation, yellow 15–18 A = raised load, red above 18 A = overload). A current above 18 A for more than 10 seconds = motor overload, risk of winding damage (temperature above 130 °C, insulation class B). Reaction: reduce the feed rate by 40 percent immediately, watch the ammeter – if it does not return to the green zone within 10 seconds, STOP.

::: warning
**The three-signs rule**: each of the three signs (smoke, waviness, overload) = an independent decision by the trainee. You do not wait for the foreman, you do not analyse, you do not think "maybe it will pass on its own". Left hand on STOP, release the material, then report. PIP (Polish Labour Inspectorate) statistics 2019–2023: 19 percent of accidents on band saws arise from **ignoring the signs** – operators saw smoke, waviness or heard the motor labouring, but "wanted to finish the board". Finishing that one board cost a finger amputation in 67 of 94 recorded cases.
:::

### Three signs of feed rate too low – accelerate or change the band

**Sign 1: Chips fine as dust.** The band produces fine dust instead of even filings 2–5 mm long. Reason: the band is rubbing against the wood instead of shearing it. The teeth are either worn (band replacement) or the feed rate is too low. If the band is fresh (mounted yesterday) – increase the feed rate by 20 percent and watch the chip shape again after ten seconds. If the band is past 50+ percent of its service life – replacement. The dust sign is the earliest warning that the band has stopped cutting in the proper sense of the word and is starting to abrade: the sooner you react, the longer the remaining service life of the band.

**Sign 2: Dark, burnt kerf.** The kerf surface has brown or black streaks, smells faintly of scorching (weaker than with the "smoke" sign). Reason: the band is heating up from friction, not from cutting – feed rate too low, the band "rides" over the surface instead of entering the wood. Reaction: increase the feed rate by 15–20 percent, watch the kerf – it should clear to the natural wood colour.

**Sign 3: The band "jumps" in the gullet with no forward travel.** You hear a rhythmic knocking – the band enters the wood, rebounds, enters, rebounds. Reason: worn teeth do not grip the wood, the feed rate does not matter (even a small rate is too high for a worn band). Reaction: STOP, band replacement (lesson 8 of week 2).

### Oleksii's first production cut – sequence of decisions

Oleksii sets the feed rate at the lower limit: **18 mm/s**. He starts the cut, the oak log enters the band, the chips fly out in even lengths of 3–4 mm, the motor sound is steady. Marek looks at the ammeter: **14 A, green zone**. After 30 seconds Marek raises his hand: "Speed up to 20". Oleksii adjusts, the ammeter rises to 16 A – the yellow zone, still acceptable. After another minute the motor returns to 14 A (the band has "got into rhythm"). Marek: "Leave it at 20, finish the log at that speed". Oleksii completes the first production cut **in 3 minutes 40 seconds** for a 3000 mm long board – productively, safely, with two decisions made under Marek's eye (start 18, accelerate to 20). He writes in the register: "Oak, moisture 28 percent, thickness 50 mm, Silvertip band series 2026-02-14, feed rate start 18, final 20, ammeter 14–16 A, chips even, kerf smooth, cutting time 3 min 40 s".

## Check yourself

1. Give the four variables that affect the feed rate of a band saw. In what order do you consider them and why?
2. Why does fresh wood require a slower feed rate, even though it "seems easier to cut"? What happens to the band when it meets wet wood?
3. Give the three signs of feed rate too high. Which of the three signs means the band has already been damaged and needs replacement, regardless of any further reduction in feed rate?
4. Give the three signs of feed rate too low. Which of the three signs means the problem is in the band (wear), not in the feed rate?
5. Why is the rule "start at the lower limit of the range, accelerate only on the foreman's signal" safer than "I estimate the middle of the range straight away"? What happens if you start at the upper limit?
6. How do you recognise motor overload? Where is the ammeter, what are the zones (green/yellow/red) and what value means "reduce by 40 percent immediately"?
7. What do you do when you see smoke from the kerf? Is it enough to reduce the feed rate, or do you have to change the band?

## Link to practice

**Exercise 1 (first production cuts under supervision, every day for the first two weeks):** Marek gives you one log a day to cut through. You set the feed rate from the table, start at the lower limit, wait for the raised-hand signal to accelerate. Marek notes in his observation notebook: (a) choice of starting feed rate from the table (target: no mistake in 10 consecutive cases), (b) reaction to the raised-hand signal (target: reaction time below 3 seconds), (c) ammeter observation (target: not crossing the yellow zone).

**Exercise 2 (sign-recognition test, once a week):** Once a week Marek asks: "What are the three signs of feed rate too high?" and "What are the three signs of feed rate too low?". You answer from memory without notes. Three plus three, in six seconds, zero hesitation. After a month extended questions: "You see smoke from the kerf – what do you do?" (answer: STOP, report to Marek, probable band replacement, not only a reduction in feed rate).

**Exercise 3 (entry in the cutting register, after every production cut):** After every cut made under supervision you enter into the workstation register: species, moisture, thickness, band type, starting feed rate, final feed rate, ammeter min/max, cutting time, kerf quality, any remarks. After 10 entries Marek reviews the register and comments. You learn how the choice of feed rate affects productivity (cutting time) and kerf quality (surface smoothness).

## Trainer notes

**Pedagogical goal**: introducing the first variable parameter in Module 2. Feed rate differs from the 3 fixed numbers from l8 W2 (2200–2400 PSI, 3–4 mm, 30 s) in that **it is not fixed** – it depends on four variables. This is the first lesson in which the trainee matches the machine to the material, not only to themselves. Pedagogically demanding: Oleksii cannot "learn one number", he has to learn to **read signals from the machine**.

**Feed-rate table**: the key teaching tool. Do not teach the trainee "16 values by heart" – give them the table on paper, let them use it every day for the first 3 months. Automatism of values will come through repeated cuts, not through memorisation. Three-month goal: the trainee knows approximate ranges for 4 species × 2 moisture levels (dry, fresh) without looking at the table – not all 16 combinations. The paper table stays on the saw permanently as a reference.

**Closing the triad of Block 1 W3**: l1 (start-up, 6 steps) + l2 (adjustment, 5 steps, 4 rollers) + l3 (cutting, 4 variables, 6 signs) = full competence in operating the band saw at the junior level under supervision. After these three lessons Oleksii **is an operator**, not an assistant. Not independent, but an operator in the full sense of the word (performs procedures, handles parameters, reads machine signals).

**Delivery pitfalls**:
1. **Do not teach "the upper limit of the range"**. A junior operator always starts at the lower limit, without exception. In 6 months they will learn to work in the middle of the range, in 12 months they can reach for the upper limit, but not in the first weeks.
2. **Do not play down the signs**. Smoke, waviness, overload: each an independent decision by the trainee to STOP without waiting for the foreman. The PIP statistic of 19 percent of accidents = ignoring the signs is to be remembered. Show a concrete case: a veteran operator with 22 years of experience, saw smoke, wanted to finish a 30 cm board, thumb amputation after the overheated band snapped.

**Integration with previous lessons**: l1 W2 (moisture as a parameter) → moisture is the second variable of feed rate. l4 W2 (defects: false heartwood in beech) → feed rate for beech must be conservative because of random harder zones. l6 W2 (timber grades A/B/C/D) → kerf quality affects the final grade, a poor feed rate = a lower grade = a smaller margin. l7 W2 (5 signs of band wear) → signs 1–2 of feed rate too low overlap with the wear signs. l1 W3 (start-up) + l2 W3 (adjustment) → l3 closes the triad "switch on – adjust – cut".

**Preview of Block 2 W3**: other machines (circular saw, multi-rip, edger, planer) and timber sorting. With competence in operating the band saw, Oleksii moves to the next machines in the sawmill. In Block 3 W3 he closes the week with kiln drying, a process that does not require a rotating machine but does require thermodynamic knowledge.
