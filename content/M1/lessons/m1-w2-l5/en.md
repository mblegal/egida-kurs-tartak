## Introduction

Until now we've talked about **wood** — what it is, where it comes from, what defects it has. Today we change topic. Today we're talking about **tools**, that is, **what you work with**.

In a sawmill **two kinds of tools** you'll meet every day:

1. **Machines** (saws, debarkers, frame saws, conveyors, forklifts). These you **don't operate** as a helper — we'll talk about them in lessons 6 and 7.
2. **Hand tools** (hand saws, chisels, planes, hammer, square, drill). These you **touch**, **carry**, **pass** from your first day.

Hand tools look harmless because they are small and have no engines. But here is the **safety paradox** that you must understand from the start:

> **A blunt tool is more dangerous than a sharp one.**

Why? Because a blunt tool requires **force**. Force + slip + blade = accident. A sharp tool cuts **with its own weight**, lightly, without pressing. A sharp tool **goes where you want it**. A blunt tool **goes where you don't want it**.

The same paradox applies to **passing tools**. A helper who **throws a hammer** to a colleague is a danger to himself and others. A helper who **passes handle-first** is a professional.

In this lesson you'll learn the seven most important hand tools, their names in Polish, and — most important — **how to behave with them**.

## Goals

After this lesson:

- You can name in Polish the seven basic hand tools: **piła ręczna** (handsaw), **dłuto** (chisel), **strug** (plane), **młotek** (hammer), **przymiar / calówka** (rule), **kątownik** (square), **wkrętarka** (power drill).
- You understand the **safety paradox**: a blunt tool is more dangerous than a sharp one.
- You know how to **safely pass a tool** to another person (handle-first, blade away from you).
- You remember the **5S** principle: every tool has **its place** to which it returns after use.

## Content

### Tool 1: Handsaw

A **handsaw** (Polish: **piła ręczna**) is a toothed blade fixed in a handle. In a sawmill you'll meet two types:

**Crosscut saw** (Polish: **płatnica**) — a large handsaw with a straight, long blade (50–70 cm). Used for cross-cutting boards and square timber. You hold it with both hands (one on the handle, the other steadying the material).

**Frame saw / bow saw** (Polish: **piła ramowa**, **lisica**) — a smaller saw with the blade tensioned in a wooden or metal frame. For precise cutting of smaller pieces.

**Safety:**

- You always carry a saw with the **blade covered** when not in use (a plastic or leather cap).
- **Never walk** with an uncovered saw — one stumble and you'll cut yourself or a colleague.
- After use the saw **goes on a hook** in the assigned place, blade pointing down, handle up.

::: warning
**A blunt handsaw blade** requires **three times more force** than a sharp one. That force easily slips — and the saw hits the **fingers holding the material**. If you feel that your saw "isn't going by itself", **stop**. Tell the foreman "the saw is blunt". The foreman will change the blade or give you another saw. **Never push harder** on a blunt saw.
:::

### Tool 2: Chisel

A **chisel** (Polish: **dłuto**) is a **steel blade** with a wooden or plastic handle. You use it with a **hammer** — you strike the handle and the blade bites into the wood.

In a sawmill chisels are used for:

- Cutting recesses (for hinges, locks).
- Cleaning out knots before drilling.
- Fine handwork.

**Safety:**

- You always hold a chisel with **both hands** (one on the handle, the other guiding the blade tip).
- **Your other hand NEVER goes in front of the blade** — always behind it or to the side.
- When passing a chisel to a colleague: **handle-first**, blade covered with your palm or a piece of wood.
- After use the chisel goes back into a **case** (leather or plastic) or a special drawer.

### Tool 3: Plane

A **plane** (Polish: **strug**) is a blade in a wooden body with which you **take thin shavings** off a board's surface. After planing the board is **smooth** and even.

In a sawmill you'll meet two types:

- **Wooden plane** — wooden body, single blade set at an angle.
- **Metal plane** — same principle but metal construction, blade fastened with screws.

**Safety:**

- You hold the plane **with both hands** by the handles (one front, one back).
- You plane **in one direction** — away from yourself, along the grain. **Never sideways or toward yourself.**
- After use **retract the blade** (back off the adjusting screw) — so the blade doesn't stick out when the plane sits in the cabinet.

::: example
On your first day, when you take a plane in your hand, **first try it on a piece of waste board**. Pull once, twice. You should see a **long, thin shaving** coming off the surface. If the shaving **breaks** or **the plane chatters**, the blade is set wrong or is blunt. Tell the foreman — he'll show you how to set it.
:::

### Tool 4: Hammer

The **hammer** (Polish: **młotek**) is the best-known tool. **Everyone** knows what it looks like. But in a sawmill there are **several types** that differ in function:

- **Engineer's hammer** — universal, for nails.
- **Carpenter's hammer** — with a **claw** on the other side — for **pulling out nails**.
- **Rubber mallet** — for blows that **leave no marks** (when fitting floorboards).

**Safety:**

- Hold the hammer **at the end of the handle** (not near the head) — that gives you **leverage** and less effort.
- **Check the handle** — a wooden handle that is **cracked** can **break** during a blow. The head then flies in an unpredictable direction.
- **Don't use a hammer with a loose head** — one strike and the head will slip off the handle. If you hear "click-click" when you swing, **put the hammer down**.
- After use it goes back **on a hook** in the assigned place.

::: warning
**The most common hammer accident** in a sawmill: a helper holds a nail with the other hand (with fingers) and strikes with the hammer. Holding a nail with **two fingers near the head** ends with a **crushed thumb** in one out of a hundred situations. **The professional way**: hold the nail with a **dolly** (a piece of board with a hole) or with **pliers**. Your fingers **never** sit in the strike zone.
:::

### Tool 5: Rule (folding rule, tape measure)

A **rule** (Polish: **przymiar**, **calówka**, **miarka**) is a tool for **measuring length**. In a sawmill you'll meet:

- **Folding rule** (Polish: **calówka**, wooden, 1 m, folds like an "accordion") — the carpenter's classic tool.
- **Tape measure** (Polish: **taśma metryczna**, a coiled steel tape, 3–8 m) — the most universal.
- **Folding metre stick** (a long metal rod, 2 m) — for longer measurements.

**Safety:**

- The folding rule and tape are **safe** — they don't cut. But **watch the recoiling tape** — if you let go of the end too fast, the tape **rewinds at speed** and **can cut a finger** at the lock.
- **Never use a single rule for a whole shift** without calibration — a wooden folding rule after a year of use can be **off by 1–2 mm per metre**. That's a lot. The foreman will show you how to check.

### Tool 6: Square

A **square** (Polish: **kątownik**) is a tool shaped like the **letter "L"**. It serves to **check a right angle (90°)** and to **draw perpendicular lines** on a board.

Two types:

- **Carpenter's try square** — wooden, for precise joinery.
- **Metal engineer's square** — more durable, for the sawmill.

**Safety:**

- A square doesn't cut. But it **can hit** if it falls from a height (a metal square weighs ½ kg).
- After use it goes **on a hook** or into the toolbox.
- **Check the angle** from time to time — set it against an even board edge and see if both sides touch evenly. A crooked square = **guaranteed errors in the work**.

### Tool 7: Power drill (cordless screwdriver)

A **power drill** (Polish: **wkrętarka**) is an **electric tool** with a motor for **driving screws** and **boring holes**. Today almost always **cordless** (battery), not corded.

A power drill **differs from all the previous tools** — it has a motor. So drill safety is **closer to machine safety** than to hand-tool safety.

**Safety:**

- **Check the bit** before use — that it isn't cracked, that it sits well in the chuck.
- **Your other hand holds the material** — never with a finger near the screw (if the screw slips, the spinning drill hits the finger).
- **Release the trigger** when not in use — a drill lying on the bench with the trigger pressed can start by itself.
- **Set the drill down** motor toward you, bit away from you. **Never** leave it on the edge of a table.

::: tip
A drill battery **drains faster** in cold. If you work in an unheated hall in winter, **keep a spare battery in your pocket** (your body keeps it warm). It's a simple trick that saves time — a full battery lasts a whole shift instead of 2 hours.
:::

### The golden rule of passing tools

Whatever the tool, you pass it to a colleague **by one rule**:

> **Handle-first, blade away from you.**

That means:
- Your hand holds the **blade or non-dangerous part** of the tool.
- The other person takes the **handle**.
- The blade **points to a safe place** — to the ground, to the wall, but **not toward people**.

This rule applies to **all of them**: saw, chisel, plane, hammer, square, drill. After a week's work you do it **automatically** — even at home, when you pass your wife a knife in the kitchen.

### The 5S principle — every tool has its place

In a well-organized sawmill, tools **don't lie just anywhere**. Every tool has:

- **Its place** (a hook, a cabinet, a box).
- **Its mark** (a label, an outline drawn on the wall).
- **Its responsible person** for maintenance (most often the foreman).

After using a tool you do **three things**:

1. **Clean it** (wipe off shavings, dust, glue).
2. **Check it** (whether it's sharp, not cracked, complete).
3. **Put it back** in its place.

**Three things. 30 seconds. Every tool. Always.**

::: warning
**Leaving a tool "anywhere"** isn't just untidiness — it's a **danger**. A helper who didn't find a hammer in the morning **loses 15 minutes** searching. A helper who steps on a left-out chisel **loses a foot**. In Polish sawmills the rule "**tools always to their places**" is part of the **safety regulations**, not just pedantry.
:::

## Practice link

In the second week you'll meet **all seven tools** in the sawmill hall. The foreman will show you **where they hang**, **who is responsible** for them, and **when to use them**. The first task he'll give you will probably be simple: "**take the hammer from the second hook and bring it here**".

Your job: **take it**, **bring it**, **put it back in the same place** after use. That sequence repeated 50 times in the first week **builds your habit**.

After two weeks the foreman will notice: "this new one never forgets to put a tool back". This is the first **trust signal**. The next step will be: "take the drill and screw these screws into this board". That means **you can now be trusted with a powered tool**.

A helper who after a month still "doesn't know where to put a chisel" **does not advance**. A helper who after a week puts every tool back like a soldier after inspection **moves on**.

## Trainer notes

**Pedagogical aim:** to build the **basic tool vocabulary** + teach **two key safety rules** (the blunt tool paradox + the golden passing rule). The trainee doesn't have to operate a plane after the lesson — he has to know **what not to do** and **how to behave**.

**120-minute rhythm:**
- 10 min — short recap of L4 (the 3-step schema).
- 25 min — group reading of the content with a tutor, with special emphasis on the **safety paradox** and the **golden passing rule**.
- 35 min — **practical exercise**: 7 tools brought to the room. Each participant takes one, names it in Polish, shows **how to hold it** and **how to pass it to a colleague handle-first**. Everyone in the group passes them around (chisel, hammer, square). This is **the most important part of the lesson**.
- 15 min — "Key terms" section.
- 25 min — "Check yourself" + a discussion of the 5S principle — has anyone ever experienced someone leaving a tool out and someone else getting hurt.
- 10 min — buffer.

**Helpful materials (essential!):**
- **7 real tools** in the room — essential. Without them the lesson is abstract.
- They can be brought from the plant warehouse or arranged with the foreman.
- **Blade covers** for the saw and chisel — to show what they look like.
- A dolly or block with a hole — to show the correct technique for holding a nail.

**A common trainee mistake:** **underestimating the drill** ("it's just a small machine"). Explain: a drill has a motor and the torque can wrench a finger if a cable / sleeve / long hair is caught. **Every power tool requires the same attention as a big machine.**

**Trainee questions:**
- "And a chainsaw?" — A chainsaw is a **machine**, not a hand tool. A helper **never** operates one — that's done by an operator with the right qualifications. We'll mention it in lesson 6 (sawmill machines).
- "Can I bring my own hammer from home?" — Better not. Every plant tool is **checked** (handle examined, not cracked). Your home tool may look fine but **has no guarantee**. In case of an accident with a private tool, **the responsibility falls on you**.

**Practical recommendation:** during the tool-passing exercise, **require a verbal confirmation**: the trainee passes a hammer, says "**masz**" (you have it), the other one says "**mam**" (I have it), and only then do you let go. This habit **prevents injuries** in a noisy environment where you can't always see the colleague's hand movement.

**Link to the next lesson (m1-w2-l6 "Machines 'from the outside' — saws"):** "Today we learned the **hand tools** the helper works with. Tomorrow we'll look from the outside at **sawmill machines** — the frame saw, the band saw, the circular saw. We won't be learning to operate them (that's not your job), but you'll learn to **recognize them** and to know **where the safe zone ends** for the helper."
