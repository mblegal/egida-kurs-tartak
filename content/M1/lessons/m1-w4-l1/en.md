## Introduction

Welcome to **Week 4**. This is the **last week of Module 1**. Over three weeks we have built the foundation:

- **Week 1 (Safety)**: you learned **why a sawmill is different**, what the hazards are (mechanical, dust, noise, fire), how to protect yourself (PPE, zones), what to do in an accident.
- **Week 2 (Material)**: you learned **what wood is** in the professional sense: log, lumber, species, defects, tools, machines, water in wood.
- **Week 3 (Processes)**: you saw **what you physically do** through a day: handling, stacking, feeding the operator, hand tools, housekeeping, marking.

In Week 4 we add the **final layer**: **organization**. That is, **how all of this is arranged in time, in people, and in paper**.

Because a helper who knows **where the saw stands** (W1), knows **what oak is** (W2), and knows **how to pass a board to the operator** (W3) is only **half a helper**. The other half is:

- knowing **what time** the briefing is,
- knowing **whom to report** a damaged stack to,
- knowing **what document** you sign after training,
- knowing **what words** the foreman shouts when something goes wrong.

This is the **grown-up helper**. Not "just hands that carry boards". An **information node in the plant**: you see, hear, read, report.

In this lesson, the first of W4, we start with the **macro** view: what a **typical day** in a Polish sawmill looks like, hour by hour. Who does what, when. When you have to be where. Whom you greet with "dzień dobry", whom you tell "koniec zmiany" (end of shift).

In later lessons we will go into the **detail**: your workstation (l2), gestures (l3), Polish words (l4), paperwork (l5), reporting incidents (l6), M1 recap (l7), test (l8).

## Objectives

After this lesson:

- You understand that a sawmill runs on a **time structure**: briefing, five-minute safety talk, work, breaks, cleanup, report. This structure **repeats every day**.
- You know the **typical shift hours** at a Polish sawmill (most often 7:00–15:00 or 6:00–14:00) and at what time you must be on site.
- You know **who gives orders**: the foreman for the whole shift, the operator for your specific workstation. In a conflict, the foreman's order prevails.
- You know **whom you report to**: the foreman as the standard route; in an urgent situation, any member of management within reach.
- You understand that the day **starts with motion** (arrival, changing, briefing) and **ends with cleanup and a report**: these are two mandatory brackets that open and close the day.
- You know the five key moments of the day: **morning briefing, five-minute safety talk, first break, lunch break, end-of-shift report**.

## Content

### Why a sawmill runs on a strict rhythm

A sawmill is not an office. In an office you can arrive at 8:15 and no one notices. In a sawmill **the machines start**. The band saw needs a **warm-up** (bearings warming, blade check). The dust extractor needs a **fan start**. The head rig needs a **first log** ready to be cut. If you are not there at 7:00, the whole line **waits** or **runs without a helper** (which breaks safety rules).

That is why sawmills run on a **strict schedule**. You arrive **15 minutes before** the shift begins (standard: shift 7:00–15:00, you at the gate at 6:45). You change clothes. You check your PPE. You go to your workstation. Briefing. Five-minute safety talk. Production starts.

A migrant in the first week often wonders: "Why so early? The shift starts at 7:00." The answer: **at 7:00 the machine is already cutting**. For that to happen, you have to be **changed and ready at 6:55**. Those 15 minutes are **your time**, unpaid, but **required**. That is how it works in every production plant in Poland, not only in sawmills.

::: info
**What if I am 5 minutes late?** In most sawmills, a first lateness is a quick talk ("no advance notice next time"). The second is a verbal warning. The third is written, and after several written warnings the contract can be terminated. **The worst part is not the lateness itself, it is not calling in**. If the bus is late, the car broke down, your child got sick, you **call the foreman**. The foreman will move work around, replace you for the first hour. But silence is what is not forgiven.
:::

### Hour by hour: a typical day

Let us look at **one day of a helper** at a medium-sized Polish sawmill (20–50 people, one shift, pine and spruce lumber). The times are indicative; your specific plant may have its own schedule.

**6:45 – arrival at the plant**. You enter through the gate. The guard sees you in the system. You go to the changing room. You change into work clothes. You put on PPE (steel-toe boots, high-visibility vest; the helmet and hearing protection come later).

**6:55 – at the workstation**. You walk onto the production hall or the yard. You put on the helmet, goggles, hearing protection (if production sounds are already there). You look around your station: what has changed since yesterday? Is there sawdust left from the previous shift? Are your hand tools in place?

**7:00 – morning briefing (5–10 min)**. The foreman gathers the whole shift. He says: what we are cutting today (e.g. "oak, then pine, then oak again"), which orders have priority, who is on which workstation, any new instructions (e.g. "heads up, operator X is on sick leave, Y is running his machine, you pass material to him"). You **listen**. You can **ask a question at the end**. You do not interrupt.

**7:10 – five-minute safety talk**. This item is required by regulations. The foreman (or the safety officer) discusses **one hazard**: "today it is pine dust, remember your masks" or "yesterday at a neighbouring plant there was an accident on a circular saw; reminder: your hand does not cross the fence". Short, concrete, **for that day**. Sometimes they pass around a training record for signature: you **sign it**, because it is the proof you were informed.

**7:15 – production start**. The saw runs. The extractor runs. The forklift runs. The first log goes onto the head rig. You are at your station doing your first task (e.g. "take boards off the return conveyor onto stack number 3"). The **main body of the day** begins.

**9:15 – first break (15 min)**. The shift has a break written into the schedule (Article 134 of the Polish Labour Code: when work lasts at least 6 hours per day, the worker has the right to a 15-minute break counted as working time). You go to the break room. Coffee, a sandwich, a short chat. **Hearing protection and mask come off in the clean area**, not on the hall floor.

**9:30 – back to work**. The second part of the morning. Often there is a **task change**: "up to now you were feeding; now go to the yard and help stack". The foreman or the operator tells you what is next. You **acknowledge** ("I understand", "done") and go.

**12:00 – lunch break (30 min)**. The lunch break is a **different kind of break**; usually it is not counted as working time (in practice, if your shift is 7:00–15:00 with 30 minutes of unpaid lunch, you are home at 15:30, not 15:00). Some plants **count lunch as paid**; it depends on the contract. Check it on your **first day** and do not assume.

**12:30 – back to the workstation**. The second half of the day begins. Often **this half is harder**: you are already tired, and four hours are still ahead. **Watch your PPE**. Many accidents happen in the second half of the day, because a person relaxes.

**14:30 – second break** (if any; some sawmills do not have a second 15-minute break, because the shift adds up to exactly 8 hours).

**15:00 – end of production, but not end of shift**. The last log goes through the rig. The last board lands on the stack. **The machines stop**. But you **keep working**, because the day ends with **cleanup**.

**15:00–15:20 – workstation cleanup**. The sawdust under your feet is swept, waste goes into the bin, tools go back to their place, stickers go back in the rack, markings are verified, the stack is covered with tarp (if that is the rule at your plant). If you are near the head rig, you help the operator clean **his** station.

**15:20–15:30 – end-of-shift report**. The foreman gathers the shift. He asks: "anything happen? any remarks? any surprises?". You say, **plainly**: "the return conveyor jammed twice; I told the operator"; "at yard number 3 I saw the stack was leaning; I wedged it; it needs fixing tomorrow"; "all good". A few sentences. **You do not gossip**, you do not judge other helpers. Only **facts from your workstation**.

**15:30 – end of shift**. Changing room. Change clothes. Exit.

::: tip
**Between breaks, drink water**. The sawmill is hot (the hall plus physical work), and airborne dust dries out your throat. In summer you lose **2–3 litres of sweat** during a shift. If you do not drink, by the end of the day you become less attentive, and a less attentive helper means a higher risk of accident. A 1.5 L bottle at your station, topped up at every break. This is your duty toward yourself.
:::

### Who gives orders, and whom you report to

At a sawmill, not anyone can tell you what to do. **The chain of orders has a structure**, even though in practice it can be invisible (especially for a migrant in the first week).

**First line: the shift foreman**. He is the one who says in the morning: "you go to head rig number 2"; "today you run the circular saw together with Tomek"; "after lunch go to the yard". The foreman **knows the whole shift**, has a plan for the day, and assigns work.

**Second line: the machine operator**. Once you are at your station, the **operator** tells you **what, specifically**: "pass the board", "wait", "go fetch a wedge", "step out of reach, kickback". The operator is responsible for **his machine and your safety at that station**.

**In a conflict, the foreman prevails**. If the operator says "stay and finish the cut", and the foreman comes over and says "go to the yard", **you go to the yard**. And you tell the operator: "the foreman ordered it; I will come back or someone will replace me". The operator understands, because **the foreman ranks above him in the shift hierarchy**.

**Who does NOT give you orders**: another helper, even one in his second year. A colleague from another shift. The yard worker (unless you are helping him at his request, but then it is a **request**, not an order). No "experienced" helper has authority over you. You are **peers** in the hierarchy.

::: warning
**Classic first-week trap**: a senior helper says to a new one: "listen, we do it this way here: go turn on that conveyor, I cannot be bothered". The migrant, wanting to do well, turns it on. Accident. **The migrant is held responsible**; the senior helper explains to the foreman: "I did not tell him to; he did it himself". And formally he is right: **a helper does not give orders to another helper**. Remember: **orders come only from the foreman or the operator**. From another helper it is a **request you can refuse**.
:::

**Whom do you report to?** Standard: the **shift foreman**. He is your "first contact" for every matter: a broken machine, a cut, a problem with a colleague, a question about a document, a question about leave. In an **urgent** situation (life threat, fire, accident), any member of management within reach: foreman, production manager, director, safety officer.

### Three pillars of the day: entry, production, exit

Notice that **every day at the sawmill has the same three-part structure**:

1. **Starting motion** (6:45–7:15): arrival, changing, briefing, five-minute safety talk. **You enter the plant's system**.

2. **Production body** (7:15–15:00 with breaks): you do concrete tasks. **You act inside the system**. Feeding, handling, stacking, ongoing cleanup.

3. **Closing motion** (15:00–15:30): cleanup, tools, report. **You leave the plant's system**.

These are **not three different days**. These are **three parts of the same day**. But each part has **a different mental function**:

- Part 1 is **switching the head on**. From home, from the road, from the bus, from thoughts about the kids, about illness, about the weather, you shift into **sawmill work mode**. PPE, caution, focus.
- Part 2 is **the work itself**. The head is already at work. The body performs what you learned in W2 and W3.
- Part 3 is **switching the head off**. You tidy up, report, close the day. You go home **lighter**, because you left the job finished.

Migrants who run into trouble often skip **part 1 or part 3**. They arrive at the last moment (skipping the head-on switch) and leave in a hurry, leaving mess behind (skipping the head-off switch). **It catches up**. After three months at this pace, a person is burnt out, scattered, and has more small accidents.

**Healthy helper's pattern**: **15 minutes before plus 30 minutes after** is your ritual. Those 45 minutes of unpaid time are an **investment in 40 years of retirement-age health**. No hourly rate can compensate for that.

### Different sawmills, different rhythms

The schedule I described (7:00–15:00) is a **common pattern**, but **not the only one**. You will find plants:

- **With two shifts**: first 6:00–14:00, second 14:00–22:00. Rotation: one week morning, next week afternoon.
- **With three shifts** (less often; only the largest mills): 6:00–14:00, 14:00–22:00, 22:00–6:00. Night is **better paid** (a 20% premium on hourly rate for night hours, under the Labour Code), but more draining.
- **With a "compressed week"**: 10 hours a day Monday to Thursday, Friday off. You gain a long weekend, you lose an even rhythm.
- **With Saturdays**: in some plants Saturday is part of the standard week; in others only when orders surge (overtime, 50–100% premium).

**Your first lesson in a new job**: find out **what rhythm your plant has** and adjust your private life. If you have a child in kindergarten that opens at 6:30, and your shift starts at 6:00, **it will not work**. You have to find an afternoon job or a kindergarten with earlier care. The sawmill **will not shift** to fit you.

::: example
**From real life**: a Ukrainian helper, a sawmill near Warsaw, shift 6:00–14:00. Commute 45 minutes by bus; the bus runs once an hour: 4:45, 5:45, 6:45. The only workable one is 4:45 (arrival 5:30, 30 minutes to spare). For the first month he commuted, he was tired but he coped. Then he was late twice (the bus did not come), and the third time the foreman did not accept it. Warning, then written warning. **The fix**: a change of plant to a sawmill in his home town, shift 7:00–15:00, a 15-minute bike ride. **The same kind of work, a different rhythm, a different life.**
:::

### Your first briefing: what to do, what not to do

The first morning briefing in a new job is the **most stressful moment** of the first week. The foreman speaks Polish quickly, with dialect, throws around names you do not know, talks about orders you do not understand.

**What to do**:

1. **Stand with the crew** (not in the corner, not by the door). The crew standardly forms a half-circle around the foreman.
2. **Look at the foreman** (not at your phone, not at the floor). In Poland, eye contact means respect.
3. **Listen, even if you do not understand everything**. In the first week you will not understand 70% of the content: that is **normal**. What matters is that you understand **your specific task** ("go to station X", "feed operator Y").
4. **Take notes in a pocket pad** (a small notepad in your PPE pocket). Your station of the day, the task, the batch number, the end time. It helps you and it shows the foreman that you are serious.
5. **Ask at the end if you did not understand**: "Proszę pana, nie zrozumiałem, gdzie dzisiaj?" (Excuse me, I did not understand where I am to go today?). There is no shame in that. The shame is in **doing nothing because you did not know**.

**What NOT to do**:

- Do not chat with colleagues during the briefing ("did you see the match last night?").
- Do not look at your phone.
- Do not eat or drink coffee (after the briefing, on the break: fine).
- Do not be late **to the briefing**. Being late to the briefing is worse than being late to the gate, because it is **visible to the whole shift**.

## Summary

Today you learned that the sawmill runs on a **strict rhythm** and that your day has **three parts**:

- **Starting motion** (6:45–7:15): arrival, changing, briefing, five-minute safety talk. You switch your head into sawmill mode.
- **Production body** (7:15–15:00 with breaks): concrete work, orders from the foreman and the operator, two or three breaks, lunch.
- **Closing motion** (15:00–15:30): cleanup, tools, end-of-shift report. You switch your head off, leave lighter.

**Five key moments**: morning briefing, five-minute safety talk, first break, lunch, end-of-shift report. Each has a **clear function** and **its time**.

**Orders** come from the **foreman** (general) and the **operator** (specific, at the station). **Another helper has no authority over you**. In a conflict the foreman prevails.

**Reports** go to the foreman; in an urgent situation, to any member of management within reach.

**15 minutes before plus 30 minutes after** is your personal ritual. Unpaid but necessary. Not overtime; an **investment in your health and career**.

Different sawmills have different rhythms (one shift, two, three, Saturdays or not). **Your responsibility**: in the first week, learn the rhythm of your plant and adjust your private life.

In the next lesson (l2) we will go into the **micro** view: **your workstation**. What you check on it in the morning. What has to be ready before the machines start.

## Kluczowe terminy

- 🕕 **Odprawa poranna** (pl) – morning briefing (en) – reunión matinal / briefing de inicio (es) – ранкова планерка (uk). A whole-shift gathering with the foreman (5–10 min at 7:00). Task assignment, priorities of the day, personnel changes. Attendance is mandatory.

- ⏱️ **Pięciominutowe BHP** (pl) – five-minute safety talk / toolbox talk (en) – charla de seguridad de cinco minutos (es) – п'ятихвилинний інструктаж з безпеки (uk). A short discussion of one hazard of the day. Required by regulations. Often ends with a signature on a training record.

- 👔 **Brygadzista** (pl) – shift foreman / team leader (en) – capataz de turno / jefe de equipo (es) – бригадир зміни (uk). Shift supervisor. Gives general orders, assigns work, receives reports. Your first contact on most matters.

- 🎛️ **Operator** (pl) – machine operator (en) – operario de máquina (es) – оператор машини (uk). A person with authorization operating a machine. Gives specific orders at your station ("pass it", "wait", "step back").

- 🔄 **Zmiana** (pl) – shift (en) – turno (es) – зміна (uk). A work-time unit, most often 8 hours (7:00–15:00, 6:00–14:00, 14:00–22:00, 22:00–6:00). The night shift carries a 20% premium under the Labour Code.

- ☕ **Przerwa 15-minutowa** (pl) – 15-minute break (en) – pausa de 15 minutos (es) – 15-хвилинна перерва (uk). Counted as working time; granted under Article 134 of the Polish Labour Code (work over 6 hours). Standardly once or twice per shift.

- 🍽️ **Przerwa obiadowa** (pl) – lunch break (en) – pausa para comer (es) – обідня перерва (uk). Usually 30 minutes, not counted as working time (though the contract may say otherwise). Check it at your plant on the first day.

- 📋 **Raport końcowy** (pl) – end-of-shift report (en) – reporte de fin de turno (es) – підсумковий звіт зміни (uk). A few sentences to the foreman about what happened at your station. Facts, not gossip. Closes your day.

- 🎒 **Rytuał 15+30** (pl) – "15 before, 30 after" routine (en) – rutina 15+30 (es) – ритуал «15 до, 30 після» (uk). 15 minutes before the shift (changing, PPE) and 30 minutes after (cleanup, report). Unpaid but necessary. An investment in health and career.

- 🌙 **Nocna zmiana** (pl) – night shift (en) – turno de noche (es) – нічна зміна (uk). A shift between 22:00 and 6:00. A 20% premium per night hour under Article 151⁸ of the Polish Labour Code. Physically and mentally more draining.

## Notes for the Trainer

**Pedagogical aim**: Opening of Week 4: introduction of the **organizational** layer. After W1+W2+W3 the learner can name hazards, materials, and actions. Now he learns the **arrangement of time and people** within which those actions play out. This is the **frame** onto which we will pin the following W4 lessons (workstation, communication, documents, reports). The key idea of the week: **"a helper is an information node in the plant, not just hands"**. We plant it here.

**120-minute rhythm**:

- **0–10 min**: opening. Question to the group: "What is happening at the sawmill at 6:55 in the morning?". Collect answers on the board; do not comment. Return to them at the end.
- **10–30 min**: narrative introduction (W1 gave safety, W2 material, W3 processes, W4 gives organization). The image of "grown-up helper = information node". Stress that this is the last week of M1; the test is in a week.
- **30–70 min**: timeline of the day on the board. **Learners draw in their notebooks** together with the trainer. 6:45, 6:55, 7:00, 7:10, 7:15, 9:15, 9:30, 12:00, 12:30, 14:30, 15:00, 15:20, 15:30. At each hour, what happens and what the helper does. Key: part 1/2/3 of the day as mental frames.
- **70–95 min**: order hierarchy. **Act out scenes**: (a) the operator says "pass the board", who is giving the order: the operator (specific); (b) the foreman says "go to the yard" while the operator needs you: the foreman prevails; (c) a senior helper says "turn on the conveyor": NO, this is not an order but a request, you refuse.
- **95–110 min**: the first briefing: what to do, what not to do. The trainer plays the foreman (dialect, fast, with names); learners practise "stand with the group, look, take notes, ask at the end". After the exercise, discuss what they did not understand and why that is **normal**.
- **110–120 min**: key terms to be written down. Short oral quiz: "When is the briefing?", "Whom do you report to?", "Who can tell you to turn a machine on?". Preview of l2 (workstation).

**Common mistake**: learners ask "are those 15 minutes before the shift paid?". **Honest answer**: no, they are not; this is the **labour-market standard in Poland**, not just at sawmills. Comparison from life: "in an office, people also arrive 10 minutes early to take off their coat, start the computer, make coffee. Here it is the same, only instead of coffee it is a helmet." **Do not apologize**, do not promise it will change. This is how it is.

**Second common mistake**: mixing up "request" and "order". The learner thinks that if a senior colleague says something in a confident voice, it is an order. **Correction**: an order comes from a **person in the hierarchy** (foreman; operator at his machine). Everything else is a request. A request can be politely declined: "no, I do not have authorization" or "no, I have to finish my task".

**Practical recommendation**: hand out an A5 card with the timeline of the day (a table: hour | what happens | your role). Two empty columns for the learner's notes after the first real day of work. Let him compare "plan" with "reality". Return to this card in l7 (M1 recap).

**Link to the next lesson**: l2 "Preparing Your Own Workstation". If l1 is the macro (a whole day, a whole shift), l2 is the micro (your one-square-metre space). What you check before the machines start. A checklist: PPE, cleanliness, walkways, lighting, tools, emergency stop. What to do if something is missing.
