## Introduction

Tuesday, day seven of week two. Yesterday Sofía understood **what the number 38% means**. Today she will learn **how to get that number so it is true**. Measuring moisture sounds like "stick something into the wood and read it off". Reality is more interesting: the same board, measured at three spots, gives three different results, all correct at once. The question is **which result is your decision**.

At 7:30 foreman Marek puts two instruments on the table. The first is a **Brookhuis FMC** resistance moisture meter with two pin electrodes and a digital display. The second is a **Merlin HM8-WS25** capacitive moisture meter, the size of a powder compact, with no pins, just a round plate you press against the board. Marek hands Sofía the first one: "Start with the resistance meter. In two months I will give you the capacitive one for export-grade sawn timber, but first you must understand what the two electrodes are for and why you always measure in two places."

This lesson is about **how to get a trustworthy value out of a moisture meter**, and how to recognise situations where even a correctly taken reading tells you something different from what you think.

## Objectives

After this lesson:

1. You understand the **working principle of a resistance moisture meter**: the electrical resistance of wood drops exponentially as moisture rises (from billions of Ω at 6% down to thousands of Ω at 30%).
2. You know how to **drive the electrodes correctly**: perpendicular to the grain, to a depth of 1/4 to 1/3 of the board thickness, from two sides of the board (top and bottom), at two points along its length (near the endgrain and at the centre).
3. You understand the **moisture gradient** in a freshly sawn board: dry endgrain (3 to 8 percentage points lower than the centre), a wet core, sapwood in between. You know why a reading from the endgrain lies.
4. You know the **species correction** and the **temperature correction**: factors built into better moisture meters, and manual corrections in simpler ones (a table from the manual, not "by eye").
5. You know the **physical limits of resistance measurement**: the reliable range is 6 to 30% MC; below 6% the signal is unstable, above 30% the resistance is so low that the electronics "give up".
6. You know **when to reach for a capacitive moisture meter**: export-grade class A sawn timber (no pin holes), veneer board, parquet for underfloor heating. You understand that a capacitive meter reads moisture in a 20 to 30 mm surface layer (shallow), while a resistance meter reads at the point where the pins are driven in (deep).
7. You know how to **record a measurement** so that the foreman and the next shift know: log or board number, species, measurement location, result in %, date, time, your signature.

## Content

### How a resistance moisture meter actually sees water

The principle is simple, even if the numbers are large. **Dry wood is practically an insulator**: an oak board at 6% moisture has a resistance between two electrodes on the order of 10⁹ to 10¹⁰ ohms (billions). **Wet wood is a conductor**: the same board at 30% moisture has a resistance of 10³ to 10⁴ ohms (thousands). The difference is six orders of magnitude, like the difference between a grain of sand and a truck.

The moisture meter passes a small test current (microamps) through the electrodes, measures the resistance, and converts it into a moisture percentage **using a calibration for beech at 20°C** (that is the industry standard, written into PN-EN 13183-2). All other species and temperatures require a correction, more on that in a moment.

::: info
**Why beech, of all species?** Because its electrical properties sit in the middle between pine (soft, resinous) and oak (hard, acidic). Manufacturers chose it as the reference point so that corrections in either direction stay small. But that means that **for pine you subtract about 1 to 2% from the reading, and for oak you add about 1%**. Better moisture meters have a species selector (15 to 30 species built in) and do this automatically.
:::

### Electrodes: which kind, how deep, which direction

The Brookhuis FMC that Sofía is holding has two **pin electrodes** (insulated along the part nearer the handle, uninsulated at the tip). The insulation matters: without it the current would flow through the surface layer (always damper or drier than the interior), falsifying the measurement.

**Driving depth**: for a 50 mm board you drive the pins to **12 to 17 mm** (1/4 to 1/3 of the thickness). For a 25 mm board, to 6 to 8 mm. For a heavy 100 mm timber, to 25 to 30 mm. Rule of thumb: **the tip of the pin sits in the core of the board, not in the surface layer**.

**Direction**: you drive the pins **perpendicular to the grain direction**, never along it. Why? Because along the grain the resistance is 2 to 3 times lower (water "flows" along the wood vessels more easily than across them), so the reading will be inflated by 3 to 5 percentage points.

::: warning
**First-day traps**:
- **Too shallow**: you are reading the surface layer, not the wood. In summer, a board in the sun has a dry crust of 2 to 3 mm and a wet core. A reading from 5 mm deep may show 14%, while the true moisture of that board is 28%.
- **Too deep with a blunt tip**: the pin bends, the contact becomes unreliable, readings jump around. Replace the tips every 200 to 300 measurements (the "spare needles" in the moisture meter case).
- **Driving into a knot or a crack**: a knot has different moisture than clean wood, and a crack conducts air (an insulator). Pick a clean spot 3 to 5 cm away from any knot.
- **Holding the handle bare-handed**: with very wet wood (above 80%), your skin forms a parallel path for the current and pulls the reading down. For measurements that certify export stock, use a meter with an insulated handle and wear cotton gloves.
:::

### Why from two sides of the board: the moisture gradient

This is where the art begins. Sofía takes a 50 mm thick pine board, fresh off the saw, still warm. She measures from the top: **36%**. She flips the board and measures from the bottom: **41%**. Which value is true?

**Both are true. The board has a moisture gradient.**

In freshly sawn, unseasoned wood, water is spread out unevenly:
- **Core** (the centre of the board thickness): the highest moisture, often **3 to 8 percentage points** more than the outer layers.
- **Outer sapwood** (surface and 5 to 10 mm inwards): the lowest moisture, because water evaporates from the surface first.
- **The face lying on the ground or a sticker**: wetter, because gravity pulls water downward and it does not evaporate as fast as from the top.

**Rule**: you measure **from the top and from the bottom**, and take the average. If the difference exceeds 5 percentage points (in Sofía's example: 36% vs 41%, a difference of 5), you record this as "high gradient" and report it to the foreman. This is a sign that the board needs air-seasoning, **it should not go straight into the kiln** (it would crack from the stress difference).

### Why at two points along the length: endgrain vs centre

Wood loses water through every surface, but the **endgrain (cross-section)** sheds water **10 to 12 times faster** than the faces of the board. The reason is anatomical: the vessels and wood tubes are open at the endgrain and closed (or at least strongly obstructed) on the faces.

**Practical consequence**: if you measure moisture **20 cm from the endgrain**, you get a reading that is **2 to 6 percentage points lower** than in the centre of the board. For a 4 m long board:
- Reading 20 cm from the endgrain: 24%
- Reading at the centre (2 m): 29%
- Reading 20 cm from the other endgrain: 23%

Average: 25.3%, **but you base the commercial decision on the centre value (29%)**, because that one represents the real state of the wood. The endgrains are a "shop window", they look nice and tell you very little.

::: tip
**The 20 cm rule**: **never measure closer than 20 cm from the end of a board**. If you must measure a short board (under 1 m), measure in the very middle, and note that it was short, because accuracy is lower in that case.
:::

### Species correction and temperature correction

A modern moisture meter (Brookhuis, Exotek, Delmhorst) has **30 to 50 species curves** built in: you pick "pine" from the menu and the device automatically adjusts the resistance to the pine calibration. Older kit (or the cheapest, under 500 PLN) has only the beech calibration, in which case you use a **correction table** from the user manual.

::: example
**Correction table (averaged values; approximate):**

| Species | Correction at a reading of 15% |
|---|---|
| Pine | -1.5% (reading 15% → actual 13.5%) |
| Spruce | -1.0% |
| Beech (reference) | 0% |
| Oak | +1.0% (reading 15% → actual 16%) |
| Larch | -0.5% |
| Birch | +0.5% |
:::

The **temperature correction** is the second dimension. The calibration is at 20°C. In winter, in a hall at 0 to 5°C, **the wood has a higher resistance at the same moisture, so the meter shows a value lower than the real one**, you have to add 1 to 3 percentage points. In summer at 30°C the resistance is lower, so you subtract 1 to 2 points. Better instruments have a temperature sensor and do this automatically; in cheaper ones you use the table.

Sofía works in a hall heated to 16°C. The temperature correction is small (-0.5 pp). The species correction for pine is -1.5 pp. Her raw reading of 38% becomes, after corrections, **about 36%**. It is still fresh wood, still above the FSP, and the commercial decision does not change. But now Sofía knows that the number is true.

### The physical limit of resistance measurement, and when to reach for capacitive

A resistance moisture meter works reliably in the **6 to 30% MC** range. Outside that range:

- **Below 6%**: the resistance climbs so high (above 10¹¹ Ω) that the meter's electronics lose precision. Readings "jump" between 4% and 8% on the same spot. For over-dried sawn timber (5 to 7%, e.g. parquet for underfloor heating), you use a laboratory moisture meter with oven-drying of a sample (*oven-dry method*, PN-EN 13183-1).
- **Above 30%**: the resistance falls so low that the meter simply shows "30%" for anything between 30% and 100%. A resistance reading on fresh wood (50 to 90%) only tells you "wet", with no specifics. For precision above 30% you use the gravimetric method (weigh wet, dry, weigh dry, calculate, takes 24 hours).

A **capacitive (pinless) moisture meter** works on a different principle: it measures the electrical capacitance of the wood (which depends on the dielectric constant, which rises with moisture). You press a round plate against the face of the board, push it down and read off. There are no pin holes, critical for class A export sawn timber, veneers and display parquet.

::: info
**Practical differences, resistance vs capacitive**:
- **Resistance**: measures at a point (wherever the pin tips are), deep (at the driving depth), accurately (±0.5 pp with corrections). Leaves holes.
- **Capacitive**: measures a surface layer down to 20 to 30 mm, averages over an area of about 10x10 cm, accuracy ±1 to 2 pp. Leaves no holes.

**For a junior's daily work**: you use the resistance meter. The capacitive one you will get when you move up and start measuring premium-client stock.
:::

### How to record a measurement so it makes sense

A number in a notebook with no context is worth nothing. The standard format:

::: example
**Log 14, pine, delivered 04.04, measured 11.04 07:45, Sofía**
- Position A (bottom endgrain, 20 cm from the end, top face): 32%
- Position B (centre of length, top face): 38%
- Position C (centre of length, bottom face): 41%
- Position D (top endgrain, 20 cm from the end, top face): 33%
- **Representative result (average of B+C): 39.5% → after corrections about 37%**
- Note: top/bottom gradient 3 pp, endgrain/centre gradient 5 pp, material is fresh, DO NOT send to the kiln before 2 weeks of air-seasoning.
- Signature: M. Santos
:::

Sofía copies this data into the measurement logbook and signs it. The measurement logbook is a **plant document**, it comes back in m2-w4-l5 (workstation documentation). Without a signature the entry is worthless. With a signature, it becomes part of the quality trail: if the board goes to a customer and comes back with a complaint of "it was too wet", the measurement logbook shows that the sawmill knew and took the decision knowingly.

## Trainer notes

**Leading analogy**: measuring with a moisture meter is like taking a blood pressure reading at the doctor's. One reading, from one arm, on the run, after coffee, is worthless. Three readings at intervals, calm, both arms, gives a real picture. Wood also has "arms" (top and bottom) and a "pulse" (the gradient). This works for trainees of every nationality, because everyone knows what blood pressure is.

**Hands-on in the hall**: this lesson calls for physical contact with a moisture meter. If you have one device for a group of six trainees, rotate every 10 minutes, measuring the same board. Show how different hands give slightly different readings (±0.3 pp, within the instrument's error band). If no physical device is available, use the PDF manual of the Brookhuis FMC or the Delmhorst RDM-3, or manufacturer videos on YouTube.

**Where this lesson comes back**: m2-w4-l1 cut planning (moisture as a parameter for blade selection), m2-w4-l5 documentation (the measurement logbook), m3-w1-l1 kiln drying (measurement as the basis for the drying decision), m3-w2-l5 complaints (the logbook as evidence of due diligence).

**Traps specific to migrant trainees**:
- "One reading is enough, the number is digital": no. A digital figure is an illusion of precision. Take four readings, compute the average.
- Driving pins into a knot (because it is visually the most distinct point of the board): point a finger and say that a knot is not wood, it is a separate structure.
- Confusing the gradient with a measurement error: a 3 to 5 pp difference is information, not a flaw. The foreman wants to see it.
- Writing down a result without a signature, "because no one will check": someone will check, if there is a complaint. A signature is professionalism.

**The Sofía case, to expand**: why does the foreman give her the resistance meter first, and the capacitive one only after two months? Because the resistance meter forces thinking about the structure of the wood (gradient, depth). The capacitive one gives a "comfortable number" and the trainee may never learn why that number is where it is. This is didactic order, not a financial one.

**Polish phonetic note**: "wilgotnościomierz" is one of the longest trade words in Polish. Accept shortening to "miernik", "przyrząd", "higrometr" (strictly, a higrometr is a broader term that also covers air). In print materials keep the full name. In the hall the short forms are normal and do not jar.
