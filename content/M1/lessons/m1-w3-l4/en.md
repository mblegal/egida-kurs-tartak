## Introduction

Fourth lesson of Week 3. Yesterday we dealt with **logs on the yard** — raw material **before cutting**. Today we move on to **sawn timber after cutting** — boards, baulks, beams that have just come off the saw and **need to lie somewhere** before going to the customer or the dry kiln.

It sounds like a simple task: "Stack boards on top of each other." **But it's a trap.** For three reasons:

1. **Wet timber must dry**. Boards just off the saw have **30–60% moisture content** (you remember from Lesson 8, Week 2). If you stack them **tightly, without ventilation** — mould, fungi, blue stain in **3–7 days**. **Timber to be thrown away** for 500–2000 zł of damage.

2. **A badly built stack breaks and collapses**. A timber stack can be **3–4 metres high** and weigh **5–10 tonnes**. If it tips over — **it crushes people nearby** and **wrecks the surroundings** (knocks down neighbouring stacks like dominoes).

3. **Boards deform by themselves**. Wood "works" throughout the drying period. If it's poorly supported, it **bends, twists, cracks** — and becomes **second-grade timber** or **pure scrap**.

Your task as a helper is to **physically build the stack** under the operator's/foreman's supervision. **You don't decide** what goes where — that's the foreman's call, based on species, purpose, order. But you must know the **technique**, because it's **your hands** that place the stickers and lay the boards layer by layer.

In this lesson you'll learn the **three elements of a stack** (bottom bearers, board layers, stickers), **stack orientation** (to the wind, the roof), **sticker technique** (where exactly and how densely) and **five signs of "this stack will collapse"** — the signals when **you walk away and call the foreman**.

## Objectives

After this lesson:

- You understand that **a timber stack = foundation + boards + stickers + roof** — each element has a function.
- You know the **thickness and spacing of stickers**: **20–25 mm thick**, every **30–50 cm** along the board, **the same wood species** as the timber (or harder).
- You know how to **orient the stack** — the longer side **perpendicular to the prevailing winds**, the roof **deflects water away from the stack**.
- You know the rule of **"boards of equal length in one layer"** — don't mix lengths, because it ends in **stack sagging**.
- You can point out **five signs of an unstable stack** — and you know that in those situations **you don't stand next to it**.

## Content

### Three levels of the stack — foundation, body, roof

A timber stack is **not a pile of boards**. It's an **engineered structure** with three distinct parts:

**Level 1: Foundation (bottom bearers).**

Under the bottom layer of boards **there must be bearers** — beams 10 × 10 cm or 15 × 15 cm, laid **perpendicular** to the boards. Spacing: **every 80–100 cm** along the length of the board (for a 4-metre board = 3 bearers; for 6-metre = 4 bearers). Beneath the bearers **hardened ground** (concrete, road slabs, compacted gravel) — NEVER soft soil.

**Why 80–100 cm?** So the board between the bearers **doesn't sag** under the weight of upper layers. Larger spacing = boards bend downwards, ending in **permanent curvature**.

**Level 2: Body of the stack (boards + stickers, layer by layer).**

On the foundation you lay **the first layer of boards** — one next to another, with **small gaps of 2–5 cm** (air has to circulate sideways too, not only lengthwise). **Whole layer from boards of the same length** — do NOT mix 3-metre with 5-metre in one layer.

On the first layer you place **stickers** — **laths 20–25 mm thick**, **30–40 mm wide**, **the same length** as the stack width (usually 1–1.2 m, depending on board arrangement). Stickers lie **perpendicular** to the boards, **exactly above the bottom bearers** (this matters — the load goes **vertically through the whole stack**, not in a **zigzag**).

On the stickers — **the second layer of boards**. Then **another set of stickers**, then **the third layer**. And so on.

**Level 3: Roof (rain cover).**

On top of the stack — **a roof**. Most often: **waste boards** laid at a **slight slope** (3–5° pitch), **overhanging the stack** by **10–20 cm** on each side (so water drips past the stack, not onto its edge). Under the roof boards — **plastic sheet or roofing felt**, so water doesn't seep through gaps.

**Function of the roof**: protection against **rain** (drops fall on the roof, not on the boards), against **sun** (UV rays don't heat the top boards to 50–60°C — because that causes **rapid drying of only the top layer** and **warping**), and against **snow** (the weight of snow spreads over the roof, doesn't load the timber directly).

::: info
**Without a roof**: water gets between layers, stickers grow mould, boards rot in 2–3 weeks. **With a badly built roof (flat, no slope)**: water sits on the roof board, soaks through, same effect. **A roof always has a slope** — minimum 3°.
:::

### Stickers — the heart of the stack

If you have to remember **one thing** from this lesson — remember **stickers**. Without them, the stack **rots**. With them — **it dries evenly**.

**Thickness**: **20–25 mm** (minimum 20). Thinner = too little clearance between layers, air doesn't circulate. Thicker than 30 mm = boards too far apart, stack wobbles.

**Width**: **30–40 mm** (contact width with the board). Narrower = **press into the board below** (marks on the surface, a quality defect). Wider = **unnecessary, waste of material**.

**Material**: **the same species** as the timber in the stack, or **harder** (e.g. oak under a pine stack). Do NOT use soft species (poplar, aspen) as stickers under heavy beech timber — **they get crushed**.

**Length**: **matched to the stack width**. If the stack is 1.2 m wide — stickers 1.2 m. **No overhang**, not **too short**.

**Spacing along the board**: **every 30–50 cm**. For a 4 m board = **8–10 stickers** per layer. For a 6 m board = **12–15 stickers**. Do NOT skimp on stickers — every missing one = **a crooked board**.

**Vertical alignment (IMPORTANT)**: stickers **in successive layers must be in the same position** — one above the other, vertically. Do NOT "offset" them by half, as if building a brick wall. The load must go **in a vertical column** through the whole stack, down to the bottom bearers. If stickers are **offset** — some boards **hang in the air** between stickers, and **sag**.

::: example
**Example of a correct stack** (pine, boards 32 × 150 × 4000 mm):
- Foundation: 4 bearers 10 × 10 × 1200 mm, spacing 100 cm, on concrete slabs.
- First layer: 8 boards, gaps 3 cm, all 4 metres long.
- Stickers: 10 pieces, 22 × 35 × 1200 mm, pine, spacing 40 cm (3 cm from each end of the board, then evenly).
- Second layer: 8 boards, **exactly above the first** (so the boards sit in the same "columns" as the lower ones).
- Next stickers, **vertically above the previous**.
- Stack height: maximum **1.5 m** for the helper (about 20 layers).
- Roof: 6 waste boards on light battens creating a 5° slope.
:::

### Stack orientation — wind, sun, water

**Where to put the stack?** Not "anywhere". There are three dimensions:

**1. Direction of the longer side**: **perpendicular to the prevailing winds**. In Poland those are mainly westerly winds — so **the long side of the stack runs south–north** (or N–S). The wind then **blows through the stickers**, enters one side, leaves the other — that is the **physics of ventilation**. If the stack stands **along the wind** (west–east) — the wind **flows around it**, little air passes through.

**2. Sun exposure**: **avoid direct south**. If you have a choice — place the stack in **half shade** (under a tree, next to a building). Direct sun accelerates **drying of the top layer** disproportionately — lower layers have 40% moisture, upper 12%, and the **stack warps** like a banana.

**3. Ground slope**: **avoid low-lying spots** where water collects after rain. Ideal ground: **a slight rise**, with a natural slope away from the stack.

::: warning
**Don't place the stack close to deciduous trees.** Leaves falling in autumn gather between stickers, **absorb moisture**, **grow mould**. Coniferous are better (needles dry faster), but also not ideal. **The ground under the stack — best clean concrete**.
:::

### The "equal length in one layer" rule

A cardinal mistake: **mixing lengths in one layer**. You have a 3-metre board and a 5-metre board — the temptation says "I'll mix them to fill the layer". **Don't do it.**

Why? Because **stickers** are spaced every 30–50 cm. A 3-metre board sits on 6–10 stickers. A 5-metre board sits on 10–17. **If those two boards are in one layer**, the support points **don't line up** — one board is supported at points A,B,C,D, the other at B,D,F,H. Between them is a **dead zone**, and the board **sags**.

That's why the correct rule is: **one layer = one length**. If you have 3 m and 5 m boards — you build **two separate stacks**.

**Exception**: if the stack is **narrow** (one column of boards) and all boards are at least **length = stack width + 20 cm** — then you can mix lengths, but **put the longer ones on the outside**, shorter in the middle (because the ends of the longer ones overhang and hold something).

### Stack height — when you stop building alone

**The helper helps build the stack up to a height from which he can safely hand over boards**: usually **1.2–1.5 m** (shoulder height). Above that = **passing overhead**, risk of a board dropping on the face or spinal compression.

Above 1.5 m — **a loader with forks works** or **two helpers with a ladder/step**. **You do not climb onto the stack** to throw a board up — the stack is **not a load-bearing structure for a human**, it can collapse.

**Maximum height of a timber stack (standard):** **4–6 metres**. For you as a helper — **1.5 m is the upper limit**. Above — **the loader operator's role**.

::: tip
**Ergonomic tip**: stacking at waist height (80–100 cm) is **healthy**. Above shoulders (1.5 m) = **shoulder and neck strain**. Optimally: pass boards from the source stack at **just above waist height** and receive onto the target stack at **elbow height** (1 m). Less strain, fewer injuries.
:::

### Five signs of "this stack will collapse"

A moment comes when you look at an existing timber stack and something is **off**. Here are **five signs of instability**:

**1. Lateral bulging.** Looking from the side, the stack is **crooked in the middle** — side walls **bulge outwards**. That's a sign that boards inside are **sagging** and layers are **spreading apart**. The stack may collapse **at any moment**.

**2. Stickers sticking out at an angle.** You see stickers **not perpendicular to the boards**, but **at an angle** or **offset**. A badly built stack — weight doesn't go in a vertical column, it **spreads sideways**.

**3. Visible sag in the upper layers.** Boards on top visibly **bend in the middle** (instead of being straight). Below them, boards are **bent even more**. That's a stack designed poorly — too few stickers or wrong material.

**4. Wet patches on the roof or on the roof board.** Water leaks through the roof — below it **boards will mould**. Moisture increases weight **locally** — the stack **tips over** towards the wet side.

**5. Local "running" of boards.** A single board in the layer is **pushed out** 10–20 cm further than the rest. That's a sign **the layer is spreading apart** — the whole stack becomes **horizontally unstable**.

When you see any of these signs — **you don't approach, you don't try to "fix"**. You go to the foreman, show him, say: "This stack looks unstable." The foreman decides: (a) leave it under warning tape until the loader operator comes, (b) unload from the top layer by layer, (c) evacuate the zone around it.

::: warning
**Never push a stack to "straighten" it**. Don't pull at a sticker sticking out. Don't climb onto the stack to "fix the top board". Every **human contact with an unstable stack** is a risk of a **board avalanche**. An 8-tonne stack that collapses — kills everything within 3–4 m around.
:::

### Wet vs dry timber — stack specifics

- **Fresh timber (wet, 30–60% moisture)**: **stickers mandatory**, **spacing 30–40 cm**, **lots of ventilation** (gaps between boards 3–5 cm), **lower stack** (up to 1.5 m), **roof mandatory**, orientation **perpendicular to the wind**. Natural drying time: **6–24 months**, depending on species and thickness.

- **Half-dry timber (15–25% moisture)**: **stickers still mandatory**, **spacing 40–50 cm**, stack **up to 2.5 m**, roof **still mandatory**.

- **Dry timber (<15% moisture, post-kiln)**: **stickers may be omitted** (tight stack, no gaps). **Roof mandatory**, because even dry timber will soak in rain. Height **up to 4 m**.

The helper doesn't decide which stack is which — **the foreman says so**. But **you know how to tell wet from dry** (colour, weight, touch — from Lesson 8 Week 2), and you won't be surprised when the foreman says "tight without stickers" for dry.

## Summary

Today you learned **how to build a stack that dries rather than rots**:

- **Three levels of the stack**: foundation (bearers every 80–100 cm, hardened ground), body (boards + stickers layer by layer), roof (cover with 3–5° slope).
- **Stickers**: **20–25 mm thick**, **30–40 mm wide**, **every 30–50 cm along the board**, **the same species** as the timber, **vertically above the previous ones** (not zigzagged!).
- **Stack orientation**: **longer side perpendicular to the wind** (ventilation through stickers), **avoid direct sun** and **low spots**, **away from deciduous trees**.
- **Equal length in one layer** — do NOT mix 3 m with 5 m. One length = one layer. Different lengths = different stacks.
- **Maximum for a helper**: **1.5 m high**. Above — the loader operator. **You don't climb onto the stack** — it's not load-bearing.
- **Five signs of instability**: lateral bulging, stickers at an angle, sagging boards, wet patches on roof, "running" boards. In those situations — **the foreman, not you**.

A stack you **built well** — stands for **12–24 months** without trouble, dries evenly, the timber comes out **first grade**, the customer pays full price. A stack you **built badly** — cracks in 2 months, timber **second grade** (30–50% value loss) or **scrap**. **Your hands** decide that difference.

## Key terms

- 🧱 **Stos tarcicy** (pl) — timber stack (en) — pila de madera aserrada (es) — штабель пиломатеріалу (uk). 3-level structure: foundation + board layers with stickers + roof. Its function is **drying** (if wet timber) and **storage**.

- 🪵 **Przekładka / sticker** (pl) — sticker / stick (en) — separador / listón (es) — прокладка / стікер (uk). Lath 20–25 × 30–40 mm between board layers. Provides **ventilation** for drying. Without it, timber **rots**.

- 🪨 **Podkład dolny** (pl) — bottom bearer (en) — durmiente inferior (es) — нижня підкладка (uk). 10 × 10 cm beam under the whole stack, perpendicular to boards, spacing 80–100 cm. On hardened ground.

- ☂️ **Daszek** (pl) — roof / cover (en) — tejadillo / cubierta (es) — дашок (uk). Top protection of the stack against rain, sun, snow. Slope 3–5°, overhangs 10–20 cm beyond the stack edges. Sheet under the roof boards.

- 🌬️ **Wentylacja stosu** (pl) — stack ventilation (en) — ventilación de la pila (es) — вентиляція штабеля (uk). Air circulation through stickers. A condition for drying without mould/fungi. That's why the stack stands **perpendicular to the wind**.

- 📏 **Rozstaw przekładek** (pl) — sticker spacing (en) — espaciado de separadores (es) — відстань між прокладками (uk). Every 30–50 cm along the board. Too sparse = boards sag. Too dense = waste of material.

- 🔺 **Pionowa kolumna obciążenia** (pl) — vertical load column (en) — columna vertical de carga (es) — вертикальна колонна навантаження (uk). Sticker-placement rule: one **exactly above** another, not alternating. The load goes **vertically** down to the bottom bearers.

- 📐 **Stos prostopadły do wiatru** (pl) — stack perpendicular to wind (en) — pila perpendicular al viento (es) — штабель перпендикулярно до вітру (uk). Longer side set so that the wind **blows through the stickers** (W–E, not lengthwise).

- 💧 **Mokra tarcica** (pl) — wet / green timber (en) — madera húmeda / verde (es) — сирий пиломатеріал (uk). 30–60% moisture, straight off the saw. **Mandatory stickers + roof + ventilation**. Dries 6–24 months.

- ⚠️ **Wybrzuszenie boczne** (pl) — lateral bulging (en) — abombamiento lateral (es) — бічне випинання (uk). Side walls of the stack bent outwards. A sign of instability — potential collapse. **Foreman**, not you.

## Trainer's notes

**Pedagogical goal**: deliver the **practical mechanics of the stack** so that after the lesson the learner **can build a stack from scratch** (with the foreman's supervision) or **spot errors** in an existing stack. The second skill is more important — someone else's mistake often costs **a life**.

**120-minute rhythm**:
- **0–15 min**: opening — recap of l8 W2 ("wet timber needs ventilation, dry doesn't"), announce l4 as a continuation. Question to the group: "What will happen if you stack boards on top of each other without stickers?" — collect answers. Show a photo of **a rotted stack** and **a well-built stack** — visual difference.
- **15–35 min**: three levels of the stack on the board — **draw a cross-section of the stack**. Bottom bearers → boards → stickers → boards → stickers → roof. Learners draw in their notebooks. Show the **dimensions** (20–25 mm sticker, 30–50 cm spacing).
- **35–60 min**: **sticker practice** — **if possible** on the shop floor, **we build a mini-stack**. Learners work in pairs: bearers, 3 boards bottom layer, stickers, 3 boards top layer. Trainer **corrects**: "too close", "crooked", "offset, align them". If there's no shop floor — wooden models (cm-scale blocks).
- **60–80 min**: **stack orientation** — show a Polish wind map (W–E dominate), explain why N–S. Show **five badly oriented stacks** (along the wind, in too-wet shade, in a low spot) — learners have to find the error.
- **80–100 min**: **five signs of instability** — photos of real stacks (from PIP archives or a sawmill floor). For each photo: the learner says "what they see", the group corrects, the trainer adds. Scenarios: "you notice lateral bulging — what do you do?" Answer: I go to the foreman.
- **100–115 min**: wet vs dry timber — revision from l8 W2, now in the context of stacking. Why a kiln allows a tighter stack. Why the helper still builds a roof.
- **115–120 min**: key terms, **oral quiz** ("What thickness of sticker?"), announcement of l5 (feeding material to the operator — **no more stacking, now transferring**).

**Frequent learner mistake**: "I'll build it tighter so more boards fit in the stack." **Trainer's response**: "Tight = rot. Ten rotting boards is a worse result than five good ones. Stickers are **not to save space**, but **to save quality**. The weight of an empty 22 × 35 × 1200 mm sticker is 0.3 kg — we don't worry about space **for the cosmos**."

**Practical recommendation**: introduce the **"count stickers before the layer" rule**. Before laying a new layer of boards, the learner **counts stickers on the previous one** and checks whether there are **enough** (minimum for the board length). If any are missing — **they add them**, and **then** lay the boards. This simple rule eliminates 80% of sagging errors.

**Link to the next lesson**: l5 is **feeding and receiving material to/from the operator**. You're no longer building a stack — you work in a **production line**, board by board, in the operator's rhythm. Gestures, working zone, synchronisation. That's where you apply the **5-step technique** from l2 (bent knees) at a pace of **50–100 boards per hour**.
