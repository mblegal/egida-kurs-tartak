## Introduction

Wednesday, the eighth day of week 1. Monday gave you the law (Oleh, Rozp. MG 2000 – Polish Ministry of Economy Regulation of 2000 on woodworking machinery safety), Tuesday gave you breath (Andrei, dust and extraction). Today the band saw is approached by **Siarhei, a Belarusian from Hrodna (Grodno), 32 years old**. Siarhei has worked in the sawmill for three years: one year as a helper, two years as a junior band-saw operator. You remember his colleague from M1 week 4 lesson 2, who lost his fingers in half-light near Olsztyn? That was one of the reasons Siarhei ended up here: he watched, he learned, he told himself "that will not catch me". Today he will take a test on what he has learned.

It is 9:40. Siarhei is cutting oak boards, 48 mm thick and 4 meters long. The fourteenth board enters the blade straight, Siarhei feeds it calmly. **After 80 centimeters the band slows, the motor tone drops, the board stalls**. The cutting blade has jammed in the grain of the wood: a bundle of unevenly shrunk oak (so-called "reaction wood") has closed the cut slot onto the band.

Siarhei has two seconds to decide. **The first reaction of an untrained operator**: pull the board out by hand, the motor is still slowly turning, "it will go through in a second". **The correct reaction**: left hand on the STOP mushroom, right hand away from the table. Siarhei presses STOP. The motor stops faster than usual (under load the coast-down is shorter, because the load torque helps brake), **in silence after six seconds**. Siarhei does not touch the board. He does not touch the band. He touches nothing. **He goes to fetch the foreman**.

This lesson describes the five steps Siarhei took and the three traps he did not fall into. They are: **STOP (Polish: STOP) – WAIT (Polish: ZACZEKAJ) – LOCK OUT (Polish: ZAKOTWICZ) – REPORT (Polish: ZGŁOŚ) – INVESTIGATE (Polish: ZBADAJ)**. A ritual of reaction, not a ritual of start (those were lessons 2-6). This closes week 1: you know how to begin the day (lessons 2-5) and how to react when something goes off-plan (lessons 6, 7). Tomorrow in lesson 8 you will learn what to do when something goes tragically wrong (amputation, hemorrhage, the 112 emergency number).

## Objectives

After this lesson:

1. You know the three most common emergency situations on band saws (material jamming, band derailment, breaker trip) and you know how to recognize each.
2. You can execute the five-step reaction protocol **STOP – WAIT – LOCK OUT – REPORT – INVESTIGATE** in under 30 seconds.
3. You understand why **"coast-down under load" is shorter** than coast-down at idle, and why this does not change the rule "never pull anything out by hand before you lock out the power".
4. You know three tools for removing jammed material (wooden push stick, magnetic hook, guard wrench) and you know which to use when.
5. You understand why handling an emergency **without the foreman** is a break of the ritual: four eyes are always better than two, and a signature in the incident log protects you legally.

## Content

### Three emergency situations you will meet in your first year

Not "if", but "when". PIP (the Polish State Labour Inspectorate) statistics for band saws, 2019-2023: **63% of operators in their first year of work experience at least one jam**. This is not a mark of incompetence, it is a mark that you are working on wood, and wood is uneven. The question is not whether you will meet an emergency, but whether you will meet it with a ritual in your hands or with empty hands.

**Situation 1: material jamming (binding)**

The board stalls in the blade. The motor slows, the tone drops from 2800 rpm to 1500, then to zero. Two causes: **reaction wood** (unevenly dried, the cut slot closes onto the band) or **a feeding error** (the board twists to one side, the blade stops in a crooked kerf). The first is the material's fault, the second is the operator's. Both get the same reaction: STOP.

**Situation 2: band derailment**

The band of a band saw runs on two wheels (upper drive wheel, lower tensioning wheel). If tension drops (bad setting, a weld joint that has opened up, worn wheel tread) or the band guide breaks, the band **jumps off the wheel**. You hear a **jerk, metallic slapping, a grinding sound**. The view: a 4-meter piece of band whipping loose inside the guard. The good news is that it is whipping inside the guard, not outside it. Reaction: STOP immediately, the band can snap with further rotation and fly out.

**Situation 3: protective breaker trip**

The motor stops by itself. On the control cabinet the **"RUN" light goes out**, the "FAULT" or "OVERLOAD" light comes on. Three causes: **thermal breaker** (motor overheated, too much load for too long), **residual-current device** (current leakage, insulation damage) or **internal failure** (bearing damage, belt failure). **Never reset the breaker just to "try again"**. The breaker tripped for a reason. Your job is to find the reason, not to bypass it.

### The five-step protocol: STOP → WAIT → LOCK OUT → REPORT → INVESTIGATE

**Step 1: STOP (0-2 seconds)**

Your left hand is already reflexively reaching for the mushroom (the ritual from lesson 3). Press it. That is the end of the cycle. Right hand away from the table, away from the board, away from the band. Do not try to "pull it out quickly, while it is still turning". The residual torque of the spinning wheels is 30-60 Nm in a band saw, which is enough to pull a hand in.

**Step 2: WAIT (2-12 seconds)**

You wait for a full stop. Under load (jamming) the coast-down is shortened: 5-8 seconds instead of 8-12. But do not press "RESET" just because the motor stopped in 5 seconds. **You check by silence**: if you hear no mechanical sound, you may approach. If you hear a quiet "slap" (the band is still drifting on the wheel), keep waiting.

::: info
**Why coast-down under load is shorter**: an electric motor at idle has to brake the rotors plus the wheels plus the band, and only bearing friction helps. Under load (jamming) **the resistance of the load adds to the braking force**, the kinetic energy goes into deforming the wood, and the motor stops in 5-8 s instead of 8-12. This is not a signal that the machine is working well. It is plain physics. The brake test required by § 13 Rozp. MG 2000 is measured at idle from STOP, not under load.
:::

**Step 3: LOCK OUT (12-25 seconds)**

Turn the **main disconnect** to the OFF position. Modern sawmills have a LOTO (Lockout/Tagout) padlock: a padlock with your name on the main disconnect that physically prevents it from being switched on. Only you have the key. If there is no LOTO, use the main disconnect plus a tag reading "MACHINE FAULT, DO NOT START, SIARHEI 9:40" handwritten on a card and hung on the cabinet. **Only now** may you step closer.

::: warning
**Trap 1: the motor is off, but the power on the cabinet is still live**. Someone may accidentally press START. Always main disconnect, always LOTO or tag. Without that, you do not approach the band with tools.
:::

**Step 4: REPORT (25-90 seconds)**

You go to the foreman. You speak in a complete sentence: **"Foreman Marek, jam on band saw no. 2, 9:40, oak board 48 mm. I stopped, I locked out, I am waiting for you."** The foreman notes it in the incident log: time, machine, operator, description. This is your **legal protection** (art. 234 KP – Polish Labour Code, accident reporting duty) and the foundation of the plant's learning.

**Step 5: INVESTIGATE (together, 2-10 minutes)**

With the foreman you return to the machine. **Never alone.** Two pairs of eyes, two heads, two experiences. The foreman judges whether this is a push-stick removal of the board, or a service call. If push-stick: **wooden push stick, not a hand, not a metal wrench** (metal + band = spark = carcinogenic dust). If service: the machine stays under the tag until the part is replaced.

### Three operator tools in an emergency

Every sawmill should have on the tool cabinet three things the operator may use **under foreman supervision**:

- **Wooden push stick** (hardwood, length 40-50 cm, ergonomic grip) – for pushing a jammed board out sideways. You NEVER use your hand.
- **Magnetic hook** (magnet on a 60 cm stick) – for picking up metal filings and metal chips from a damaged band that have fallen under the table.
- **Guard wrench** (a 4 mm torque screwdriver) – for removing the band guard only after LOCKING OUT. Never without lockout, never without the foreman.

::: tip
**The wood rule**: only wood touches the cutting band. Oak wood + band = oak dust (carcinogenic, but slowly). Metal + band = spark + metallic dust + dust fire (fast). It is a thin line, but in a jam it decides whether you leave the situation with a board, or with a fire.
:::

### Case Siarhei: six seconds of coast-down

Let us return to Siarhei at 9:40. He presses STOP, waits for silence (6 seconds under load). He walks to the control cabinet, turns the main disconnect, and hangs a card "MACHINE FAULT, DO NOT START, SIARHEI 9:40". He goes to hall 2, where the foreman is finishing a conversation with a driver. He says: "Mr. Marek, jam on band saw 2, oak board 48 mm, I stopped, I locked out, I am waiting." The foreman looks at his watch: 9:43.

::: example
**The foreman writes in the log**: "9:40 band saw no. 2, jam of oak board 48×200×4000 mm in longitudinal cutting. Operator S. Bichnou. Reaction: STOP-WAIT-LOCK OUT-REPORT-INVESTIGATE. Event with no injury. Board pushed out with wooden push stick under supervision of foreman M. Kowalski. Band undamaged, weld sound. Machine restarted 9:58."

That entry is 18 minutes of work and zero accidents. If Siarhei had pulled the board out by hand at 9:40:05, the band's residual torque might have drawn his hand in. ZUS (Zakład Ubezpieczeń Społecznych, the Polish Social Insurance Institution) statistics 2020-2023: **34% of finger amputations among band-saw operators happened during removal of jammed material without stopping**. Siarhei's five steps are five measures of safety.
:::

## Check yourself

**Question 1**: You are cutting an oak board, the motor slows, the board stalls. You have two seconds to decide. What do you do?

*Good answer*: left hand on STOP, right hand away from the table, wait for silence. Do not try to "pull it out while it is still turning". Full 5-step protocol: STOP, WAIT, LOCK OUT, REPORT, INVESTIGATE.

**Question 2**: The thermal breaker has tripped, the motor has stopped. A colleague says: "Reset it and keep going, clearly it got too hot, it will cool down." What do you do?

*Good answer*: do not reset. The breaker tripped for a reason. You go to the foreman, who will find the reason (overload, short circuit, bearing failure). Reset without diagnosis = a second failure in 10 minutes, potentially with a fire this time.

**Question 3**: You have a jammed board, the foreman is in another hall. How long do you wait for him to come back? Why do you not act alone?

*Good answer*: you wait for the foreman's return, even 15-30 minutes. The reason: two pairs of eyes, a signature in the incident log (art. 234 KP), support in case of a surprise (e.g., the band releases and snaps after the board is pulled). The machine is locked out, nobody will be hurt. 30 minutes of cost vs the cost of a finger = incomparable.

**Question 4**: Why is coast-down under load shorter than at idle? And why does that not mean you can approach sooner?

*Good answer*: the load helps braking (wood resistance + bearing friction). But the band's residual torque (30-60 Nm) lasts several seconds after the motor stops, especially once the load releases. Silence + 3 seconds of reserve = certainty.

## Link to practice

Tomorrow morning before your shift:

1. Check that you have a **wooden push stick** at your machine (the tool cabinet). If not, ask the foreman to buy one – 40 zloty, a lifelong insurance policy for a finger.
2. Check that you know **where the main disconnect of your machine is** (often in the left corner of the control cabinet, a red switch). Check whether you have **LOTO tags**, or at least **paper cards and a marker**, in the cabinet.
3. Practice the 5-step protocol **dry**: STOP, WAIT (count to 10), LOCK OUT, REPORT (say "foreman, failure"), INVESTIGATE. It will take you 45 seconds. Repeat weekly.
4. When the first jam comes: remember Siarhei. Six seconds. Eighteen minutes. Zero accidents. That is your model.

## Trainer notes

This is a **muscle** lesson, not an intellectual one. By lesson 7 the student already knows what a STOP mushroom is (l3), what a checklist is (l4), what Rozp. MG 2000 is (l5), what dust extraction is (l6). Now he has to put it together into a **reaction ritual**. A ritual enters the body through repetition, not through understanding.

I recommend ending the lesson with a **10-minute dry drill**: each student walks into the room and, at a machine mock-up (or a large A2 photograph), performs the 5 steps out loud: "STOP – WAIT – LOCK OUT – REPORT – INVESTIGATE". You count seconds. Under 30 seconds = good. Over 60 = repeat. After a week, students complete it in 15-20 seconds. This is the pattern that stays.

The Siarhei case can be played as a **three-person role-play**: operator (Siarhei), foreman (Marek), driver (distracts the foreman). The operator has to report the failure in a full sentence with all the data: machine, time, description, status. The foreman has to produce the incident log for signature. The driver has to interrupt with "yes but I have to go". Teaching point: **the operator does not report a failure in a hurry. A failure is more important than a courier**.

A last reminder of the week 1 ritual: **check yourself (l2) → check STOP (l3) → check the machine (l4) → check compliance with the law (l5) → check the extraction (l6) → react to a failure with 5 steps (l7)**. Tuesday brings week 2: **daily maintenance**, different subject, the same ritual core.
