## Fault 1 (9:21–9:45): wavy kerf, diagnosis + operator-level repair

### Diagnosis (9:21–9:27, 6 minutes)

**Wavy kerf** (Polish: *falisty rzaz*, EN *wavy kerf*, ES *corte ondulado*, UK *хвилястий пропил*) is a cutting defect in which the surface of the board is not flat but has regular or irregular waviness with an amplitude of 0.5–5 mm. It is not a single smudge or a random scratch, it is a **pattern** that repeats along the length of the cut. It arises from the following causes:

- **A dull blade** (blunted teeth, uneven, cracks at the base) – the most common cause (~55% of all wavy kerfs at EGIDA per Marek's logbook).
- **Too high a feed rate** for the condition of the blade (the blade is fine but the speed is too aggressive, the teeth "skate" instead of cutting).
- **A misaligned guide** (from Adrian's l7 yesterday – gap above 4 mm, the blade has too much lateral play).
- **Incorrect blade tension** (too loose, vibrates during the cut).
- **A defect in the wood** (a hidden branch, a hard knot), but then the waviness is not regular, only local.
- **Worn band wheels** (worn guide grooves) – rare, requires servicing.

Mykoła starts the diagnosis in order from the simplest to the most service-heavy:

**Step 1, blade condition.** Mykoła opens the cover of the upper band wheel (2 bolts, 13 mm spanner, 40 seconds). He does not yet disconnect the power (the blade doesn't move because the engine is in STOP, but for safety he doesn't put fingers between the teeth, only looks). **He checks the blade cut counter:** on the sticker (mounted 2026-06-17 at 7:20, cuts since sharpening **147**). The limit for stellite 38/10 on oak: **usually up to 180–200 cuts**, but 147 is already close to the lower bound. A 10× loupe (in his pocket, standard M3 kit), Mykoła inspects the teeth, 20 seconds:

- Tooth 1 (counted from the weld): **micro-crack at the base**, barely visible, a 1 mm deep hairline.
- Tooth 2: OK.
- Tooth 3: **fully cracked**, the top of the tooth has come off, only a 2/3-height stub remains.
- Teeth 4–8: micro-cracks on 3 out of 5.
- Tooth 9: OK.

**Conclusion: the blade is blunted and has damaged teeth, to be replaced immediately.** 147 cuts is not many, but stellite can be capricious on fresh 35%-moisture oak (oak resin + tannic acid eat the edge faster than dry oak wood). Mykoła closes the cover, walks to the blade cabinet.

**Step 2, check of feed rate and tension** (shortened, because the blade clearly needs replacement, but Mykoła does it for a complete diagnosis):

- **Feed rate**: KDP-001 recorded 60% on the coarse scale (the norm for fresh oak, 32 mm tops: 55–65%). Not too fast.
- **Tension**: gauge at 2350 PSI, within spec (2200–2400 for oak on LT70).
- **Guide**: Mykoła checks the gap with a metal feeler gauge (30 seconds), 2.5 mm front and 3.1 mm back (he set 2.5/3 in the morning, a back gap of 3.1 is acceptable, vibrations after half an hour of work are minor). **Guide OK.**

Diagnosis complete in **6 minutes**: **blade replacement**, remaining parameters OK.

### Repair (9:27–9:45, 18 minutes)

Mykoła performs the **full LT70 blade replacement procedure** (see l2 M3 T3, where Mykoła did it with Wasyl for the first time on his own; today he does it routinely):

1. **Main STOP** (green button, machine at rest, already done).
2. **LOTO** (Lock-Out Tag-Out): Mykoła walks to the main panel, switches off the power, fits padlock number 7 (his personal padlock, red, key in his pocket). All in 45 seconds.
3. **Opens the covers** of the upper and lower band wheels (4 bolts in total).
4. **Releases the tensioner** (lever into LOOSE position, gauge drops 2350 → 0 within 3 seconds).
5. **Takes off the old blade** from the wheels (carefully, work gloves on, because the teeth are sharp even when dull). Places it in the used-blade bag for shipment to the Wood-Mizer Polska sharpening shop (a weekly pickup).
6. **Takes a new blade**: looks in the cabinet, picks **stellite 38/10 no. ST-38-10-S13** (the next reel after A12/A13, the S marking = stellite). Sharpening date 2026-06-12, cut counter 0. Unwraps it from the protective paper.
7. **Mounts the blade** on the wheels (90-second routine), guides opened to 4 mm temporarily (to let the blade pass).
8. **Tensions** to 2350 PSI (lever from LOOSE to TENSIONED, gauge rises in 5 seconds).
9. **Adjusts the guides** to 2.5 mm front, 3 mm back (feeler gauge, tightens).
10. **Aligns** the blade on the wheels (centres it mid-wheel, 90 seconds).
11. **Closes the covers** (2 + 2 bolts).
12. **Removes LOTO** (back to the main panel, padlock 7 unlocked, key in his pocket).
13. **Switches power on.**
14. **Idle run test** at low speed (no wood, 30 seconds, the blade runs evenly, nothing squeaks, nothing vibrates).
15. **Test control cut** on a scrap of pine (does not ruin the good oak): 3 quick passes, inspect the surface. **Clean, no wave.**

Total time: **18 minutes**. Mykoła goes back to oak log 3, continues. At 9:50 he starts the fourth cut on log 3, this time it goes clean, the board surface is smooth.

### Pedagogical commentary: operator alone, zero escalation

Fault 1 was a **typical operator-level fault**: the blade is a consumable item, replacement is routine, takes 18 minutes, requires no one beyond the operator. EGIDA statistics: **~85% of all machine stoppages during a shift are "operator-level faults"**, i.e. ones the operator solves alone within 10–30 minutes without escalation. The most frequent:

- Worn-blade replacement (18–25 min) – **40% of cases**.
- Motor overload alarm reset (5–10 min, engine cools on its own) – **15%**.
- Clearing the sawdust extraction duct when clogged (10–20 min) – **12%**.
- Correcting a misaligned guide (5 min) – **10%**.
- Controller reset after a minor electronic glitch (2–5 min) – **5%**.
- Other minor (3%).

**The remaining 15% of stoppages** are faults that require escalation: to EGIDA maintenance (typically 3–5% of shifts), to the manufacturer's service Wood-Mizer Polska or Serra (1–2%), emergency call-out to a technical rescue team (<0.5%). Today's Fault 2 belongs to this second category.

Vibration amplitude in the handle: fine but noticeable, similar to a vibrating phone.

Mykoła stops the cut (green STOP), not E-STOP (as there's no emergency). The blade slows in 4 seconds. Mykoła stands for 3 seconds, listening. **The engine is cooling at the normal pace, the engine sound = OK.** He puts his fingers on the blade coil (through the glove), **blade tension = OK** (gauge reads 2350). *„Wheel runout,"* Mykoła thinks. Wasyl comes over from sorting, seeing the stop. *„What's going on?"* Mykoła answers slowly in Polish: *„Something with the drive wheel. Do you feel the vibration if you put your hand on it?"* Wasyl puts his hand on the grip, but the blade is still, there is no vibration. Mykoła says: *„No, there's none now, because the engine is off. When the cut was running, there was a vibration. It's the wheel."*

Such an acoustic-tactile signal is the **third sense of the M3 operator** rarely appreciated by beginners: the operator has **sight** (sees wavy kerfs, smoke, oil), **hearing** (hears changes in engine revs, bearings, blade, file), and **touch** (feels vibrations in the handle, panel heat, floor vibrations). Mykoła, over 3 months of independent work, has learned to **hold the handle lightly** during cutting in order to feel vibrations – Damian taught him this in his first week of M3. Without that signal today Mykoła would only have noticed the problem after 4–5 logs (he would have seen vibrating boards), and that would already be a batch with 20% at risk.

### Diagnosis (12:40–12:48, 8 minutes)

**Band wheel runout** (Polish: *bicie koła*, EN *wheel runout*, ES *descentrado del volante*, UK *биття колеса* – in Ukrainian technical usage we adopt *биття*, not *удар*) means that the band wheel (the driving lower or the tensioning upper; on LT70 there are two Ø 550 mm wheels) **does not rotate true, with a vibrating path guiding the blade**. Consequences:

- **The blade vibrates laterally** while the wheels turn, which transmits vibration into the blade itself.
- **Boards come out wavy** (differently than with a wavy kerf – here the wave is not a regular 10–15 cm, but random, depending on the runout amplitude).
- **Wheel bearings wear out faster** (uneven load).
- **Risk of blade breakage** after several hours of work (vibration accumulation, material fatigue).

Causes of wheel runout:

- **Worn wheel bearings** (most common, ~60%).
- **Mechanically deformed wheel** (impact, drop).
- **Bent wheel rim** after years of work.
- **Play on the drive or tensioner shaft.**
- **Worn wheel surface** (blade groove deeper on one side, irregularity).

Mykoła starts the diagnosis after stopping:

**Step 1, LOTO.** Mykoła walks to the main panel, switches off the power, fits padlock 7. Always, when diagnosing a machine with suspected vibration – never touch the wheels without LOTO.

**Step 2, visual inspection of the wheels.** Opens both covers (upper and lower). Takes an LED torch from the cabinet, lights up each wheel. Looks from various angles for 2 minutes.

- **Upper wheel (tensioning)** Ø 550 mm, the surface appears OK, the blade groove is even.
- **Lower wheel (driving)** Ø 550 mm, he looks more closely... **something is off**. When Mykoła turns the wheel by hand (through the glove, 2 full turns), he sees that **the wheel does not rotate perfectly flat** – a slight irregularity. Checks visually: **radial runout about 0.8 mm** (the LT70 DTR norm from Wood-Mizer: below 0.3 mm; above 0.5 mm requires intervention; above 1 mm requires urgent replacement).

**Step 3, auscultation test of the bearings.** Mykoła turns the lower wheel by hand, slowly, through 3 full turns, **listening**. Right ear close to the wheel axle, not close to the blade. **He hears a characteristic "grinding" sound**, as the balls in the bearing roll over an uneven surface. A healthy bearing gives an even "dry hum", a pathological bearing gives a "crunch" or "grinding". Mykoła hears grinding. **Lower wheel bearings worn.**

**Step 4, work-feasibility assessment.** Mykoła takes stock: can I continue cutting?

- Runout 0.8 mm = **above the 0.5 mm limit** (DTR), introduces vibrations into the blade.
- Bearings grinding = **in the process of deteriorating**, not stable, may seize within hours or days.
- Blade breakage on a seized bearing = **potential injury to the operator** (the blade flies onto the platform).
- Boards come out wavy (potential claim).

**Decision: STOP, external escalation, machine LOTO to service.**

### Boundary of M3 operator vs maintenance vs service competence (12:48–12:50)

Mykoła **does not replace the bearing on his own**. Why? The EGIDA internal regulations (reg.wew.02/2024 § 12) and the Wood-Mizer DTR clearly state:

**Replacing a band-wheel bearing** requires:

- **Dismantling the factory casing** (special bolts with a tamper-evident seal, breaking the seal voids the warranty).
- **A hydraulic press** to pull the wheel off the shaft (the wheel is press-fitted, not screwed).
- **Centring the wheel after mounting** to 0.1 mm accuracy (special dial indicator).
- **Static + dynamic balancing** of the new wheel with bearing (on the manufacturer's stand).
- **A trial run test** for 4 hours under load per the WM procedure.

So this is a task for **Wood-Mizer Polska service** (not even EGIDA maintenance, because EGIDA does not have a hydraulic press or a 0.1 mm centring gauge). The M3 operator only does:

- **Initial diagnosis** (Mykoła did it today, 8 minutes).
- **Escalation to the foreman** (call to Marek, Marek to Wood-Mizer).
- **Machine LOTO** (padlock with his number stays on).
- **Entry in KKS-001 + breakdown report.**
- **Migration to another machine** if the shift continues (Mykoła will move to P1 today).

**Competence boundary** is a key concept for the self-reliant M3 operator: you know what you can do, and you know what you **cannot**. Crossing the boundary (e.g. attempting to replace the bearing on your own) means:

- **OHS hazard** (a hydraulic press requires UDT-certified training).
- **Voiding the machine warranty** (tamper-evident seal).
- **Potential damage to the wheel** through incorrect mounting.
- **The operator's legal liability** for the consequences.

Mykoła never thought about replacing the bearing himself. That is the **external boundary** (*granica zewnętrzna*, EN *external boundary*, ES *límite externo*, UK *зовнішня межа*) – the line separating the operator's competence from that of a specialist service. It contrasts with the **internal boundary** (M3 operator vs M2, or M3 vs EGIDA maintenance) – in that case the M3 operator can do some things, maintenance can do others, with partial overlap.

## Table of 7 fault types with the operator / maintenance / service boundary

At EGIDA M3 the standard table (from internal regulation reg.wew.02/2024, annex 3, printed on an A3 poster in the hall on the wall next to P3, updated 02.2026):

| Fault type | Symptom | Operator diagnosis | Competence boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Wavy kerf** | regular 0.5–5 mm wave on the board | blade condition, feed rate, guide | **operator alone** (blade replacement 18 min) |
| **Wheel runout** | 5–15 Hz vibration in the handle, board waves irregularly | visual inspection + bearing listening | **manufacturer's service** (press, centring) |

Today Mykoła ran into **two of these 7 types**: wavy kerf (boundary column **operator alone**) and wheel runout (column **manufacturer's service**). The ability to distinguish between them quickly is **the key diagnostic competence of M3**.

## The three senses of the diagnostic operator

EGIDA M3 works with **three senses** in diagnostics:

### 1. Sight (visual assessment)

The most obvious sense, but it requires a **trained eye**. An M3 operator sees:

- **A wave on the board** (wavy kerf, wheel runout, guide wear).
- **Smoke** (an electric motor never smokes in normal operation, any smoke is a fault).
- **Oil leak** (a puddle on the floor, a drop on a hose).
- **Wear on a blade tooth** (10× loupe, micro-cracks at the base).
- **Wheel rotation irregularity** (visible when turning by hand, if runout is >0.5 mm).
- **Chip colour** (black or burning = engine too hot or blade too tight).

### 2. Hearing (acoustic assessment)

The second sense, but the hardest to put into words. An M3 operator hears:

- **Engine revolutions** in normal work (a steady hum), under overload (a drop, a "groan") or under fault (a clicking, a crack).
- **Wheel bearings** (norm: even hum; pathology: "grinding", "crunch", "squeal").
- **The blade** (norm: quiet, cuts smoothly; pathology: "squeals" on friction, "jumps" on a misaligned guide, "clatters" on a worn weld).
- **Hydraulic pumps** (norm: a quiet buzz; pathology: a loud buzz = air in the system, a squeal = too little oil).

You only learn this by **listening over years**. Mykoła in his first month of M3 did not distinguish "norm" from "pathology". Today he distinguishes within 2 seconds.

### 3. Touch (sensory assessment)

Most often **underestimated by beginners**. An M3 operator feels:

- **Handle vibrations** (frequency and amplitude, like today with wheel runout at 10 Hz).
- **Heat on the control panel** (norm: cold or lukewarm; pathology: warm = electronics fault).
- **Heat on bearing covers** (norm: warm after an hour of work; pathology: hot = seizure imminent).
- **Vibrations in the hall floor** (norm: none; pathology: a knock = machine foundation loose).
- **Draft from the sawdust extractor** (norm: even; pathology: a sudden change = duct blockage).

This third sense often saves one from a catastrophe. Had Mykoła today not felt the wheel runout through the handle, he would only have noticed wavy boards after 4–5 logs, batch cost = several thousand zł. The sense of touch runs ahead of sight by hours to days.

**EGIDA M3 maxim:** *„We hear with the ear, but we do not feel with the hand."* Beginner operators train mostly sight (easiest), hearing somewhat (in time), touch rarely. Today Mykoła deliberately held the handle lightly, "more sensitively". That is a training one goes through after a year of M3 work.

## Key terms

- **Operator-level fault diagnosis** (*diagnostyka usterek operatorska*, EN *operator-level fault diagnosis*, ES *diagnóstico de averías a nivel de operador*, UK *операторська діагностика несправностей*)
- **Wavy kerf** (*falisty rzaz*, EN *wavy kerf*, ES *corte ondulado*, UK *хвилястий пропил*) – a 0.5–5 mm wave on the board surface, regular or irregular
- **Band wheel runout** (*bicie koła taśmowego*, EN *band wheel runout*, ES *descentrado del volante*, UK *биття колеса стрічки*) – irregularity in the rotation of the Ø 550 mm LT70 wheel
- **External boundary** (*granica zewnętrzna*, EN *external boundary*, ES *límite externo*, UK *зовнішня межа*) – the line separating the operator from the manufacturer's service
- **Internal boundary** (*granica wewnętrzna*, EN *internal boundary*, ES *límite interno*, UK *внутрішня межа*) – the line between M3 operator and EGIDA maintenance, with partial overlap
- **Operator-level fault** (*usterka operatorska*, EN *operator-level fault*, ES *avería de nivel operador*, UK *операторська несправність*) – one the operator solves alone within 10–30 minutes
- **Three operator senses: sight, hearing, touch** (*trzy zmysły operatora: wzrok, słuch, dotyk*, EN *three operator senses: sight, hearing, touch*, ES *tres sentidos del operador: vista, oído, tacto*, UK *три чуття оператора: зір, слух, дотик*)
- **Tamper-evident seal** (*plomba antymanipulacyjna*, EN *tamper-evident seal*, ES *precinto antimanipulación*, UK *антиманіпуляційна пломба*) – the manufacturer Wood-Mizer secures the wheel bearings, breaking it voids the warranty
