## Introduction

Andrij came back on Friday from his first solo shift at the bandsaw with his eyes full of splinters. Nothing serious: two tiny splinters under the eyelid, flushed out with saline by the on-duty first-aid officer in five minutes. But it was enough that the next morning Andrij stood at the PPE locker longer than ever in Module 1.

Because in Module 1 his PPE was a **hard hat, high-visibility vest, and toe-capped boots**. Today, as a junior operator, he opens the same locker and sees a second set: **safety goggles, hearing protectors, a respirator mask, operator footwear, and clothing with no loose parts**. Five items that, in a sawmill, mean the difference between a normal day at work and a hospital visit.

This lesson explains why operator PPE is not helper PPE plus one extra item, but a **separate set**. After Module 1 you stood three to five metres from the saw. Today you stand **half a metre from the rotating blade**. The dust that once flew past you now flies **into you**. The noise you once heard from afar now hits your ears **one hundred and six times per second**. The chips that once fell into empty space now fall **at eye level**.

You start the "check – start up – report" ritual from step one: **check yourself**.

## Objectives

After this lesson:

1. You know the five PPE items for a bandsaw and circular saw operator, and how they differ from the helper PPE in Module 1.
2. You understand why an FFP3 mask with oak and beech is not a recommendation but a legal requirement, based on the IARC group 1 classification.
3. You match the attenuation class of hearing protectors (the SNR parameter) to the actual noise of a bandsaw (95-100 dB) and a circular saw (100-110 dB).
4. You know why the ban on gloves near rotating parts weighs more than a hand-protection recommendation, and you know its legal basis in Rozp. MG 2000 (Polish Ministry of Economy Regulation of 14 April 2000 on OSH in woodworking machine operation).
5. You recognise the three loose-clothing traps that ended in amputations in Polish sawmills, and you can eliminate them before your shift starts.

## Content

### Three exposure vectors

In Module 1 you learned that a sawmill has **six hazards** (dust, noise, chips, crushing, fire, transport). As a helper you were exposed to all of them, but to each one **from a distance**. The operator trades distance for closeness. That is the whole difference.

At a bandsaw or circular saw, operator exposure concentrates on **three vectors**:

- **Noise**: a bandsaw runs at 95-100 dB, a circular saw at 100-110 dB. The harmful threshold is 85 dB of daily exposure, the pain threshold is 120 dB. Without hearing protectors, after two hours of work you already have **permanent hearing loss** in the speech frequency band.
- **Dust**: the operator stands in the exhaust stream. Pine and spruce produce irritating dust. Oak and beech produce **IARC group 1 carcinogenic dust** (same class as asbestos and tobacco). Without a mask, after five years of work you have a measurable risk of cancer of the nose and sinuses.
- **Chips**: during a kickback a piece of wood flies out at 30-40 m/s toward the operator. Exit height: **between belt and face level**. Without safety goggles, the first hit to the eye means a trip to the emergency room.

These three vectors translate directly into **the first three PPE items for the operator**.

### Five operator PPE elements

::: warning
**Remember the five items in one fixed order. You put them on from the top down, from face down.**
:::

**1. EN 166 safety goggles**

Class F (object impact at 45 m/s) or B (object impact at 120 m/s). Class F is enough in a sawmill, because chip speed rarely exceeds 45 m/s. The goggles **must also cover the sides of the eyes** (full goggles or safety glasses with side shields), because chips most often enter at a 30-45 degree angle, not head-on.

Your private prescription glasses **do not replace** safety goggles. If you need vision correction, the plant must provide prescription safety glasses or overspecs that fit over your own glasses. Report this to the foreman in your first week.

**2. Hearing protectors with SNR ≥ 25 dB**

SNR (Single Number Rating) is the number of dB by which the protector attenuates the noise reaching the ear. At 100 dB with an SNR 25 dB protector, **75 dB** reaches the ear, below the harmful threshold.

In a sawmill, **earmuff-style protectors** are the standard (more effective than earplugs, easier to take off and put back on during a break). Earplugs are acceptable for short tasks (under 30 minutes). Electronic pass-through protectors are acceptable but more expensive and need a battery check before every shift.

**3. An FFP2 or FFP3 mask**

FFP is the classification for filtering face pieces under PN-EN 149. Three classes:

- FFP1 filters out 80% of particles. **Insufficient** for a sawmill saw operator.
- FFP2 filters out 94% of particles. **The minimum for an operator cutting pine, spruce, and fir**.
- FFP3 filters out 99% of particles. **Mandatory when cutting oak, beech, birch, and alder** (IARC group 1 carcinogens).

Check the expiry date (printed on the mask). A reusable mask once opened has an 8-hour working life. After single-use, throw it away.

**4. S3-class safety footwear**

S3 per PN-EN ISO 20345 means:

- a steel toe cap (protection against crushing by a falling board or log, up to 200 J),
- a puncture-resistant midsole (against nails or spikes in the sole),
- an SRC slip-resistant outsole (highest class),
- water resistance (sawmill workstations are wet after cleaning and near the kiln).

In Module 1 the helper often wears class S1 (no puncture-resistant midsole, no water resistance). The operator needs S3: the workstation is a machine environment with nails (from packaging) and grease spills.

**5. Work clothing with no loose parts**

A long-sleeve shirt with cuffs fastened by a button or hook-and-loop strip. Work trousers without frayed edges. **No neckties, no scarves, no long drawstrings** on a hoodie. Long hair tied back and tucked under a bandana or cap.

::: warning
**Three loose-clothing traps that ended in amputations in Polish sawmills:**

1. A loose shirt cuff pulled into the band, the hand ends up under the blade.
2. A watch strap pulled into a shaft, the arm rotates along with the shaft.
3. A long braid pulled into the shaft of a circular saw, scalping.
:::

### No gloves near rotating parts

This trap always catches a migrant worker by surprise. **In Module 1 gloves were mandatory**. In Module 2, at the bandsaw and circular saw, **gloves are forbidden**. Where does this contradiction come from?

Legal basis: **Regulation of the Minister of Economy of 14 April 2000 on OSH in the operation of woodworking machines**, § 5 para. 3. A glove catches a rotating blade the way a thread catches on a spool. The difference between "catches a string" and "catches a finger" is **one quarter of a second**. An operator who instinctively tries to pull the hand back will not pull it back in time: the glove has already drawn the hand in with the fabric.

At the saw, hands must be **bare, or in anti-vibration gloves with no loose parts**, fitted snugly. When transporting logs and timber between machines, gloves go back on.

::: tip
**Rule of thumb**: you put gloves on when nothing is touching a rotating part. You take them off before you set anything spinning.
:::

### The "check" ritual, starting with yourself

The week's pilot introduced the **check – start up – report** ritual. The first step, "check", begins **with yourself, before you reach the machine**. In Polish plants where foremen run Module 2 consistently, the order is fixed:

1. **PPE locker**: open it, count five items, check the mask expiry date, check the batteries on electronic protectors (if you use them).
2. **Put them on top-down**: goggles, hearing protectors, mask, footwear, clothing fastened.
3. **Touch check**: run your hand along the cuff (fastened?), through your hair (tied back?), over your wrist (watch **removed**).
4. **Report to the foreman**: "Ready to work at station number X." The foreman checks you visually from less than 3 metres and confirms out loud.

Only now do you walk up to the machine. "Start up" comes in lesson 3. "Report" in lesson 6.

::: example
**Andrij's case**: on his first day of Module 2 Andrij skipped step 3 (the touch check). He had his watch on his wrist and did not notice. At the first start-up of the band, the foreman saw it and stopped the machine with the word "stop". Andrij took off the watch and went back to the ritual. Zero disciplinary consequences, because the foreman saw a new operator learning the ritual. But Andrij remembered it for the rest of his career: **the touch check is not a formality**.
:::

::: info
**Regulation of the Minister of Labour and Social Policy of 11 June 2002 on general OSH rules** (§ 39-41) states that the employer provides PPE and trains workers in its use, but **the duty to actually wear PPE lies with the worker**. A worker who starts work without PPE is personally responsible for that (Art. 211(4) of the Polish Labour Code).
:::

## Check yourself

**Question 1.** You are working at a circular saw, cutting an oak board. Which mask do you put on?

A) FFP1, because oak dust only irritates after long exposure.
B) FFP2, because that is the standard for all wood species.
C) **FFP3, because oak is an IARC group 1 carcinogen and requires the highest filtration class.**
D) A surgical mask is enough, as long as I am also wearing hearing protectors.

**Question 2.** Why are you not allowed to wear gloves at a bandsaw?

A) Because gloves reduce the grip on the wood.
B) **Because a glove pulled into the blade drags the hand in a fraction of a second, before you can pull your hand back.**
C) Because gloves cost more than hearing protectors.
D) Because the employer has no obligation to provide them.

**Question 3.** The helper in Module 1 had S1-class footwear. Why does the operator need S3?

A) Because the operator walks more.
B) **Because operator footwear must have a puncture-resistant midsole (nails from packaging) and an SRC slip-resistant outsole (grease spills).**
C) Because S3 is more fashionable.
D) Because S1 wears out faster than S3.

**Reflection**: After work today, open the PPE locker and count five items. If something is missing (for example batteries for electronic protectors, an FFP3 mask, spare goggles), report it to the foreman before the end of the week. Your legal duty is to **use PPE**. The employer's duty is to **provide it**. Between these two duties there is no room for "I'll manage somehow".

## Link to practice

**Tomorrow morning at the sawmill:**

1. **Open the PPE locker** and count the five items in the operator set. Write down in your notebook what is missing. If you have the full set, check the mask expiry dates and the protector batteries.
2. **Walk up to the foreman with the FFP3 mask in your hand** and ask: "What are we cutting today?" If oak, beech, birch, or alder, FFP3 is mandatory. If pine, spruce, or fir, FFP2 is enough, but FFP3 will not hurt.
3. **Run your hand along the cuff, through your hair, and over your wrist** before you start the "start up" ritual. If anything is loose, a watch on your wrist or a lock of hair sticking out, stop before the machine and fix it. **Zero gloves at the saw. No exceptions.**

## Trainer notes

**Lesson accents:**

- The "check – start up – report" ritual returns here as **the refrain of the Module 2 spine**. The "check" step begins with yourself; that is the heart of the lesson. Emphasise: PPE is not an "add-on", it is **the first step of the ritual**.
- Core arc: in Module 1, PPE protected the helper from 3-5 m. In Module 2, PPE protects the operator from 0.5 m. Closeness turns a recommendation into a requirement.
- Andrij continues the pilot's arc. In the pilot he was "alone for the first time". Here he learns to "dress like an operator". In lesson 3 he puts his hand on the STOP. In lesson 4 he runs a checklist. The week closes around a single character.

**Migrant traps to demonstrate physically:**

- In many countries of origin (Ukraine, Colombia, Venezuela, Peru, Bolivia), a cotton glove is often standard for any work with wood ("because of splinters"). The Polish saw operator **does not wear gloves**, by virtue of Rozp. MG 2000 § 5. Physically show what a glove does when pulled into a band (a rag-and-shaft experiment).
- In many countries FFP2 and FFP3 are a recommendation, not a legal duty. In Poland, with oak and beech dust, it is a legal duty based on the IARC classification. Show the IARC label, and the Regulation of the Ministry of Family, Labour and Social Policy of 12 June 2018 on the NDS (occupational exposure limit) for wood dusts.
- A watch is, in many cultures, part of personal identity (a gift from family). Show why taking it off before the shift is not **losing** the gift, but **protecting** the hand. The watch stays in the locker and goes back on after the shift.

**Link to Module 1:**

- Module 1, Week 1, Lesson 4: the 6 PPE items of the helper, gloves included. Show which items come back unchanged (high-vis vest no, hard hat yes at some stations) and which change class (footwear from S1 to S3, PM mask to FFP3).
- Module 1, Week 1, Lesson 7: Art. 211(4) of the Polish Labour Code, "to use personal protective equipment". Remind learners that this is a **worker's duty**, not a courtesy.

**Hard questions that may come up:**

- "Can I wear my own glasses, because the work-issued ones do not fit me?" No, if they do not carry the CE mark and the EN 166 class. Yes, if they do. Ask the foreman to adjust the fit or order a different size.
- "Can I work with pine without a mask, because pine only irritates mildly?" No. FFP2 is the minimum. NDS for hardwood dust: 2 mg/m³, for softwood: 3 mg/m³, but the operator stands in the exhaust stream, where the concentration can be 10-15 mg/m³.
- "Can I take the hearing protectors off for a moment to talk with a colleague?" Yes, but **only outside the machine zone** (more than 3 m). Inside the zone: the hand signals from Module 1, Week 1, Lesson 3.

**Supporting materials:**

- A physical display of the operator PPE locker: 5 items laid out on a table before the lesson.
- An IARC group 1 label (oak, beech, birch, alder): printed A4 on the wall.
- A 10-second video of a glove pulled into a band (from the Polish Labour Inspectorate OSH channel, 2019).
- Rozp. MG 2000 § 5: printed extract, with "ban on gloves near rotating parts" underlined.
