## Introduction

Wednesday, 20.04.2026, 7:15. Yesterday Andrij finished dispatch DP-2026-042 (42 beams on Monday, 3 beams this morning in 50 minutes) and filled in the log ZMI-2026-042-02 for today's short session. Foreman Marek comes in at 7:20 with a new yellow form. "Andrij, here is the next dispatch, but this time something different - an **export client**. Look at the client field". Andrij reads: **DP-2026-045**, client "Bouwhout van Doorn B.V.", Breda, Netherlands, export order BHD-2026-011. Product: English oak, **structural sawn timber 50 × 200 × 3000 mm, strength class C24**, 60 pieces, priority B. Under the product field he sees two extra notes that were not present on Polish dispatches: **"PN-EN 14081-1:2016"** and **"FSC 100% – chain of custody FSC-C012345"**.

"Mr. Marek, what do C24, PN-EN 14081 and FSC mean? I did not see this on Polish dispatches". Marek smiles: "Because this is an **export client**. For him our Polish label 'visual class A' means nothing: he buys timber for a building project where the structural engineer has calculated that the roof must carry 150 kg/m² of snow, and for that he needs timber with a declared load-bearing capacity, not timber that simply 'looks nice'. On top of that the Dutch buyer has customers who ask whether the timber comes from a forest managed under sustainable forest management. The first issue is handled by the PN-EN standard, the second by the FSC certificate. Let us sit down for five minutes and I will explain what these abbreviations mean - and **why** an operator should know them, even though he does not issue them by his own hand".

This lesson does not teach you how to issue certificates or declarations of conformity with a standard - that is the job of the quality department or the dispatcher. It teaches you **how to understand the documents you hold in your hand**. The operator in the brigade reads dispatches, batch labels, export invoices. On each of these documents three types of codes may appear: **PN-EN 1611** (visual grading), **PN-EN 14081** (strength grading), **FSC or PEFC** (chain of custody of timber). Each code means something - without knowing the meaning, you cannot escalate a non-conformity ("the dispatch says FSC but the batch label has no FSC mark - should I ask someone whether this is a mistake?"). Today you are gaining a basic understanding of these three systems.

## Objectives

After this lesson:

1. You understand **PN-EN 1611-1 "Visual grading of softwood by appearance"**: a European standard adopted in Poland, describing four visual quality classes for sawn softwood (**S0, S1, S2, S3, S4**), with criteria based on knots, checks, slope of grain and surface defects. Visual grading is done **manually by a grader with formal qualifications**, not by the saw operator. You see the result of grading - a batch label with the class code.
2. You understand **PN-EN 14081-1 "Structural timber graded for strength"**: a European standard (the same system across the whole EU), defining strength classes from **C14 to C50** for softwood and from **D18 to D70** for hardwood. The number indicates declared bending strength in N/mm² (for example C24 = 24 N/mm²). Strength classification is performed by a **grading machine** (for example an ultrasonic scanner or a dynamometer) or by a qualified visual grader, not by the saw operator.
3. You understand **FSC (Forest Stewardship Council)** and **PEFC (Programme for the Endorsement of Forest Certification)** as two systems of chain-of-custody timber certification: they confirm that the timber comes from a forest managed under sustainable forest management. The chain-of-custody certificate (Chain of Custody, CoC) is **company-level, not product-level** - the whole EGIDA Foundation holds one number **FSC-C012345** or **PEFC/17-43-XXX**, not each batch separately.
4. You can read **a batch label and a dispatch with standard and certificate codes**: you recognise which code is the standard one (PN-EN XXXX-Y) and which is the certificate one (FSC-Cxxxxxx / PEFC/yy-zz-aaa), and you understand the hierarchy (**a batch without a standard code = domestic batch, a batch without a certificate code = non-certified batch**).
5. You know **when to escalate**: if the dispatch requires C24 and FSC and on the yard you see a pack without a strength label or without the FSC mark on the batch label - go straight to the foreman. You do not produce "because it is probably certified" - certification without proof = no certification.

## Content

::: info
**Why standards and certificates concern the operator even though he does not issue them.** The documentation chain of export looks like this: a Dutch client orders 60 C24 FSC beams from a Polish trade intermediary. The intermediary passes the order to the EGIDA sawmill. The EGIDA dispatcher checks the stock of certified oak in class C24 (or orders its production). He writes out a dispatch for the operator. The operator saws the beams, sorts the batches and fills in the log. The grader classifies each beam for strength (visually or by machine) and attaches a strength-class marker. The quality department issues a declaration of conformity with PN-EN 14081-1. The warehouse worker labels the batch with an FSC sticker. Accounting issues an invoice with the company CoC code. The beams leave by truck to the Netherlands. **Every link must match**, otherwise the client files a complaint - not that "the timber is worse than I thought", but that **the paperwork does not match the goods**. A documentation complaint costs the sawmill 2-5 thousand zlotys per batch (working time of the quality department, contractual penalty, return transport). The operator is the first link that physically "sees" the export dispatch - if he notices early that the batch number on the yard has no FSC label, the damage does not travel further.
:::

### PN-EN 1611-1 – Visual grading of softwood

Full name: **PN-EN 1611-1:1999+A1:2003 "Sawn timber – Appearance grading of softwoods – Part 1: European softwoods"**. A European standard (identical across the whole EU), in force since 1999, updated with A1 in 2003. It covers **sawn softwood** (pine, spruce, larch, fir, Douglas fir) intended for uses that are not strength-marked (joinery, packaging, secondary construction boards, non-load-bearing structures).

Four visual classes: **S0, S1, S2, S3, S4** (five in total: S0 the highest, S4 the lowest). Some Polish sawmills additionally use the commercial split **class I / II / III / IV** - this is an older Polish convention (standard PN-D-95022 withdrawn), today used mostly for the domestic market. The classes S0-S4 belong to the export and European market.

Grading criteria (summary from the standard):

- **Knots** - limits for sound intergrown knots (ø in mm relative to the width of the face), unsound knots and loose knots. S0 allows knots up to 1/4 of the board width, S4 has no size limit.
- **Checks** - surface checks (mm of length per running metre), end checks (% of the board width), through checks (controlled in S0 and S1, allowed in S3 and S4).
- **Slope of grain** - maximum deviation in degrees per running metre (S0: 4°/m, S4: no limit).
- **Surface defects** - blunt edges, chip-outs, surface mould, metal stain.
- **Dimensions and accuracy** - dimensional tolerances in line with PN-EN 336.

Grading is done by a **qualified visual grader** (training in line with PN-EN 14081-2, minimum 40 hours of theory + 3 months of supervised practice). The saw operator **does not grade** - the operator sees the result of grading on the batch label in the form of a class code (for example "S2" added to the label "SO-A-260420-012-**S2**"). **Class A from l6 T2** (the EGIDA notation) is not identical to S0 under PN-EN 1611 - the Polish class A is a sawmill convention, S0 is the European standard and corresponds roughly to class A-Premium in the Polish classification. Sorting out these two systems is the duty of the dispatcher and the quality department, not of the operator, but the operator should know that **they exist in parallel**.

### PN-EN 14081-1 – Strength grading for construction

Full name: **PN-EN 14081-1+A1:2019 "Timber structures – Strength graded structural timber with rectangular cross-section – Part 1: General requirements"**. A European standard in force in the EU since 2005, updated with A1 in 2019. It covers **structural timber** - the timber that goes into load-bearing elements of a building (roofs, ceilings, frame walls, load-bearing stairs, timber bridges).

Strength classes for softwood: **C14, C16, C18, C20, C22, C24, C27, C30, C35, C40, C45, C50** (twelve classes, the number = declared bending strength in N/mm²). Strength classes for hardwood: **D18, D24, D30, D35, D40, D50, D60, D70**. C24 is the class **most often used in single-family houses** (roofs, ceilings), which is why it appears in the dispatch DP-2026-045 for the Dutch client.

Strength grading is carried out by **two methods**:

- **Visually, by a qualified strength grader** - a visual assessment based on the limits of standard PN-EN 14081-2: knots, slope of grain, checks, resin pockets, taper, density of annual rings. The grader holds qualifications issued after an 80-hour training + exam. Output: about 2-4 m³/hour per grader.
- **By machine, using a scanner** - a production line with a measuring device (ultrasonic, laser or dynamometric). The board passes through the scanner, a computer calculates the modulus of elasticity and density and classifies the board automatically. Output: 30-80 m³/hour. The EGIDA Foundation has one scanner line in hall 3 (MicroTec Goldeneye 702 scanner).

The batch label of a strength-graded batch carries a **strength-class code** (for example "C24") next to or instead of the visual code (S0-S4). A batch can carry both codes at the same time - for example "C24 / S1" means that the boards are in strength class C24 and at the same time in visual class S1. **Dispatch DP-2026-045** requires only C24, without a specified visual class - because the Dutch structural engineer designs for load-bearing and the timber goes hidden inside the structure. Not every client requires both classifications - the operator checks what is written on the dispatch.

### FSC and PEFC – chain-of-custody certification

**FSC (Forest Stewardship Council)** - an international non-governmental organisation founded in 1993 after the Earth Summit in Rio de Janeiro (1992). It issues two types of certificate: **FSC FM (Forest Management)** for forest owners and managers, and **FSC CoC (Chain of Custody)** for the entire industrial chain from the sawmill to the retail shop. The EGIDA Foundation holds FSC CoC number **FSC-C012345** (a sample number - the real certificate number is shown on company documents). The certificate is renewed every five years and the annual audit is carried out by an independent accredited body (for example NEPCon, SGS, Rainforest Alliance).

**PEFC (Programme for the Endorsement of Forest Certification)** - a European alternative to FSC, founded in 1999 by associations of foresters and forest owners, mostly European. Today the dominant certificate in the Nordic and Central European countries (about 60% of European certified forests are PEFC). The Polish State Forests hold PEFC certification over the whole forest area, and FSC for selected forest districts. The CoC certificate number for a sawmill has the format **PEFC/17-43-XXXX**.

Three possible product markings:

- **FSC 100%** - the whole product comes from an FSC-certified forest.
- **FSC Mix** - a mixture of FSC timber and controlled wood (non-certified but checked against illegal origin and protected species).
- **FSC Recycled** - a product made of recovered wood (waste paper, dismantled pallets).

PEFC analogously carries the labels "**PEFC Certified**" and "**PEFC Certified – from sustainable sources**". For an export contract, the Dutch client usually requires **FSC 100% or PEFC Certified**, and does not accept FSC Mix without prior agreement (because a further customer of the Dutch intermediary may demand full compliance).

**The certificate is company-level, not product-level** - the whole EGIDA Foundation is registered under one CoC number, but this does not mean that every batch from the sawmill is automatically certified. A specific batch is certified **only if the input raw material came from a certified forest** (confirmed by a supplier invoice with the CoC code) and if the batch has not been mixed with non-certified wood at any stage. That is why the EGIDA sawmill has **physically separated storage piles**: a "FSC" pile (fenced, marked with FSC plates), a "PEFC" pile, and a "domestic without certificate" pile. The operator who sees an FSC pack must know which pile it came from - and if he is sawing against an FSC dispatch, the raw material must come from the FSC pile.

On an export dispatch (DP-2026-045) the CoC number appears in the field "Client and certification notes" as **"FSC 100% – FSC-C012345"**. On the batch label after sawing: the code "FSC 100%" and the certificate number are repeated. On the export invoice: the same code + an extra **claim statement** "This product is from FSC 100% sources" (a declaration of origin, obligatory for every export invoice carrying FSC goods).

::: warning
**Three most common operator mistakes with a dispatch that carries standard and certificate codes.** First mistake: **"it is probably certified, since it is going for export"**. Not so - whatever fits, goes for export. A certificate proves origin, not guesswork. If a pack carries no FSC label, the pack is not FSC, even if it is standing "next to" an FSC pack on the yard. Second mistake: **confusing the visual class S0-S4 with the strength class C14-C50**. The visual class describes appearance (knots, checks), the strength class describes load-bearing capacity (N/mm²). A batch can be S0 (very handsome) and C16 (structurally weak) at the same time - because a nice appearance does not imply high density. A construction client buys C24, not S0. Third mistake: **substituting PEFC for FSC**. "We have PEFC, the client ordered FSC, it is basically the same thing, I will make PEFC". No - the end customer (often a retail chain in Western Europe) has its own requirements and some of them accept only FSC (for example the IKEA or Kingfisher chains). Mixing up certificates = rejection of the whole batch on delivery.
:::

::: tip
**Where to look for information about a standard or certificate in the EGIDA sawmill**. Three resources are available to the operator: (1) **the information board in the staff room** - summaries of the most important standards are posted there (PN-EN 1611, PN-EN 14081, PN-EN 336 dimensions, PN-EN 13183 moisture) together with basic class criteria and the sawmill's certificate numbers; (2) **the "Standards and certificates" binder** held by the shift dispatcher - full standard texts in Polish, copies of certificates FSC-C012345 and PEFC/17-43-XXX with their validity period, a list of qualified graders; (3) **the FSC and PEFC websites** - public certificate databases where you can check whether a number is valid (fsc.org/certificates and pefc.org/certificate-database). If a client doubts your certificate, you can show him the entry in the public database - and you can do exactly the same with a supplier's certificate if you doubt the origin of the raw material.
:::

## Key terms

Po polsku / In English / En español / Українською:

- **PN-EN 1611 / PN-EN 1611 / PN-EN 1611 / PN-EN 1611** - European standard for visual grading of softwood; four classes S0-S4 based on knots, checks and slope of grain; grading is done by a qualified visual grader, not by the operator.
- **PN-EN 14081 / PN-EN 14081 / PN-EN 14081 / PN-EN 14081** - European standard for strength grading of structural timber; classes C14-C50 for softwood, D18-D70 for hardwood; classification done visually by a grader or by a scanning machine, not by the saw operator.
- **Klasa wytrzymałościowa C24 / Strength class C24 / Clase de resistencia C24 / Клас міцності C24** - declared bending strength of 24 N/mm²; the class most often used in single-family houses (roofs, ceilings), the European market standard.
- **FSC (Forest Stewardship Council) / FSC (Forest Stewardship Council) / FSC (Forest Stewardship Council) / FSC (Forest Stewardship Council)** - international certificate of sustainable forest management; a company-level certificate (not product-level); the EGIDA Foundation holds FSC CoC number FSC-C012345.
- **PEFC (Programme for the Endorsement of Forest Certification) / PEFC / PEFC / PEFC** - alternative certificate of sustainable forest management, dominant in Central Europe and Scandinavia; in Poland it is issued for the Polish State Forests; it is not interchangeable with FSC in the eyes of the end customer.
- **Chain of Custody (CoC) / Chain of Custody (CoC) / Cadena de custodia (CoC) / Ланцюг постачання (CoC)** - the documentation chain linking a certified forest with the final product; every link must hold its own certificate; breaking the chain (mixing with non-certified wood) invalidates the whole batch.
- **Pole certyfikowane / Certified pile / Pila certificada / Сертифікована ділянка** - a physically separated storage area for certified raw material on the sawmill yard; marked with plates and fenced; moving a pack between the FSC pile and a non-certified pile requires reclassification and a record.

## Check yourself

1. An export dispatch requires C24 FSC. On the yard, on the FSC pile, you see an oak pack with the label "DĘ-A-180420-005" without an FSC code on the label. What do you do: (a) cut it, because the pack stands on the FSC pile, (b) escalate to the foreman - a pack on the FSC pile without an FSC label is a non-conformity, (c) stick an FSC code on it yourself, (d) call the Dutch client?

2. Strength class C24 means: (a) compressive strength 24 N/mm², (b) bending strength 24 N/mm², (c) density 240 kg/m³, (d) board length 24 cm?

3. A batch marked "S1 / C18" - what does it mean: (a) boards in visual class S1 and strength class C18 at the same time, (b) the next class after S0 and before C24, (c) a standard from 2018, (d) the grader's number?

4. The client orders FSC 100%, the sawmill holds a PEFC certificate. Can a batch be shipped with a PEFC marking instead of FSC: (a) yes, it is basically the same thing, (b) yes, if the client agrees, (c) no, the certificates are not interchangeable and they require a separate agreement from the client, (d) yes, if the dispatch does not carry an explicit ban?

5. Who in the EGIDA sawmill classifies timber for strength under PN-EN 14081, and with what equipment (two answers)?

## Link to practice

In the training workshop you will get three exercises. First: recognising codes on five batch labels (you provide a decoding: species, visual class, strength class, certificate status, date). Second: matching dispatches to raw material - you receive three dispatches (a domestic one, an export PN-EN one with a certificate, an export one without a certificate) and three pack labels, and you must pair them up + point out which pair does not match and why. Third: reading an FSC certificate in the original (a copy of the company document from fsc.org) - you must find four pieces of information: CoC number, date of issue, expiry date, certification body. Pass mark: 4 out of 4 pieces correctly identified.

## Trainer notes

This lesson is **the most "theoretical" of the whole week 4** - it does not concern operating a machine or physical work, only understanding documents. Trainees may perceive it as an "academic lecture" and lose attention. It is worth starting with an example of a real batch label from the EGIDA sawmill (an anonymised scan) with a request to decode it - most trainees will see only the species and the class A/B/C/D, they will not notice the strength code or the FSC mark; that is the starting point. The second difficult moment: explaining that the visual class and the strength class are **two different systems** that may be used independently or together. It is worth drawing a 2×2 table: (visual yes/no) × (strength yes/no) = four types of batch - domestic without classes, domestic with visual, export with strength, export with both. The third risk: the trainee decides that "the quality department does all of this, it has nothing to do with me". The reply: the operator is the first link that **physically works with the pack**, and if he does not notice the missing FSC label on the FSC yard, the error passes through four further links before someone catches it - usually during the annual audit check, when losing the certificate for a month costs the sawmill 150-300 thousand zlotys. A practical exercise at the end: the trainer writes four codes on the board (PN-EN 1611, PN-EN 14081, FSC, PEFC) and asks the trainees to say what each of them "tells" about timber - the goal is that after 5 minutes every trainee can tell "how it looks" (visual) apart from "how it holds" (strength) apart from "where it comes from" (certificate).
