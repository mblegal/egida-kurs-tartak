## Introduction

Friday, 2026-04-23, 9:12. Hai is sawing the fourth beam from the FSC oak package (disposition DP-2026-045, which Krzysztof brought in yesterday). The first three beams went cleanly, feed 20 mm/s, ammeter at 15 A (green zone), a Silvertip 51 × 1.07 mm band that snapped yesterday evening and was replaced with a new one this morning. Beam number 4 – a crack. The band enters the wood normally, but after 40 cm a short, dull crack is heard from inside the beam. Not a band break, not a metallic impact, not the classic "jolt" on a knot. Something different. Hai slows the feed to 10 mm/s and finishes the cut. On the table lie the board and its slab – inside the beam you can see **an internal crack perpendicular to the grain, 8 cm long, 3 mm wide**, with a rusty deposit. It looks like an old defect, maybe frost from a few years back, maybe an impact during skidding. You cannot see it from the outside of the beam. This is **a defect that was not in the disposition** – the Dutch customer bought C24, and an internal defect can exclude the board from the strength grade.

Hai stops the saw (STOP, red mushroom button to the stop), writes on the board in pencil "beam 4 – internal crack 8 × 3 mm – 9:15". He goes to the A6 notebook in his pocket: "9:15 b.4 DP-045 internal crack". He pulls out his work phone, opens contacts – Marek Nowak foreman – calls. Four rings, no one answers. Hai remembers: Marek had his weekly meeting with the production manager at 9:00 in the conference room by the office. He will not pick up for another 30–40 minutes. What now? Keep sawing and report at end of shift (like the helper did in M1)? Stand 40 minutes doing nothing? Call the switchboard? Go in person to the conference room? This is **the question this lesson answers**: how, when, and to whom to report a problem when the foreman is unavailable.

## Objectives

After this lesson:

1. You know the **three mandatory reporting situations**: (a) always **before changing a work parameter** (e.g. changing feed outside the EGIDA table, changing the cutting class, disconnecting a guard even briefly), (b) on **suspicion of a raw material defect** not listed in the disposition (internal cracks, false heartwood, rot invisible from the outside), (c) on **an unusual machine sound** not matching the steady repertoire (crack, grinding, high squeal, uneven vibration). You do not report "for convenience" – you always report in these three situations, even when "I am not sure".
2. You know the **4W report format**: **What** (description of the phenomenon, factual) / **Where** (machine, beam number, location) / **When** (time accurate to 5 minutes) / **What I did** (what decision I made independently: STOP, slowdown, continuation with observation). You do not describe a diagnosis ("I think the band is dull") – you describe symptoms.
3. You know the **three urgency categories**: **IMMEDIATE** (fire, accident, dangerous machine, blood, threat to life) – phone or physical presence, no waiting; **END-OF-SHIFT** (doubt about a batch, minor malfunction, disposition inconsistency) – logbook plus verbal handover when the shift changes; **WRITTEN** (organizational suggestion, improvement proposal, long-term observation) – remark form into the box.
4. You know the **EGIDA escalation chain**: operator → foreman → shift supervisor → production manager → director. The operator always reports first to the foreman. If the foreman is unavailable and the matter cannot wait, you skip one level up (to the shift supervisor), and you inform the foreman about the skip at the first opportunity. You do not call the director bypassing three levels, unless it is an "IMMEDIATE" situation.
5. You know **where and when not to report**: you do not call the foreman after hours on a private number (unless emergency), you do not call during meetings (you check the schedule), you do not report by SMS during working hours (only in person, by phone, or in the logbook), you do not write "just in case" emails when the matter requires a conversation. You match the channel to the urgency.

## Content

::: info
**Escalation is a skill, not "sending a message".** The foreman coordinates a shift of 6–10 operators. A well-formulated report = 30 seconds of his attention. A poorly formulated one = 5–10 minutes of clarifying questions. At 10 operators, the difference between good and bad reporting is an hour of the foreman's shift per day. EGIDA 2025 statistics: 38% of reports require clarification, 12% are reported too late, 6% through the wrong channel. Good reporting is the competency that distinguishes a junior ("says whatever comes to mind") from an operator ("speaks so the foreman understands immediately").
:::

### Three mandatory reporting situations

**First: before changing a work parameter.** Cutting parameters at the EGIDA sawmill are written in tables (feed table from l3 T3, band tension table from l8 T2, drying temperature table from l7 T3). The operator **does not change a parameter outside the table** without the foreman's approval. Examples of situations when you want to change a parameter: oak with higher moisture than standard (the table says feed 18 mm/s, you are thinking 15 mm/s), a board with knots larger than average (you want to slow down by 20%), a band after 4 hours (you want to check tension between shifts). Each such change is **a technical decision** – the right to make it belongs to the foreman, not the operator. Reporting before the change takes 30 seconds, Marek's decision takes 10 seconds ("OK, slow down" or "No, stick to the table, I will come by shortly to have a look"). An "after the fact" report = breaking procedure; in the shift logbook you have to note the reason, and in the weekend report of procedure changes the entry goes to the production manager.

**Second: suspicion of a raw material defect not listed in the disposition.** Hai's internal crack is a classic example – the disposition says "oak C24 FSC", nothing about defects. In the yard there is the FSC package, the label says "visual class A", does not mention internal defects (because visual sorting does not detect them). The operator sees the defect only after cutting. What now? Three options: **(a) throw the board into rejects** – no, because disposition DP-2026-045 requires 60 pieces, throwing out = minus 1 piece from the batch, plus the question whether other beams are also defective; **(b) keep sawing and worry in the evening** – no, because if the whole package has internal defects, in a batch of 60 pieces you will hit 5–10 defective ones, and the quality department will raise a complaint; **(c) STOP, report to the foreman, wait for a decision** – yes. Marek will decide: you continue with observation of the remaining beams + reclassification of 5–10 pieces to class B, or the input package is changed, or the whole disposition goes back to the dispatcher with a request for different raw material.

**Third: an unusual machine sound.** The band saw has a steady repertoire of sounds – the motor at 90 dB, the band entering the wood rustles, the end of the beam pops lightly ("clap"), chips are expelled and the board falls. Sounds outside the repertoire are an alert: **a deep crack** (something is cracking in the beam, in the machine, in the guide), **metallic grinding** (the band is rubbing against something, a tooth chipped off), **high squeal** (a bearing is drying up or loosening), **uneven vibration** (the package is poorly balanced, the saw sways while cutting). You do not diagnose the sound yourself – you report the symptom to the foreman. Diagnosis requires experience (Marek, after 18 years, distinguishes 15–20 types of sounds) and often tools (a mechanic with a vibrometer, listening acoustically to a bearing). You give **STOP + 4W**, the foreman decides whether to send the mechanic or whether you can keep sawing with observation.

### The 4W report format

The 4W report is **four sentences**, each sentence answering one question. Not three, not five – four. Order: **What / Where / When / What I did**. Together 30–45 seconds of speech.

**What** – description of the symptom, factual, businesslike, without diagnosis. "I hear an internal crack in the beam, after 40 cm of cutting, one-off, no repeats" = good. "The beam is cracking, probably has a defect" = bad (diagnosis + hesitation). "Something is wrong" = useless.

**Where** – physical location and beam/batch identifier. "Saw 2, beam 4 from package SO-FSC-210420-003, disposition DP-2026-045, about 40 cm from the start of the beam" = good. "At the saw" = bad (which one? which beam?). "In the hall" = useless.

**When** – time accurate to 5 minutes. "9:15" = good. "A moment ago" = bad (5 min or 30 min?). "In the morning" = useless.

**What I did** – your independent decision before you reported. "STOP, mushroom pressed, board on the table, waiting for instructions" = good. "I am sawing on because I do not know" = bad (continuing a raw-material defect is entering risk). "Nothing yet" = bad (you always have to make the first decision – most often STOP).

Hai's full report to Marek would sound like this: "Marek, Hai, saw 2. What: internal crack in the beam, after 40 cm of cutting, one-off. Where: beam 4 from package SO-FSC-210420-003, disposition DP-2026-045. When: 9:15. What I did: STOP, board on the table, I can see an internal crack 8 × 3 mm with a rusty deposit. Waiting for a decision". Length: 25 seconds. Marek knows the situation fully, he can make a decision by phone or come in person.

### Three urgency categories and channel selection

**IMMEDIATE** – a situation threatens life, health, large-scale property, or production is stopped and generates minute-by-minute losses. Examples: fire (any), accident (person injured), a snapped band with machine damage (the machine is down, repair only by service), hydraulic oil leak onto the floor over 2 m² (slip hazard + ecological contact). Channel: **phone + physical presence if possible**. You call the foreman; if he does not answer within 30 seconds – you call the shift supervisor (number on the board by the saw). If there is a fire, you use the manual alarm (buttons on the hall columns) before you call. You physically stay at the station (extinguisher, securing the zone) until someone arrives.

**END-OF-SHIFT** – the situation needs the foreman's attention, but production can continue and the problem is not escalating. Examples: a package with defective parameters (you continue with observation), a minor malfunction (e.g. a broken pusher, you have a spare in the cabinet), doubt about the batch classification. Channel: **shift logbook + verbal at shift handover**. You enter it in the logbook "remarks" field with the time and batch number. At the end of the shift, when Marek comes to collect the logbook, you say verbally "Marek, I entered three things in the remarks field – check, especially package 003". Marek then has the logbook + your voice + the evening time to make a decision for tomorrow.

**WRITTEN** – the situation does not require a decision today, but it is worth putting on record. Examples: organizational suggestion ("a 6 mm Allen key always gets lost somewhere, maybe it should be tied to the saw"), long-term observation ("for 3 months the oak feeds probably ought to be 2 mm/s lower than in the table"), improvement proposal ("an «operator observations» column could be added to the board"). Channel: **remark form to the box in the break room**. An A4 form, 5 fields (date, operator, saw, topic, description), you drop it into a box locked with a key, opened once a month by the production manager. Written response within 30 days. This path is not for urgent situations – if you are in doubt whether it is "written" or "end-of-shift", choose "end-of-shift".

::: warning
**Three crew-operator mistakes in escalation.** First mistake: **reporting through the wrong channel for the urgency**. Example: the machine is down with an oil leak, the operator sends an SMS "Marek, there is a leak, call me". No. A leak = IMMEDIATE = phone + physical presence. An SMS may arrive after 20 minutes, the oil will spread over 10 m², the maintenance crew will spend an additional 2 hours cleaning up. Rule: **phone beats SMS, physical presence beats phone** in IMMEDIATE matters. Second mistake: **a diagnostic rather than symptomatic description**. Example: "Marek, we probably have false heartwood in the package". No. The claim "false heartwood" is a diagnosis the operator does not perform (it requires inspection of cross-section planes, density measurement, sorter assessment). Symptom: "I see brown discoloration inside beam 4 × 2 cm". The foreman will diagnose it himself or call in a sorter. Third mistake: **I will wait until end-of-shift, so as not to disturb**. The operator sees an internal crack at 9:15, reports it in the logbook at 14:00. In 5 hours he has cut 8 more beams, of which 2 had similar cracks – a batch of 60 pieces now has 3 rejects instead of a potential 1. Rule: **when in doubt, you report immediately**. The foreman would rather have 3 reports too fast than 1 report too late.
:::

### The escalation chain and when to shorten it

EGIDA has five levels: **operator → foreman → shift supervisor → production manager → director**. Each level up has broader responsibility and less time. The foreman (Marek) coordinates a shift of 8 people, the shift supervisor (Piotr Czerwiński, 45 years old, 22 years of service) coordinates 3 crews (24 operators), the production manager (Anna Kwiatkowska, 50 years old) is responsible for all EGIDA sawmill production, the director is responsible for strategy and finance. The higher up, the longer the decision horizon and the less time for minor matters.

**Single-step rule**: the operator always reports to the foreman, not to the shift supervisor. Even if the operator personally knows supervisor Piotr, he does not call Piotr bypassing Marek – that introduces chaos in the decision chain. Marek will hear about the problem from Piotr, after the problem has already "passed" without his input, and next time the operator loses trust ("why is he going around me?").

**Exception – a one-level skip**: the foreman is unavailable and the matter cannot wait. Then the operator calls the shift supervisor. The rule of **a one-level skip** – never straight to the director bypassing three. After the conversation with the supervisor you inform the foreman at the first opportunity: "Marek, I could not reach you this morning, I reported a crack in beam 4 to Piotr. Piotr told me to continue with observation. Now you have the context for the end of the day". Marek does not take offense at the skip – he takes offense at **not being informed about the skip**.

**Absolute exception – a life and health situation**: if the operator sees someone drowning, injured, the hall on fire, or something happening that in 5 minutes will threaten a life – **you call the first available person**, even if it is the director, even if it is 112. No escalation chain rules are more important than a human life. At the EGIDA sawmill there is a poster with numbers: foreman mobile + hall alarm (pager) + switchboard + 112. In a hazard situation you call in sequence until someone answers. The escalation chain is for normal communication, not for critical situations.

### Where and when not to report

Three "do-not-report" rules that operators break most often:

- **Do not call the foreman during meetings**. Marek's meeting schedule hangs on his office door (updated Monday morning for the whole week). Monday 8:00–8:45 briefing with the manager, Wednesday 14:00–15:00 coordination with the second-shift foremen, Friday 9:00–10:00 meeting with the production manager. If the matter is not IMMEDIATE, you wait until the end of the meeting. If it is IMMEDIATE, you call the supervisor (level skip).
- **Do not call after working hours**. Marek ends his shift at 15:00. His private number is on the board, but **only for emergency situations** (fire, serious accident, theft). Normal matters wait until the morning. A crew operator who calls in the evening "because I forgot to write it in the logbook" gets a reprimand.
- **Do not write email during working hours**. Email is for "WRITTEN" matters (not urgent, can wait a week). During working hours you use the phone or physical presence. Email during working hours = ignoring the urgency of the matter. Exception: an email with an attachment (photo of the defect, scan of a document) as a supplement to a phone call.

::: tip
**How to behave when the foreman is unavailable – a decision map.** Three steps in 2 minutes: **step 1** – you classify the urgency (IMMEDIATE / END-OF-SHIFT / WRITTEN); **step 2** – if IMMEDIATE, you call the shift supervisor (number on the saw board), then you inform the foreman by SMS "I had to report to Piotr, call me back"; if END-OF-SHIFT, you enter it in the logbook and wait for the end of the shift to speak verbally; if WRITTEN, a form to the box; **step 3** – you go back to work, doing what you decided in your "4W" as "What I did" (usually STOP and observation). **Three questions you do NOT ask other operators during escalation**: "should I even report this?" (yes, you always report in mandatory situations), "will Marek be angry?" (no, a good escalation is a compliment), "am I overreacting?" (better one report too many than one too few). Other operators are not the foreman – they will not give you the right answer because they do not have the context of the disposition and priority.
:::

::: example
**Hai's case – escalation at 9:15 with an internal crack in the beam.** Marek does not answer (meeting until 10:00, schedule on the board). Hai classifies the urgency: the crack does not threaten life, but disposition DP-2026-045 is 60 pieces, and losses grow with every beam. He decides IMMEDIATE, calls shift supervisor Piotr Czerwiński (number on the saw board). Piotr answers after 2 rings. Hai gives 4W in 45 seconds: "What: internal crack in beam 4 from package SO-FSC-210420-003, after 40 cm of cutting, I see an internal crack 8 × 3 mm with a rusty deposit. Where: DP-2026-045, oak C24 FSC. When: 9:15. What I did: STOP, waiting. Marek is in a meeting, that is why I am calling you". Piotr: "Good. Do not saw on. I am coming down in 10 minutes. Send an SMS to Marek about the skip". Hai writes: "Marek, 9:15 internal crack beam 4 package 003, I had to report to Piotr because of the meeting. Piotr is on his way". At 9:25 Piotr checks beams 1–3 among the offcuts, beam 2 has a similar discoloration. Piotr decides: package SO-FSC-003 goes back to the FSC yard for quarantine, we take SO-FSC-002. At 10:05 Marek calls back, Hai reports in 40 seconds, Marek: "You did well". Three things done well: IMMEDIATE classification (growing losses), skip with SMS (transparency), 4W format (Piotr understood right away).
:::

## Key terms

Po polsku / In English / En español / Українською:

- **Format 4W: Co/Gdzie/Kiedy/Co zrobiłem / 4W format: What/Where/When/What I did / Formato 4W: Qué/Dónde/Cuándo/Qué hice / Формат 4W: Що/Де/Коли/Що я зробив** – a four-sentence operator report, 30–45 seconds; factual symptom description + location + time + independent decision; no diagnosis.
- **Pilność NATYCHMIAST / IMMEDIATE urgency / Urgencia INMEDIATA / Терміновість НЕГАЙНО** – report category for situations threatening life, health, or generating large minute-by-minute losses; channel phone + physical presence; a level skip in the escalation chain is permitted.
- **Pilność KONIEC ZMIANY / END-OF-SHIFT urgency / Urgencia FIN DE TURNO / Терміновість КІНЕЦЬ ЗМІНИ** – category for situations requiring the foreman's attention but not escalating; channel shift logbook + verbal conversation at handover.
- **Pilność NA PIŚMIE / WRITTEN urgency / Urgencia POR ESCRITO / Терміновість ПИСЬМОВО** – category for organizational suggestions and long-term observations; channel remark form to the box; response within 30 days.
- **Łańcuch eskalacji / Escalation chain / Cadena de escalado / Ланцюг ескалації** – EGIDA's five levels: operator, foreman, shift supervisor, production manager, director; the operator always reports to the foreman, a one-level skip is permitted when the foreman is unavailable and the matter cannot wait.
- **Przeskok szczebla z transparentnością / Skip-level with transparency / Salto de nivel con transparencia / Перескок рівня з прозорістю** – reporting to the shift supervisor bypassing the foreman + immediately informing the foreman by SMS or in the logbook, so the foreman does not learn about the matter from his superior.
- **Opis objawowy kontra diagnostyczny / Symptomatic vs diagnostic description / Descripción sintomática vs diagnóstica / Симптоматичний vs діагностичний опис** – the operator reports a symptom ("I hear a crack", "I see brown discoloration"), not a diagnosis ("dull band", "false heartwood"); diagnosis requires experience and tools, the operator has neither.

## Check yourself

1. You hear an unusual crack while cutting a beam. What do you report to the foreman: (a) "the band is probably going dull", (b) "there is some kind of problem", (c) "I hear an internal crack in the beam, after 40 cm of cutting, one-off, STOP pressed, waiting", (d) "the beam has cracked"?

2. The foreman is in a meeting until 10:00, you have an internal crack at 9:15 and growing losses on the batch. How do you classify it: (a) END-OF-SHIFT, because the machine is not down, (b) IMMEDIATE, because losses are growing, and you call the shift supervisor with an SMS to the foreman, (c) WRITTEN, form to the box, (d) you wait until 10:00?

3. You have a suggestion to change the oak feed table (it should be 18, not 20 mm/s). How do you report it: (a) phone to the foreman immediately, (b) SMS after working hours, (c) remark form to the box in the break room, (d) you tell the other operators?

4. Marek does not answer five rings. You check the schedule – meeting until 10:00. The matter: a minor pusher malfunction (you have a spare). What do you do: (a) you call supervisor Piotr, (b) you replace the pusher and enter it in the logbook at end-of-shift, (c) you saw on without the pusher, (d) you stand and wait until 10:00?

5. A fellow operator asks "won't Marek be offended that you reported the crack to Piotr?". What do you answer: (a) "probably yes, next time I will wait it out", (b) "a good escalation is a compliment, and I informed Marek by SMS about the skip", (c) "I do not know, we will see", (d) "I always report only to Marek"?

## Link to practice

At the training facility you will get three exercises. First: **five reporting scenarios** – you get five cards describing situations (band break, oil leak 2 m², internal crack in a beam, suggestion about the board, forgotten logbook entry). In each scenario you indicate the urgency category (IMMEDIATE / END-OF-SHIFT / WRITTEN) and the channel (phone / SMS / logbook / form). Threshold: 4 out of 5 correct. Second: **formulating a 4W report** – the trainer describes a situation to you in 20 seconds ("the saw has been vibrating unevenly for 15 minutes, earlier it was normal, yesterday you replaced the band"), your task is to speak a 4W in 30–45 seconds. Five different situations. Scoring: any 4W containing all four elements (what/where/when/what I did) without diagnosis = pass. Threshold: 4 out of 5. Third: **escalation decision map** – on a sheet of paper you draw a flow chart "reporting situation" → questions → urgency classification → channel → level skip if needed. The trainer checks completeness (minimum six fields: start, urgency, channel, foreman available, shift supervisor if not, SMS to foreman about the skip).

## Trainer notes

This lesson is **the hardest in terms of competency in Block 2 T4** – it teaches a soft communication skill that cannot be tested by a "step-by-step" procedure. Migrant learners often come from cultures with different escalation customs: in some cultures "do not disturb the superior" is so strong that the operator would rather ruin a batch of 60 pieces than call during Marek's meeting; in other cultures "you call the top" is the default ("why call the foreman, when the director is the decision-maker?"). The trainer must **ground the escalation culture in the concrete EGIDA practice**, not in an abstract "rule".

Three typical learner difficulties:

1. **Fear of "disturbing"**. Learners from East Asia (Vietnam, Philippines) often have the cultural pattern "I do not report until I absolutely must". The trainer shows that **the foreman loses MORE on a late report than on an early one**. The math: 1 minute of conversation at 9:15 vs 5 hours of a defective batch reported at 14:00 = a 300x cost difference for the sawmill. Exercise: each learner tells a situation in which they "did not report" in a previous job/life, and we analyze what would have happened if they had reported right away.
2. **Diagnostic rather than symptomatic description**. Learners with experience in another industry (construction, mechanics) sometimes "like to diagnose" – "it is probably the gearbox". The trainer consistently corrects: **symptom, not diagnosis**. Exercise: the trainer describes a situation, the learner answers with a "symptom" (verbs: I see, I hear, I smell, I feel a vibration). Diagnosis is already a different role.
3. **Skip without transparency**. Learners understand that you can skip to the shift supervisor but forget about the SMS to the foreman. A foreman who finds out about the skip after the fact is frustrated ("why did I not know?"). The trainer teaches: **skip + immediate SMS**. Exercise: simulation, the learner calls the "supervisor" (trainer), right afterwards sends an SMS to the "foreman" (second trainer) with the message "I had to skip, details in the 4W below".

Final exercise (25 minutes): **full simulation of three situations**. The trainer plays the foreman, the shift supervisor, the dispatcher. The learner gets three cards (oil leak 2 m², internal crack in a beam, organizational suggestion); for each: classify the urgency, choose the channel, formulate the 4W, execute the call/SMS/form. Scoring: correct classification 3/3, correct channel 3/3, 4W in 2/3 phone situations, transparent skip in the situation with the foreman unavailable. Failure = an extra day of simulation before conversations on the real hall.
