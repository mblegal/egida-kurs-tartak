## Introduction

Yesterday you learned about hazards you **can see**: moving parts, kickback, falling weight. Today we talk about two hazards you **cannot see** so easily: **wood dust** and **noise**.

Both are "silent". They do not show you that damage has already been done. Dust settles in the lungs — nothing hurts. Noise damages the ear — you do not know when. The bill comes years later: respiratory disease or hearing loss. A worker who did not wear a mask and hearing protection often finds out about the damage when it is already too late.

This lesson will teach you to **recognize the situation in which a mask and hearing protection are mandatory** and which equipment fits which task. Your 40 years of retirement health depend on decisions you make today — 25 years in advance.

## Objectives

After this lesson:

- You understand why wood dust (especially oak and beech) is a long-term health hazard.
- You know when you must put on a **dust mask** and which type (FFP2, FFP3).
- You know the noise thresholds (85 dB) and when **hearing protection** is mandatory.
- You understand that **local exhaust ventilation** (extraction) is more important than a mask — it is the baseline, not the add-on.

## Content

### Wood dust — where it comes from and what it does

When a saw, planer, or sander works on wood, **wood dust** is created. The finest particles (below 10 micrometres) do not settle fast — they hang in the air for hours. You breathe them in for the whole shift. You cannot see them against the light as well as large chips, but it is these fine particles that go deepest into the lungs.

**Short-term** — cough, blocked nose, watery eyes, skin irritation. This clears up after the shift.

**Long-term** — occupational asthma, chronic sinusitis, lung fibrosis, and with some species of wood — **cancers**.

::: info
The European Union, in Directive 2017/2398 (amended by Directive 2019/130), classified dust from **hardwoods** (including oak, beech, walnut, maple, mahogany) as a **category 1A carcinogen** — a substance proven to cause cancer in humans. The most common occupational cancer in carpenters and sawmill workers working without protection is **sinonasal adenocarcinoma**. Latency: 20–40 years.
:::

### Quantitative limits — OEL

Poland has an **OEL** (Occupational Exposure Limit) for wood dust in the air at the workstation. Since 2023 the limits are strict:

- **Dust from oak and beech**: 2 mg/m³ (8-hour time-weighted average).
- **Dust from other wood**: 3 mg/m³.
- **Total dust**: 4 mg/m³.

These numbers are less than the dust you raise by tapping a slipper on the table. The employer measures dust concentration with instruments and must **keep it below the limit**. If they do not — you report to PIP, not to the foreman.

### Dust mask — when and which

A **dust mask** is a piece of PPE that traps dust particles before they reach the lungs. Three classes:

- **FFP1** — very basic (about 80% efficiency). **Too weak for a sawmill.**
- **FFP2** — standard (about 94%). The minimum for wood dust no finer than pine/spruce.
- **FFP3** — best (about 99%). **Mandatory for oak and beech**, for sanding, and for a planer in a room without ventilation.

::: warning
A mask only works when it is **fitted to your face**. A few days of stubble lets dust through the side of the mask. If you want to be protected — shave your beard along the edge of the mask, or use a half-mask with a sealing gasket. A mask worn "just because" does not protect you — it only soothes the employer's conscience.
:::

### Local exhaust ventilation — more important than a mask

The best mask is one you do not need to wear. **Local exhaust ventilation** (extraction at the machine) pulls dust out **at the source**, before it reaches your nose. This is the primary protection. The mask is only **the second line of defence**.

Every large machine in a sawmill should have an extraction hose connected to a filter. When you see the extraction is not working (hose disconnected, extractor motor off, filter full), report to the foreman. **Do not work at the machine without working extraction**, even if you have a mask.

### Noise — how it destroys hearing

When a saw cuts, the air vibrates at a frequency so high that it penetrates the inner ear and **breaks microscopic hearing cells**. Human hearing cells **do not grow back**. Destroyed = destroyed forever.

**Decibels (dB)** is the unit of noise. A logarithmic scale — every 10 dB means a 10× stronger sound:

- 30 dB — a quiet bedroom.
- 60 dB — a conversation.
- 85 dB — **the threshold for mandatory hearing protection** (EU Directive 2003/10/EC).
- 90–100 dB — a typical sawmill saw running.
- 110 dB — a saw cutting hardwood, a large planer.
- 130 dB — a jet taking off. The pain threshold.

### Hearing protection — earmuffs and earplugs

Two types:

- **Earmuffs** — cover the whole ear. Attenuation about 25–30 dB. More comfortable, harder to lose. Quick to put on — good when you take them off often. Worse in heat (ears sweat).
- **Earplugs (ear inserts)** — foam or silicone. Attenuation about 15–25 dB. Cheaper, lighter, easier to fit under a hard hat. Require correct insertion — a badly inserted plug gives weak protection.

::: tip
When you are on a floor where a saw is running, **wear hearing protection for the whole shift**. Do not take it off "just for a moment to say something". Most hearing damage happens in short moments without protection — after 1 second the ear does not "recover".
:::

### Long-term effects — what you will not see right away

- **After 1–2 years**: loss of hearing in the high tones. Someone whispers behind you — you do not hear.
- **After 5 years**: trouble understanding speech in a loud place (restaurant, party).
- **After 15–20 years**: 30–50% hearing loss. A hearing aid becomes a necessity.

::: example
From a PIP report: a sawmill worker employed for 25 years. He did not wear hearing protection consistently — "because in my youth it wasn't needed". Current hearing loss: 50 dB in both ears. He cannot talk to his grandchildren without a hearing aid. The same damage would have arisen with consistent hearing protection after 80 years, not 25.
:::

## Check yourself

1. You are working near a saw that cuts oak. Which mask do you put on?
   - A) FFP1 — that's enough, the dust is coarse.
   - B) FFP3 — oak is a carcinogen, protection must be at the highest level.
   - C) A mask is not necessary because I have extraction.

   → **B**

2. At what noise level **must** you have hearing protection on?
   - A) From 100 dB (when the ears start to hurt).
   - B) From 85 dB (the mandatory threshold under EU Directive 2003/10).
   - C) From 70 dB, always, regardless of the floor.

   → **B**

3. You see the extraction at a planer is not working — motor off. You have an FFP2 mask. What do you do?
   - A) I work — the mask protects me.
   - B) I report to the foreman and do not work at that machine without working extraction.
   - C) I turn the extraction on myself.

   → **B**

## Link to practice

When you walk onto the floor on your first day, the foreman will take you to the **PPE cabinet** (a dedicated locker or rack) and show you your masks and hearing protection. You will notice something interesting: on the shelf there will be different masks — FFP2 and FFP3 — marked on the packaging. The foreman will show you the workstation card on which it is written which type of mask fits which task. This is a document you **have to be able to read** — not only for yourself, but to correct a colleague who reaches for the wrong type.

At the end of the shift you throw the disposable mask in the bin and put the earmuffs in your personal box. Every worker has their own protection — no borrowing, no swapping.

## Trainer notes

**Pedagogical goal:** the learner understands that **silent damage exists** — this is a turning point that changes their attitude to PPE for life. The hardest learners are those who "never had a problem" after a year of working without a mask. For them you need visualization: a photo of a trachea with dust, GUS statistics on diseases in retired sawmill workers.

**120-minute rhythm:** 10 min revision of lesson 2, 20 min "wood dust" (with a visual experiment — fine dust in a torch beam shows how little is needed for the whole room to be "polluted"), 15 min on masks with a hands-on demo of FFP2 vs FFP3 (touch, fit), 20 min on noise with a decibel-meter demo (if available — otherwise a phone app), 15 min on hearing protection (the learner tries to insert foam earplugs correctly — almost nobody gets it right the first time), 15 min on long-term effects (photos, PIP data), 15 min on terms and self-check, 10 min of recap.

**"Dust in the beam" experiment:** darken the room, turn on a torch, clap a glove against a glove. The dust shows. In a sawmill there is a thousand times more — only you work in bright light and do not see it. The experiment takes 30 seconds and stays in memory.

**Common mistake:** "a mask is uncomfortable, so I will not wear it". Trainer's reply: "Sure, it is uncomfortable. Your lungs in retirement will also be uncomfortable — only that one you cannot change anymore. 30 minutes of discomfort vs. 30 years of discomfort. Your choice."
