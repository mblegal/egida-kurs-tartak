## Content

### Why two different standards for appearance and strength

Wood is a construction material used in two different functional contexts:

- **Aesthetic context:** a visible board (flooring, panelling, windows, furniture). The client buys **appearance**: ring uniformity, no falling knots, no visible cracks, surface smoothness. Mechanical strength is secondary, because the board is not loaded with large forces.

- **Structural context:** a board hidden in a load-bearing construction (floor beam, roof rafter, column). The client buys **load capacity**: the ability to carry load without fracture, without excessive deflection, without loss of stability. Appearance is secondary (a beam in a floor is concealed by the flooring above and the ceiling below).

For the two contexts two different sets of criteria are needed. Visual grading (PN-EN 1611-1) optimises for appearance; strength grading (PN-EN 14081) optimises for load capacity. A board can have a **high strength class** (C24) but a **low visual class** (G2-3, because it has falling knots, blue stain, visible cracks) if its knots are sound and located outside the critical bending zone. And the other way round: a board can have a **high visual class** (G2-0, without knots, without cracks, smooth) but a **low strength class** (C16, because it comes from a fast-growing tree with wide rings and low density).

::: info
In a Polish sawmill the combined class marking is written on the end of the board as a two-part code: **G2-2/C24** means "visual class G2-2, strength class C24". Typical combinations in Poland: G2-2/C24 (most common, the standard), G2-1/C24 (better appearance, standard strength), G2-3/C24 (worse appearance, good load capacity, for concealed structures), G2-0/C30 (premium on both counts, rare, mainly export). The building client looks at the second part (C), the furniture client at the first (G).
:::

### Four C classes under PN-EN 14081

PN-EN 14081 is a family of five standards (PN-EN 14081-1 to PN-EN 14081-5):

- **PN-EN 14081-1:** General requirements for strength grading of structural timber.
- **PN-EN 14081-2:** Machine grading (MSR), requirements for machines, calibration, personnel.
- **PN-EN 14081-3:** Visual grading, requirements for graders and criteria.
- **PN-EN 14081-4:** Machine grading, verification of correctness.
- **PN-EN 14081-5:** Requirements for pre-graded timber (for grading on a drying line).

The strength classes are common to both methods (visual and machine) and are defined in the **related standard PN-EN 338** (Structural timber, strength classes). PN-EN 338 lists **twelve C classes** (from C14 to C50) plus eight D classes for hardwood (from D18 to D70). In Poland four classes dominate for softwood:

**Class C16:**
- Bending strength (MOR): 16 N/mm² (characteristic, 5th percentile).
- Modulus of elasticity (MOE) along the grain: 8 000 N/mm² on average.
- Characteristic density: 310 kg/m³ (at 12% moisture).
- **Use:** economy roof trusses (budget detached houses), shuttering planks, temporary structures, export pallets.
- **Share of Polish production**: about 15-20%.

**Class C24 (the Polish standard):**
- MOR: 24 N/mm².
- MOE: 11 000 N/mm² on average.
- Density: 350 kg/m³.
- **Use:** residential floors (detached and terraced houses), standard roof trusses, balcony structures, structural stairs.
- **Share**: 50-55% (the most common class in a Polish sawmill, the majority of construction orders).

**Class C30:**
- MOR: 30 N/mm².
- MOE: 12 000 N/mm².
- Density: 380 kg/m³.
- **Use:** more heavily loaded floors (multi-family buildings, production halls with light cladding), load-bearing elements in modular structures, point-loaded beams.
- **Share**: about 15-20%.

**Class C35:**
- MOR: 35 N/mm².
- MOE: 13 000 N/mm².
- Density: 400 kg/m³.
- **Use:** glued-laminated structural elements (lamellas for GL24h, GL28h), high-load structures (timber bridges, sports halls, exhibition elements), beams over 6 m long without intermediate support.
- **Share**: 5-8%.

**Above C35:** rare in a Polish sawmill (the wood must come from dense habitats, older, often larch or imported Scandinavian pine). EGIDA does not routinely issue classes above C35.

::: tip
A building client saying "I want C24" does not always know that C24 is a designation in N/mm² of bending strength. For him "C24" is the old "*KS* class" (*konstrukcja standardowa*, standard construction) from the former Polish standard PN-82/D-94021 (withdrawn in 2006). For older building foremen "*KS*" means "what goes into the floor of a normal house". The new code C24 means exactly the same thing in practice. The operator and grader at EGIDA sometimes meet clients who dictate "*klasa KS*", "*klasa IIa*", "*klasa budowlana*" (building class). It is the grader's task to translate this into the correct C code under PN-EN 14081. Most often *KS* = C24, *IIa* = C16, *Ia* = C30.
:::

### Three mechanical parameters of wood

**Parameter 1: bending strength (MOR, Modulus of Rupture).**

The maximum stress that wood will withstand in bending before it breaks. Measured in N/mm² (newtons per square millimetre, equivalent to megapascals MPa). Measurement procedure: a wood specimen 50 × 50 × 950 mm is placed on two supports 900 mm apart, a vertical load is applied in the middle, and the breaking force is measured. MOR value = the maximum stress at the moment of fracture.

Polish softwood has MOR in the range 20-70 N/mm² depending on species and habitat. Scots pine averages 40 N/mm² (for sound wood, without defects, density 420 kg/m³ at 12% moisture). Spruce averages 35 N/mm². Larch up to 70 N/mm². But these are average values for small defect-free specimens. In sawmill practice, where we grade 4-metre boards with knots and cracks, we apply **characteristic** values (5th percentile, that is the threshold below which only 5% of boards in the population may fall). For ordinary spruce of class C24 the characteristic value is precisely 24 N/mm² (for a knot-free zone it would be 35-40 N/mm², but knots lower the guaranteed value).

**Parameter 2: modulus of elasticity (MOE, Modulus of Elasticity).**

A measure of the **stiffness** of wood: how much the board deflects under load before it fractures. Wood with a high MOE is **stiff** (deflects little); wood with a low MOE is **elastic** (deflects a lot).

The MOE of Polish pine is about 11 000-14 000 N/mm² (for sound wood at 12% moisture). Spruce 10 000-13 000 N/mm². Larch 13 000-16 000 N/mm². Class C24 requires an MOE of 11 000 N/mm² on average (a mean population value, not a characteristic one).

Why does MOE matter, alongside MOR? Because **deflection of a beam under load** (rather than fracture) is often the critical criterion in a structure. A floor that deflects by 10 mm over a 4 m span creaks, the ceiling cracks, the approach to doors is spoilt. The Polish Technical Conditions of Construction (*Warunki Techniczne*, WT) set the maximum permissible deflection at 1/300 of the beam length (for residential floor beams). For a 4 m beam = 13 mm of deflection at most. A C24 beam with an MOE of 11 000 N/mm² meets this under typical floor loads (2-3 kN/m²). A C16 beam with an MOE of 8000 N/mm² does not (deflection too large).

**Parameter 3: characteristic density.**

The mass of wood per unit volume, in kg/m³, at the standard 12% moisture content. Denser wood = more material per unit volume = more strength and stiffness. Density correlates with the C class: C16 about 310 kg/m³, C24 about 350, C30 about 380, C35 about 400.

How density increases: (a) with the age of the tree (older trees produce denser wood), (b) with harsher growing conditions (a tree on sandy soil, slow-growing, has denser wood than one on fertile soil; hence pine from the Tucholskie Forests is denser than pine from Wielkopolska), (c) with location in the trunk (lower wood, the so-called *twardziel* (heartwood), is denser than the upper *biel* (sapwood)).

The grader assesses density **visually** through three indicators: (i) annual ring width (narrower = denser), (ii) the proportion of heartwood in the cross-section (more dark heartwood = denser), (iii) the mass of the board in the hand (heavier at the same size = denser). Experienced graders can estimate density to within 5-10% merely by picking up the board and looking at its end.

### Visual indicators in strength grading

Standard PN-EN 14081-3 sets out five main visual indicators which the grader assesses:

**Indicator 1: annual ring width.**

Measured on the end of the board as the average width of 10 consecutive rings in the dominant zone. Classes:
- **Below 4 mm/ring**: dense wood, class C24 or higher possible.
- **4-6 mm/ring**: medium wood, class C24 in the absence of other defects.
- **6-8 mm/ring**: loose wood, class C16 at most.
- **Above 8 mm/ring**: very loose wood (fast-growing), below C16, not graded for structural use.

**Indicator 2: slope of grain (*odchylenie słoja*, slope of grain).**

The degree to which the wood fibres deviate from the board's axis. Measured as a ratio (1:X, where X is the number of mm along the board axis per 1 mm of lateral deviation). Classes:
- **1:14 or better** (straighter than 1:14): C30 or higher.
- **1:10 to 1:14**: C24.
- **1:6 to 1:10**: C16.
- **Worse than 1:6**: below C16, rejection.

Slope of grain is critical, because **wood splits along the grain**, not across. If the grain runs at an angle through the board, fracture under load takes place diagonally through the whole cross-section (a log with a 1:4 slope splits the way scissors cut paper). Hence C16 with a 1:6 slope is already the borderline.

**Indicator 3: knots and their location.**

In contrast to visual grading (where the size of the knot and its soundness count), in strength grading what counts is **the location of the knot in the beam's bending cross-section**.

A beam under bending load has:
- **A tension zone** (the lower edge in a classic floor): here the wood is in tension, fractures begin here. Knots in the tension zone are **very dangerous** (they concentrate stresses, initiate fracture).
- **A compression zone** (the upper edge): here the wood is compressed, fractures do not start here easily. Knots are **less dangerous**.
- **A central zone**: between compression and tension (the so-called neutral zone), knots least dangerous.

The criterion in the standard: for class C24 a knot in the tension zone is at most 1/3 of the cross-section height; in the compression zone at most 1/2; in the neutral zone no size limit (only the number counts, up to 3 knots per 1 m of length). For C16 the thresholds are more lenient (1/2, 2/3, no restriction). For C30 they are stricter (1/4, 1/3, 1 knot per 1 m).

The grader must know in which orientation the board will be built in (which side up, whether in compression or in tension). For floor beams the lower face is in tension, the upper in compression. The board is marked with an arrow showing the lower face (**the mark C24↓**).

**Indicator 4: cracks.**

- **End cracks**: permissible up to a certain length (C24: up to 150 mm from the end); above that they disqualify owing to the effect on splitting.
- **Open longitudinal cracks**: cracks visible through the entire board cross-section are **very dangerous** for strength (the board splits along the existing crack). C24 permits longitudinal cracks up to 1/3 of board width, C30 up to 1/4, C35 does not permit them.
- **Frost cracks**: always disqualify from C24 and above (too deep, unstable, risk of propagation under load).

**Indicator 5: disqualifying defects.**

Three defects **always** exclude from structural C classes (even C16):
- Heart rot (structure destroyed, strength unpredictable).
- Deep-penetrating frost cracks (over half the cross-section).
- Severe heat damage (dark wood with a 15-30% loss of strength).

Surface mould (*blue stain*) **does not disqualify** from the C classes (strength preserved). Blue stain through the whole cross-section likewise does not. That is the difference from visual grading, where blue stain eats the G2 class away.

::: warning
A strength grader in Poland **cannot rely on visual appearance alone**. Standard PN-EN 14081-3 requires objective measurements: ring width (ruler, in mm), slope of grain (a special tool in the shape of a pin inserted along the fibre, observation of the deviation), knot size in relation to cross-section height (calipers, arithmetic). A grader who "eyeballs it" and assigns a class without measurements is breaking the standard. In the event of a complaint (e.g., a cracking beam in a client's floor) an ITB inspector will come to check whether the grading was carried out according to the procedure. Absence of measurement records = grading invalid, the sawmill bears full liability.
:::

### The machine method (MSR) as an alternative

Machine grading (*Machine Stress Rating*, MSR) is an alternative to the visual method. A machine (an MSR unit, in Poland the dominant models are **Goldeneye** or **CombiGrader**, cost 150 000-400 000 PLN) directly measures **the board's stiffness** by a test bending under a low load (safe, without damaging the board). Every board is run through the machine, measured and graded automatically. Throughput: 50-150 boards per minute.

**Advantages of MSR:**
- **Objectivity**: the machine does not tire, is not affected by visual assessment errors, is not dependent on operator experience.
- **Speed**: hundreds of thousands of boards daily are possible in large sawmills.
- **Accuracy of MOE measurement**: direct measurement of stiffness, rather than estimation from indicators.

**Disadvantages of MSR:**
- **Investment cost**: 200-400 thousand PLN one-off, plus 30-50 thousand PLN a year for servicing and calibration.
- **Minimum throughput**: economically viable above 20 000 m³ of structural timber per year. EGIDA produces about 8000 m³ of structural timber per year, which does not justify the investment.
- **Less flexibility**: the machine grades standard cross-sections, more difficult for non-standard dimensions or shapes.

In Poland MSR is used mainly by **large integrated sawmills** (Stora Enso in Murashov, Ikea Industry in Orla, a few private operations in Scandinavia). Medium and small sawmills (like EGIDA, 45 000 m³ of production per year, of which 8000 m³ structural) grade **visually**. All in all, visual grading dominates in Poland (70-75% of structural timber placed on the market).

### ITB accreditation for strength graders

The **Building Research Institute (ITB, Instytut Techniki Budowlanej)** in Warsaw is the main certification centre for strength graders for structural timber in Poland. ITB is accredited by the Polish Centre for Accreditation (PCA) in line with PN-EN ISO/IEC 17024 for running personnel certification for graders under PN-EN 14081.

**The accreditation procedure:**

- **Prerequisite**: a current visual grader certificate PN-EN 1611-1 plus 2 years of grading practice in a sawmill producing structural timber.
- **Training**: 14 days (112 hours) at the ITB centre in Warsaw. Programme: strength of materials (mechanics of beams, stresses, strains), principles of timber construction (Eurocode 5, PN-EN 1995), anatomy of structural wood (what determines load capacity), PN-EN 14081 criteria for classes C16-C50, criteria for classes D14-D70 of hardwood, measurement practice in the ITB sawmill.
- **Examination**: 50 reference boards, graded over 8 hours, minimum 85% agreement with the ITB expert's assessment, plus a written exam (80 questions, 80% pass mark).
- **Validity**: 3 years (shorter than the visual one, because standards change faster in the Eurocode context).
- **Refresher**: a 3-day course after 3 years, a shortened exam (20 boards + 40 questions).

**Cost**: 7200 PLN net for the first accreditation (training + exam + certificate), 2400 PLN for the refresher. EGIDA finances accreditations as part of its development strategy (D14 of the spec: "the operator as a potential grader, with training investment from the annual HR budget").

**In Poland about 2400 people hold ITB accreditation** (status as of 2024 according to the PCA register). For comparison, about 8500 people hold the visual PN-EN 1611-1 certificate. The 1:3.5 ratio shows that strength graders are **harder to qualify** (they require deeper mathematical-engineering knowledge) and rarer on the market.

### Strength-grading scene: pine from Piaseczno, 9:40-10:30

Ms. Ania picks up the pine board. She puts it on the table. She switches on the overhead lamp, takes a 1 m rule, calipers, and a pin for the slope of grain.

> **Ms. Ania:** "First indicator: ring width. Look at the end of the board, Mykoła. I count 10 consecutive rings in the dominant zone."

Mykoła looks on. Ms. Ania measures 10 rings with the rule, says: "38 mm over 10 rings = 3.8 mm/ring. Dense wood, capable of class C24 or higher."

> **Ms. Ania:** "Second indicator: slope of grain. Take this pin. We will drive it along the fibre to a depth of 100 mm. Watch how the pin deviates from the board's axis."

Mykoła drives the pin in. After 100 mm of depth the pin deviates 8 mm from the vertical. Ms. Ania calculates: "8 mm of deviation over 100 mm of depth, that is a slope of 1:12.5. The threshold for C24 is 1:10, we fit within it. Still good for C30 (the 1:14 threshold not reached)."

> **Ms. Ania:** "Third: knots. Measure all knots over 5 mm in diameter, their location in the cross-section."

Mykoła measures: "Knot 1 in the central zone, 18 mm. Knot 2 on the upper edge (compression), 32 mm. Knot 3 in the central zone, 12 mm. Knot 4 on the lower edge (tension), 18 mm. Knot 5 on the upper edge, 25 mm."

> **Ms. Ania:** "The most important one: knot 4, in the tension zone, 18 mm. Board cross-section 50 × 150, the tension zone is the lower 1/3 = 50 mm. A knot of 18 mm over 50 mm of zone height = 36%. The threshold for C24 is 1/3 = 33%. We exceed it. **It drops to C16.**"

Mykoła looks on, not understanding: "The whole board, because of one knot of 18 mm?"

> **Ms. Ania:** "Yes. In strength grading location is **critical**. A knot in the tension zone concentrates stresses. If that knot were shifted 20 mm upward (into the neutral zone), we would have C24. But it is in the tension zone, in the lower 1/3 of the cross-section. The class is lowered to C16."
>
> **Mykoła:** "And what if we change the orientation of the board? The tensioned side up, the compressed side down?"
>
> **Ms. Ania:** "Good question. For this board in the floor one can indeed do that, because the knot is a single one. But then knots 2 and 5 (25 and 32 mm) are in the tension zone (flipped), and they also exceed 1/3 = 50 mm, 25 and 32 mm being 50% and 64%. We exceed the threshold. It falls below C16. In that orientation it is even worse."
>
> **Mykoła:** "So the board has to go as C16, if we keep the original orientation."
>
> **Ms. Ania:** "Yes. We record **C16** with a note about the lower tension edge (the mark C16↓)."

Ms. Ania fills in the form. Class C16. The board **does not meet** the client's order (C24). It goes onto the C16 stack (other orders, e.g., economy roof trusses). For the order from the Piaseczno client the grader has to find another board.

> **Ms. Ania** (to Mykoła): "This is today's lesson. By appearance the board looks like a proper C24. After the measurements it comes out as C16. Strength grading **does not forgive**. One knot in the wrong place, the class drops. This is why a C24 client pays more: not for the board, but for the **selection**. We cut and grade 100 boards to find 40 C24s (plus 45 C16, 10 below, 5 rejects). The price of C24 includes the cost of that selection."

Mykoła writes everything down in his notebook. The second lesson of the assistance. The first board of the day has ended with **a class drop below the order**. For the Piaseczno client Ms. Ania will take the next board, and the next, until she finds 40 boards meeting C24 from a 60-board batch.

### What Mykoła records in the documentation

EGIDA form KLS-001 (structural grading card) for each C-class board:

- **Board identifier:** number in the batch, dimensions, species.
- **Measurement 1:** ring width (mm/ring).
- **Measurement 2:** slope of grain (ratio 1:X).
- **Measurement 3:** knots (number, location, size), with location marking (tension / compression / neutral).
- **Measurement 4:** cracks (length, width, type).
- **Disqualifying defects**: tick (rot, frost crack, severe heat damage), if present.
- **Final class**: C16, C24, C30, C35, or "reject" (below C16).
- **Orientation marking**: arrow indicating the lower edge (tension) for floor beams.
- **Strength grader's signature** (mandatory, with the ITB accreditation number).
- **Assistant's signature**: Mykoła signs to confirm participation in the measurements.

The KLS-001 card goes into the batch file, with a **10-year** retention (longer than for visual grading, because structural complaints often occur 5-8 years after construction, when the structure is working under variable loads).

### Summary of lesson three

Strength grading (PN-EN 14081-3 visual, PN-EN 14081-2 machine, classes in PN-EN 338) assesses the **mechanical load capacity** of wood, not its appearance. The four most common Polish classes: C16 (economy, 15-20% of production), C24 (standard, 50-55%), C30 (reinforced, 15-20%), C35 (high-load glued-laminated, 5-8%).

Three mechanical parameters defining the class: MOR (bending strength, the number in the code), MOE (modulus of elasticity, stiffness), characteristic density.

Five visual indicators in grading: ring width, slope of grain, knots with location in the cross-section (tension more dangerous than compression), cracks (longitudinal more dangerous than end cracks), disqualifying defects (rot, frost cracks, severe heat damage).

ITB accreditation for strength graders: 14-day training, a 50-board exam, 3-year validity, cost of 7200 PLN. Prerequisite: a PN-EN 1611-1 certificate plus 2 years of practice. In Poland about 2400 people are accredited.

Ms. Ania at EGIDA holds both: the PN-EN 1611-1 certificate (visual, since 2014) and ITB accreditation for PN-EN 14081 (strength, since 2020, refreshed 2023). She grades both sides of wood (appearance + load capacity) within one batch.

Today Mykoła has learned that **one knot in the wrong place** lowers the class from C24 to C16. A board that looked proper came out as C16 on the measurements. The C24 client pays more for the **selection**, not for the board itself. Selection requires a grader with ITB accreditation who measures objectively.
