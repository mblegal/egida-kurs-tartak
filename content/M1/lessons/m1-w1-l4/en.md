## Introduction

A sawmill is a place where everything you look at is **fuel**. Wood, sawdust, chips, dust. Hydraulic oils in the machines, greases, varnishes in the store. Everywhere dry, everywhere flammable. In a place like this **one spark can turn a production floor into a fire in a minute**.

That is why fire hazards in a sawmill are treated differently from an office. An extinguisher in the corner is not enough. You need **three lines of defence**: order (we do not let fuel pile up), attention to ignition sources (we know what sparks), quick reaction (we know how to alarm and escape). The helper participates in all three.

In this lesson you will learn the **fire triangle**, typical **ignition sources** in a sawmill, the places where **dust can explode**, and what you do **in the first 30 seconds after seeing fire**.

## Objectives

After this lesson:

- You understand what the **fire triangle** is (fuel + oxygen + ignition source) and why a sawmill is especially exposed.
- You recognize five typical **ignition sources**: friction, mechanical sparks, electricity, cigarettes, hot surfaces.
- You know what a **wood-dust explosion** is and where you will meet it.
- You know the response pattern **ALARM → STOP → EVACUATE → (optionally) extinguish**.
- You tell apart types of **extinguishers** (ABC, CO₂) and you know where the **evacuation routes** are.

## Content

### The fire triangle

Every fire needs three things together:

1. **Fuel**: something that burns. In a sawmill: wood, sawdust, dust, oils, varnishes, paper.
2. **Oxygen**: is in the air.
3. **Ignition source**: a spark, a flame, a hot surface, friction.

If you take away one corner of the triangle, there is no fire. **You cannot take the fuel and oxygen away from a sawmill.** That is why the whole fight for fire safety is the fight for **the third corner, control of ignition sources**.

### Five ignition sources

**1. Friction.** A machine running long without lubrication heats the bearings. A jamming saw heats up from the cut. After ten or more minutes friction can produce temperatures above 300°C, enough for sawdust around it to catch.

**2. Mechanical sparks.** A stone or a metal piece embedded in the wood hits the blade, a spark flies. Grinding, welding, cutting metal, in a sawmill these activities **require the foreman's permission and a shield**. They are not done near sawdust.

**3. Electricity.** A worn cable, a loose contact, a motor with damaged insulation. A short circuit produces an arc temperature of 3000°C in a fraction of a second. If you see a smoking cable, you press STOP and report to the foreman.

**4. Cigarettes.** In a sawmill there is an **absolute smoking ban** outside designated areas (usually outdoors, away from buildings). A cigarette butt tossed into a bin with sawdust = fire. This is not just disciplinary, it is criminal law if damage results.

**5. Hot surfaces.** An electric motor heats up after hours of work. A tractor exhaust pipe 400°C. Halogen lamps in older halls, 300°C. Do not touch without gloves, do not place near flammable materials.

### Wood-dust explosion: a special hazard

Fine wood dust in the air, at the right concentration, behaves like a **flammable gas**. One spark, and the dust cloud ignites in a fraction of a second across its whole volume. This is a **dust explosion**.

::: warning
A wood-dust explosion in a sawmill is not theory. In Poland, events of this type are recorded every few years, most often at dust silos and at extractors. Consequences: loss of the hall roof, burns to workers, death. That is why **special rules apply at extractors, sawdust silos, and drying kilns**: no-sparking, earthing, ventilation. A helper does not work here unsupervised.
:::

### Risk locations in a sawmill

- **Under machines**: sawdust and chips pile up. One hot bearing = fire under the floor.
- **At extractors and filters**: dust in the filter, next to a spark = explosion.
- **Drying kilns**: high temperature, wood with oil, closed space.
- **Sawdust and biomass stores**: spontaneous combustion with poor ventilation.
- **Repair workshop**: welding, grinding, sparks. Must be separated from the production floor.

### Your 30 seconds: the ALARM → STOP → EVACUATE pattern

You see fire or smoke. You now have **30 seconds** for three things, in this order:

**1. ALARM (5 seconds).** You press the fire alarm button (red, on the wall) or shout loudly "FIRE!". Everyone on the floor must know.

**2. STOP (5 seconds).** You press the nearest emergency stop on a machine. Turning machines off removes one of the ignition sources and stops dust production.

**3. EVACUATE (20 seconds, you walk).** You walk the **evacuation route** to the assembly point outside the building. Do not run through the hall by a shortcut, do not go back for personal belongings, do not use a lift.

::: info
**You can attempt extinguishing only** if the fire is small (the size of a waste-bin), you have an extinguisher in your hand, you know how to use it, and you have a clear escape route behind you. In every other case, evacuate, and the firefighters or designated workers extinguish.
:::

### Extinguishers: types and use

In a sawmill you will meet two main types:

- **ABC powder extinguisher**: the most universal. A = solid materials (wood, paper), B = liquids (oils, varnishes), C = gases. **This is the first-choice extinguisher on the floor.**
- **CO₂ extinguisher**: for **live electrical equipment**. Powder can damage electronics, CO₂ cannot. Kept close to switch cabinets.

Using an extinguisher, **PASS**:
- **P**ull the pin.
- **A**im the nozzle at the base of the fire (not at the flame, at the fuel).
- **S**queeze the lever.
- **S**weep side to side.

An average 6-kg powder extinguisher works for about **15 seconds**. You have one chance.

### Evacuation routes

- **Marking**: a green sign with a white running figure and a direction arrow.
- **State**: the route must be **always clear**: you cannot store material on it, you cannot block doors. If you see a blocked evacuation route, report to the foreman.
- **Emergency lighting**: green lamps above the exit, lit when the main power is out (battery lasts about 1 hour).
- **Assembly point** (the place to meet after evacuation) is outside, away from the building. In the first week the foreman will show you where it is. **Attendance is checked there**: every worker reports, so that the firefighters know who may still be inside.

::: example
From the records of the State Fire Service: a small sawmill fire in the Mazowieckie voivodeship, 2019, a cigar butt dropped into a sawdust bin. The worker noticed smoke after 3 minutes. He pressed the alarm, turned off the nearest machine, informed the foreman, and evacuated with a group of colleagues. The fire service extinguished the fire in 15 minutes. Losses: the sawdust bin and a hall wall. **Zero casualties.** The key to a good ending: the worker **did not try to extinguish by himself**: he did ALARM, STOP, EVACUATE and handed the action over to professionals.
:::

- **PPOŻ** (ochrona przeciwpożarowa) 🔰, fire protection / protección contra incendios / протипожежний захист

## Check yourself

1. You see a big fire on the floor, an electrical cabinet in flames, smoke spreading fast. What do you do in the first 30 seconds?
   - A) I take an ABC powder extinguisher and put it out.
   - B) ALARM → STOP → EVACUATE. I extinguish only if the fire is small and I have a clear escape route.
   - C) I run for the foreman and wait for orders.

   → **B**

2. In which place in a sawmill is the **dust-explosion** hazard greatest?
   - A) At the transport crane.
   - B) At the extractor, in a sawdust silo, in a drying kiln.
   - C) In the foreman's office.

   → **B**

3. For fighting **live electrical equipment** fires you use an extinguisher:
   - A) ABC powder.
   - B) CO₂.
   - C) Water.

   → **B**

## Link to practice

In your first week the foreman will walk you along the **evacuation route** from your workstation to the assembly point outside. Count the steps, remember the turns. When the hall is full of smoke, you do not see the signs, you walk the way your body remembers.

He will also show you **every fire alarm** and **every extinguisher** in your area. Extinguishers have seals (green = operational, seal missing = used, must be replaced) and an inspection date. If you notice an unsealed or expired extinguisher, report to the foreman.

## Trainer notes

**Pedagogical goal:** the learner has an **automated sequence** ALARM → STOP → EVACUATE. This is a sequence to practise physically, not just to memorize. In a fire there is no time to think, there is time for a trained reaction.

**120-minute rhythm:** 10 min of lesson 3 revision (dust as the bridge, from a health hazard it becomes a fire hazard), 20 min fire triangle + five ignition sources, 15 min on the wood-dust explosion (visualization: a video of a controlled dust explosion in a laboratory, available on university YouTube channels), 20 min the 30-second pattern with a simulation (physical exercise: the trainer shouts "FIRE!", learners run the sequence), 15 min on extinguishers with a hands-on demo (without using, just in the hand, feel the weight, check the seal), 15 min on evacuation routes (a simulated walk), 15 min on terms and the self-check, 10 min wrap-up of week 1 (this is the last lesson on hazards before l5 on PPE).

**Fire simulation:** the best is a **dark/smoke-filled evacuation drill**: but it requires a professional smoke machine and permission. Instead, ask the learners to close their eyes, give the alarm signal (a bell or an app), and have them find the way to the door from memory. Whoever touches the wall learns the geography of the room.

**Common mistake:** a learner wants to "be a hero" and put out a big fire. Trainer: explain this is a **professional decision, not a personal one**. Firefighters know how to extinguish; a worker who tries to put out something too big becomes a casualty they then have to rescue. A good helper is one who can escape and does not hamper the rescue.
