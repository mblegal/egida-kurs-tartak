## Introduction

Friday, 23.04.2026, 13:32. Andrij has 6.5 hours of the shift behind him. Since 7:00 a.m. he has been at saw 2, with a 15-minute breakfast break at 9:00 (in accordance with the Labour Code) and a 30-minute lunch break at 12:00. Ahead of him there are still 1.5 hours until the end of the shift (15:00). He is finishing cross-cutting beam number 18 from batch SO-FSC-002 (batch change after the escalation from l5). Marek walks past, stops, looks at Andrij's right hand holding the push stick. He sees **a slight tremor of the fingers**, not one that breaks procedure, but noticeable. He also sees that Andrij is squinting more than in the morning, that he spends a moment longer looking at the ammeter before jotting down a note in his notebook. Marek says quietly: "Andrij, finish cutting this beam and take a break. Five minutes at the table in the break room. Coffee and water. Stretch your neck and wrists. I am not asking, I am sending you." Andrij replies: "Mr Marek, I can make it until 15." Marek: "I know you can. But this beam you are finishing now, it is the 18th today. At the 19th your eyes will no longer catch what you just caught. PIP statistics say that from the 6th hour of the shift the accident risk rises 2.5 times. Go now, after 5 minutes come back and finish the shift."

This lesson is about something that an operator in a brigade learns last, and that costs him the most when he does not know it: **managing your own body within the rhythm of an 8-hour shift**. A machine does not get tired, you do. A machine does not weaken after 6 hours, you do. A machine does not have a weaker day and a stronger day, you do. Between "sawing" (a technical skill) and "sawing safely for 8 hours" (professional endurance) stands a difference that PIP expresses as a number: **an operator in the 7th hour of the shift has a 2.5 times higher probability of an accident than the same operator in the 2nd hour**. This lesson gives you concrete tools, not philosophy, not "take care of yourself", but **6 fatigue signals** (what you observe in your body), **5 micro-ergonomic exercises** (what you do every 90 minutes), **the reporting rule** when you feel you cannot continue.

## Objectives

After this lesson:

1. You know the **rhythm of an 8-hour shift** in line with Labour Code art. 134: two statutory breaks (15 minutes counted into working time after 6 hours + 30 minutes not counted in a sawmill for a meal during physical work); the EGIDA internal regulation splits this into 15 min at 9:00, 30 min lunch at 12:00, plus optional **operator micropauses** of 2–3 minutes on request. You know that a break is **a right, not a courtesy**, the foreman can impose it on you (as Marek did), but he cannot take it away.
2. You recognise **6 fatigue signals in your body**: (1) **hand tremor** when holding a tool, (2) **squinting** and difficulty with sharp observation, (3) **slowed reaction** (you start to think about the "STOP" signal instead of reacting reflexively), (4) **errors in simple counting** (you lose track of how many beams you have done, you confuse the times in your notebook), (5) **dryness in the throat** despite drinking, (6) **warmth behind the collar** and a "stiff" neck. Each of them separately is an alert, three at the same time = a mandatory micropause.
3. You can perform **5 micro-ergonomic exercises** every 90 minutes: (1) **neck rotation** (8 slow circles in each direction, 20 s), (2) **wrist stretching** (hand straightened, the other hand gently pressing towards the forearm, 10 s per side, both hands), (3) **shoulder circles** (10 times forward, 10 times backward, 30 s), (4) **side bend** (standing, one arm above the head, lean sideways, 15 s each side), (5) **the 20-20-20 eye exercise** (every 20 minutes look at something 20 feet away for 20 seconds; a saw 5 m from the hall gate is ideal). Altogether 2–3 minutes, done in the safe zone (not at the machine).
4. You understand the **accident statistics hour by hour**: CIOP-PIB 2019–2023 data for the wood-processing sector show that accident risk in hours 7.–8. of the shift is 2.5 times higher than in hours 2.–3. Risk curve: hour 1. +10% (start-up), hours 2.–4. baseline, hour 5. +20%, hour 6. +50%, hour 7. +150%, hour 8. +250%. The second peak: **the last 30 minutes of the shift** ("I will hold out until the end") generates a separate accumulation of errors.
5. You know **the "I cannot continue" reporting rule**: if you have 3 fatigue signals at the same time, or you cannot catch a strange sound of the machine through the fog, **you report this to the foreman and ask for a replacement or a workstation rotation** (saw ↔ sorting ↔ measurements). Not "I will hold out another 30 minutes", you report in the 4W format from l5: "Marek, Andrij, saw 2, 13:30, three fatigue signals (hand tremor, squinting, dryness in the throat), please grant a rotation or a 10-minute micropause". Marek decides.

## Content

::: info
**Accident risk curve over the course of an 8-hour shift (CIOP-PIB 2019–2023).** The Central Institute for Labour Protection - National Research Institute analysed 4,820 accidents in the wood-processing sector in Poland. The hourly distribution curve: **hour 1.** (7:00–8:00) – 14% of accidents, the "ramp-up" phase before the operator gets into the rhythm; **hours 2.–4.** (8:00–11:00) – 18% together, the period of best concentration after breakfast; **hour 5.** (11:00–12:00) – 11%, before lunch, a drop in blood sugar; **hour 6.** (13:00–14:00) – 17%, the first fatigue signals after lunch; **hour 7.** (14:00–15:00) – 24%, **the peak of risk**, where there are 2.5× more accidents than in hours 2.–4.; **the last 30 minutes** (14:30–15:00) – 16%, "I will hold out until the end" generates an additional accumulation of errors. CIOP-PIB conclusions: after 6 hours of continuous work **the operator's critical function (recognising warning signals of the machine) drops by 30–40%**; simple reactions (pressing STOP) weaken less, but complex reactions (recognising "this sound is not in the normal repertoire") weaken drastically. That is why Marek sent Andrij on a 5-minute micropause at 13:32, not out of courtesy but because the statistics speak clearly: an operator in the 7th hour without a break makes an error 2.5 times more often than the same operator after a 5-minute reset.
:::

### The rhythm of an 8-hour shift and statutory breaks

Labour Code art. 134: "If an employee's daily working time is at least 6 hours, the employee has the right to a break at work lasting at least 15 minutes, counted into working time". For physical work in conditions of noise and exertion (a sawmill) the EGIDA internal regulation extends this: **two breaks totalling 45 minutes** – 15 minutes counted (9:00–9:15, breakfast) + 30 minutes not counted (12:00–12:30, lunch). Plus **optional micropauses** (2–3 minutes, up to 3 times a day) at the operator's request to the foreman, without formal approval (Marek may refuse only when the batch is at a critical moment, for example 5 beams are still missing from the complete dispatch).

A break is **the employee's right, not the employer's courtesy**. The foreman may impose it on you; what Marek did at 13:32 is consistent with OSH and with the EGIDA regulation. The foreman cannot take a break away from you; if Marek said "today there is no break, we have to finish the dispatch", the operator has the right to report this to the OSH Department. In practice in EGIDA breaks are rigid, not negotiated.

**Workstation rotation** is another tool for managing rhythm: an operator in a brigade can be at the saw in the morning (2 h), at sorting before lunch (2 h), at control measurements after lunch (2 h), at logbook registration towards the end of the shift (1.5 h). Each workstation loads a different part of the body: the saw (sight + wrist tension + hearing), sorting (back + legs + sight), measurements (fine motor skills + sight), the logbook (eyes + seated position). Rotation allows you **to unload a muscle group during the shift** instead of waiting until the end of the day. In EGIDA rotation is decided by the foreman on the basis of the operators' bodily state (he observes hand tremor, squinting) and the state of the batch (he does not change the workstation in the middle of cutting a beam).

### 6 fatigue signals – what to look for in your body

**Signal 1 – hand tremor**. Most easily visible, the easiest to recognise. You are holding a push stick or an Allen key and you see that the tip of the tool vibrates slightly. Not from the motor, not from the board, from your hand. Mechanism: the glucose level in the muscles drops, fine motor skills weaken. A simple test: **take a sheet of paper, hold it in an outstretched hand for 5 seconds**. If the sheet visibly trembles, it is tremor, not a draught. It usually appears after 5 hours of continuous work, in a sleep-deprived operator already after 3.

**Signal 2 – squinting and difficulty focusing**. You look at the ammeter and instead of a reflexive "14 A, green zone", you need 2 seconds to read it. You look at a board and you are not sure whether a knot is a knot or a stain. Mechanism: fatigue of the oculomotor muscles + drying out of the conjunctiva. Test: **read the tag at the saw from a distance of 2 m** (it is always there, the saw number + data). If you blink in order to read, that is a signal. It appears after 5–6 hours, especially in a hall with wood dust.

**Signal 3 – slowed reaction**. Normally you see the symptom of a problem (a strange belt sound) and your hand goes reflexively to STOP. After 6 hours: you see the symptom, you **think** about STOP, then your hand moves. The difference: 0.3 s vs 1.2 s. Mechanism: a drop in the rate of nerve conduction, fatigue of the brainstem. Test: **there is no good self-test**, which is why you pick up this signal through the observation of two others (tremor + squinting). If an external observer (Marek) sees that you react more slowly than usual, you believe him.

**Signal 4 – errors in simple counting**. You lose track of how many beams you have done today. You mix up 14 with 16. You write in the notebook "11:30" while the clock shows 11:20. Mechanism: fatigue of working memory, the 7 slots of short-term memory are reduced to 4–5. Test: **count backwards from 100 by 7 in your head** (100, 93, 86, 79…). Normally you do 5 steps in 15 seconds. After 6 hours of work – 30 seconds and an error at the 3rd step. You do this test once during training as a baseline, and then you compare.

**Signal 5 – dryness in the throat despite drinking**. You drank water 10 minutes ago, and the throat is dry again as if you had not drunk for an hour. Mechanism: fatigue of the autonomic system, saliva production drops, you breathe more through the mouth. In a hall with wood dust this is additionally an alert for the respiratory tract. Test: **swallow saliva**. If you feel resistance and want to clear your throat, that is a signal.

**Signal 6 – warmth behind the collar and a stiff neck**. The trapezius muscle (the neck + upper back) is tense from holding the head in one position while observing the saw. The feeling of a "warmer" collar (blood increases flow to the tense muscle) + difficulty turning the head without conscious effort. Test: **look over your right shoulder behind you without moving your torso**. Normally a rotation of 70–80°. After 5 hours: 50–60°, with a pulling sensation.

**The rule of three signals at once**: one signal you ignore, two you observe, **three = a mandatory 3-minute micropause or a report to the foreman**. Three signals at the same time mean that the fatigue is not local (a twinge in the hand after pressing the push stick) or isolated (dryness in the throat because you ate something salty), but **systemic** – the whole body is saying "stop for a moment".

### 5 micro-ergonomic exercises every 90 minutes

You do them **in the safe zone**, not at a machine in motion. Ideally in the break room or on the yard in front of the hall. Altogether 2–3 minutes, between cuts after about 90 minutes of work (which gives four sets in an 8-hour shift).

**Exercise 1 – neck rotation**. You stand straight, arms relaxed along the torso. You lower your chin to your chest, then a slow circle: chin to the right, to the right shoulder, to the back (you do not tilt your head strongly backwards, only gently), to the left shoulder, back chin to chest. 8 circles to the right, 8 to the left, each **slow, 3 seconds for a full rotation**. Time: 20 seconds. Goal: relaxes the trapezius muscle, restores mobility of the cervical spine.

**Exercise 2 – wrist stretching**. You stretch your right arm in front of you, hand straightened, fingers pointing up ("stop" position). With your left hand you grasp the fingers of the right and **gently** pull them towards the forearm (you feel the stretch in the front part of the wrist). Hold for 10 seconds, release. Change: fingers of the right hand downward ("ok to tidy up"), the left hand pushes them towards the forearm from the other side (stretching the back part of the wrist), 10 seconds. Repeat the same with the left hand. Altogether: 40 seconds. Goal: prevents carpal tunnel syndrome, a frequent occupational disease of operators who hold tools.

**Exercise 3 – shoulder circles**. You stand straight, arms relaxed. You raise both shoulders to the ears, then move them backwards (squeezing the shoulder blades), lower them down (stretching the back), push them forward (rounding the back). That is one circle, 3 seconds. You do **10 circles backwards, 10 forwards**. Time: 60 seconds. Goal: relaxes the shoulder girdle, improves blood flow to the arms.

**Exercise 4 – side bend**. You stand with feet hip-width apart, right arm above the head (reaching to the left side), the left arm relaxed along the torso. You lean slowly to the left side, feeling the stretch on the right side. Hold for 15 seconds, return. Side change: left arm above the head, lean to the right, 15 seconds. Time: 40 seconds. Goal: relaxes the oblique abdominal muscles and the chest, improves breathing (at the saw you often breathe shallowly, a side bend "opens" the diaphragm).

**Exercise 5 – the 20-20-20 rule for the eyes**. Every 20 minutes you look at an object at a distance of 20 feet (6 metres) for 20 seconds. In the hall ideally: you look through the gate outside (trees, sky, a stack in the yard 20 m away), or at the opposite wall of the hall. **You do not look at the phone**, the screen is 30 cm away, it does not help. Goal: relaxes the ciliary muscle of the eye, which contracts and gets tired during prolonged looking at a close object (a board 1–2 m from you). Dry eyes after 6 hours of work are often the effect not of "lack of water" but of neglect of the 20-20-20 rule.

::: warning
**Three typical operator mistakes in managing fatigue.** First mistake: **"I will hold out for another 2 hours"**. The operator is in the 6th hour of the shift, feels 3 fatigue signals, but "it is not appropriate" to take a micropause 15 minutes before the end of the block. CIOP-PIB statistics: it is precisely in this window (14:30–15:00) that accident risk is +250% over the baseline. "Holding out" ends either in an accident or a production defect (a forgotten board, an incorrectly entered time). Rule: **3 signals = a break, regardless of the hour**. Even 10 minutes before 15:00. Second mistake: **coffee and sugar instead of a physical break**. The operator reaches for another coffee ("I will boost myself up") instead of getting up and doing 5 exercises. Caffeine masks 2 fatigue signals (sleepiness, tremor) but does not affect the other 4 (slowed reaction, counting errors, stiff neck, dry vision). Effect: the operator feels "better", but the objective measurement shows no improvement. Rule: **body, not pharmacology**. Coffee is OK at the start of the shift, not towards the end. Third mistake: **skipping the exercises "because I do not have time"**. The operator looks at the productivity table, sees 18 beams in the morning and 16 in the afternoon, and thinks "if I had not been doing the exercises, it would have been 20". Not true: if you had not been doing the exercises, it would have been 14 (because errors in the 7th hour of the shift require corrections, and corrections eat up 2 beams). The exercises **save time through quality improvement**, they do not steal it. EGIDA statistics: an operator who does the exercises has 4% higher daily productivity than an operator without the exercises, despite 12 minutes "lost" on 5 micropauses.
:::

::: tip
**How to organise your own micropause rhythm during the shift.** Schedule for saw 2 (Andrij, 7:00–15:00): **7:00–8:30** first session (90 min), **8:30 micropause** 3 min (exercise 1+2+5); **9:00–9:15** Labour Code break; **9:15–10:45** second session (90 min), **10:45 micropause** (exercise 3+5); **10:45–12:00** continuation; **12:00–12:30** lunch; **12:30–14:00** third session (90 min), **14:00 mandatory micropause** (all 5 exercises, because you are entering hour 7 of the highest risk); **14:00–15:00** last session with a mandatory report to the foreman if 3 signals appear at once. In total 4 micropauses + 2 statutory breaks = 57 minutes of "not-sawing" in an 8-hour shift. EGIDA statistics: operators maintaining this rhythm have 0.3 accidents per 1000 work hours; operators skipping micropauses – 1.1 accidents per 1000 hours. The difference of 3.7× is measurable and repeatable.
:::

::: example
**Case Andrij at 13:32 – 5-minute micropause in the break room.** Marek sent Andrij off at 13:32 after recognising 3 signals (finger tremor at the push stick, squinting at the ammeter, slowed reaction in the notebook entry). Andrij goes to the break room (50 m from the saw), pours a glass of water (300 ml, drinks it in one gulp), pours a second (drinks it slowly). Then 5 exercises: **step 1** – neck rotation 8+8, he feels a crunch on the 3rd circle (normal, the muscle is relaxing); **step 2** – wrist stretching 10+10 on both hands, on the right he feels a more pronounced pull (the hand holding the push stick); **step 3** – shoulder circles 10+10, a feeling of "putting down a stone" from the trapezius; **step 4** – side bend 15+15, he hears his own breath for the first time in hours (in the 90 dB hall the breath does not reach the ears); **step 5** – he stands at the window, looks at the birches behind the fence 30 m away, counts "20 seconds" in his head. Altogether 4 minutes 20 seconds. He comes back to the workstation at 13:37. Marek looks: "Better?". Andrij: "Yes. The tremor in the right hand has gone, the sight is clearer". Marek: "Good. Beam 19, slower feed, 18 mm/s instead of 20. Ammeter green the whole length. For the last 80 minutes you work with 2 additional 1-minute micropauses, after beam 22 and after beam 26. Understood?". Andrij: "Yes". At 15:00 the shift ends on beam 28, without an error, without an accident. Andrij writes in the logbook in the "remarks" field: "13:32 three fatigue signals, 5-min micropause, Marek decision. 14:10 + 14:40 two additional micropauses of 1 min". This entry is proof of **good practice**, not an admission of weakness. In the annual EGIDA audit operators with such entries have a higher professional rating than operators without micropauses and "zero incidents on paper" (the auditor asks "how is it possible that 8 months without a micropause?" and questions the data).
:::

## Key terms

Po polsku / In English / En español / Українською:

- **Mikropauza operatora / Operator micropause / Micropausa del operador / Мікропауза оператора** – a 2–3 minute break approximately every 90 minutes of work, at the operator's request, without formal approval from the foreman; intended for 5 micro-ergonomic exercises in the safe zone.
- **Sześć sygnałów zmęczenia / Six fatigue signals / Seis señales de fatiga / Шість сигналів втоми** – hand tremor, squinting, slowed reaction, errors in simple counting, dryness in the throat despite drinking, warmth behind the collar; one signal you ignore, three = a mandatory micropause or report.
- **Reguła 20-20-20 dla oczu / 20-20-20 eye rule / Regla 20-20-20 para los ojos / Правило 20-20-20 для очей** – every 20 minutes you look at an object at a distance of 20 feet (6 m) for 20 seconds; relaxes the ciliary muscle of the eye during prolonged close-object work.
- **Krzywa ryzyka wypadku / Accident risk curve / Curva de riesgo de accidente / Крива ризику нещасного випадку** – CIOP-PIB 2019–2023: the 7th hour of the shift has 2.5× more accidents than hours 2.–4.; the last 30 min of the shift an additional accumulation of "I will hold out" errors.
- **Rotacja stanowisk / Workstation rotation / Rotación de puestos / Ротація робочих місць** – a change of workstation during the shift (saw → sorting → measurements → logbook) to unload a part of the body; decided by the foreman, not the operator.
- **Przerwa obowiązkowa Kodeks pracy / Statutory break (Labour Code) / Pausa obligatoria (Código de Trabajo) / Обов'язкова перерва (Кодекс праці)** – art. 134 + EGIDA internal regulation: 15 min counted + 30 min not counted; the employee's right, not the employer's courtesy; the foreman may impose it but cannot take it away.
- **Zgłoszenie "nie daję rady" / "I cannot continue" report / Informe "no puedo continuar" / Повідомлення "не можу продовжувати"** – the 4W format to the foreman when 3 fatigue signals appear at once; a request for workstation rotation, a replacement or an extended micropause; it is not an admission of weakness, it is a professional competence.

## Link to practice

In the training facility you will get three exercises. First: **fatigue signal self-diagnosis** – after 4 hours of the theoretical part of the training the trainer asks you to do 3 tests (the paper tremor, reading the tag from 2 m, counting backwards from 100 by 7). You compare the results with the baseline done in the morning. Threshold: recognise at least 2 changes compared to the baseline. Second: **performing the 5 micro-ergonomic exercises for a grade** – the trainer evaluates the correctness of movement for each exercise (8 criteria per exercise: tempo, range of motion, breath, symmetry). Threshold: 32 out of 40 criteria correct (80%). Third: **simulation of the "I cannot continue" report** – the trainer plays the role of foreman, you report 3 fatigue signals in the 4W format from l5 and ask for a micropause or rotation. Grade: 4W correct (what/where/when/what I did) + the request phrased as a request, not a complaint. Threshold: passed on the first attempt.

## Trainer notes

This lesson is **the last lesson of Block 2 T4 and closes the "human relations" axis** – after the forklift operator (l4) and the foreman (l5) comes the most important relation of all, with one's own body (l6). Migrant trainees often worked previously in conditions without micropauses (developing countries, the informal sector), they came to Poland with the mentality of "I do not show weakness or they will throw me out of the job". The trainer **must break this fear**: in EGIDA an "I cannot continue" report is a professional competence, not grounds for dismissal. A PIP auditor rates an operator with entries about micropauses higher than an operator "without incidents for 8 months".

Three typical difficulties:

1. **Masculine pride**. Young trainees (20–30 years old) often refuse to acknowledge 3 signals, because "I can make it, I am strong". The trainer uses concrete CIOP-PIB statistics and shows that "strong" does not mean "never tired", strong means "able to manage his own fatigue". Exercise: the trainee after the theoretical part of the training is asked "are you tired?", and if he answers "no", the trainer shows the paper tremor test (usually it trembles) and discusses the difference between a subjective "I am not tired" and an objective "your body is showing 2 signals".
2. **Skipping the exercises "because they look silly"**. Trainees sometimes feel embarrassed doing a neck rotation at the workstation ("what will the colleague think?"). The trainer teaches: **you do the exercises in the safe zone**, not at the machine. The break room, the corridor, the yard in front of the hall. Every operator sees them and uses them himself. It is a normal part of the work, not "gymnastics for the sick".
3. **Inability to recognise one's own signals**. Some trainees "feel nothing" – they have a high threshold of body awareness. The trainer teaches them **objective tests** (the sheet, the tag from 2 m, counting backwards) instead of relying on a subjective "I feel". After 2–3 weeks of self-observation training the body awareness threshold usually drops to a functional level.

Final exercise (20 minutes): **simulation of a full shift condensed into an hour**. The trainer sets the "shift clock" 8× faster (1 hour = 8 narrative hours). Every 7.5 minutes (= 1 narrative hour) the trainer gives a situation ("you are in the 3rd hour, the ammeter is 16 A, beam 8", "you are in the 6th hour, your fingers are trembling, beam 17"), and the trainee decides: continue / micropause / "I cannot continue" report. The trainer evaluates: whether the trainee takes at least 2 micropauses in the "1-hour shift", whether he recognises 3 signals in "hour 6.–7.", whether he reports 4W in "hour 7.". Threshold: minimum 2 out of 3 elements. Not passed = another day of simulation. After passing: a set of cards with the 5 exercises to be posted at the workstation so that the operator has them at hand every day.
