## Introduction

Friday, the eighth day of the second week. Yesterday Carlos learned to **see** four wood defects. Today he is learning to **name what he sees on the stacks after cutting**. At 7:30 master Jan leads him along the eastern wall of the hall, where five different stacks of lumber lie, each marked with a number plate from 1 to 5.

"Look, Carlos. All of this came out of one oak log from yesterday. **Five different stacks, five different prices, five different customers.** Before I teach you to sort into these stacks, you must know what to call the things lying there. Otherwise you write 'board' in the order book and nobody knows what you mean."

This lesson is about **lumber assortments**: trade names for what comes out of the sawmill after a log is broken down. Words that look like synonyms are, in the timber trade, different categories with different prices and different customers: **edged and unedged** is not the same as **board and beam**, and the difference of a single edging pass can triple the price per cubic meter. A *cant* (Polish: *pryzma*) is neither a log nor a board, but a semi-finished product in between, and confusing it with a beam is one of the most common mistakes a new helper makes. A *slab* (Polish: *oblina*) is a scrap valuable as firewood or a packaging board, but never structural, and treating it as garbage costs the sawmill real money. Today Carlos will learn to distinguish five basic assortments, know which machine each one comes out of, and understand why the same log can produce products whose prices differ by a factor of ten.

## Objectives

After this lesson:

1. You understand that an **assortment** is a trade category of lumber: a set of features (edging, dimensions, grade) defining what the product is suitable for and how much it costs. One trunk yields 5–8 different assortments.
2. You distinguish **edged lumber** (four straight edges, 90° angle, no bark) from **unedged lumber** (two straight edges from the saw, two natural side edges with bark or live edge).
3. You recognize a **slab** (Polish: *oblina*, English: *slab*): the first or last piece of a cut log, with bark preserved on one side and a flat cut on the other. You know it goes to firewood, packaging boards, or wood chips.
4. You recognize a **half-sawn log** (Polish: *opół*, English: *half-sawn log*): a log cut through the middle into two halves, with a half-round cross-section. An intermediate assortment, either raw material for further breakdown or a rustic product.
5. You recognize a **cant** (Polish: *pryzma*, English: *cant*): a log squared down to a rectangular or square cross-section, a semi-finished product before final cutting into boards. A veneer-grade oak cant 40×40 cm can cost 2500 zł/m³.
6. You know **what comes out of which machine**: the **band saw** (headrig) cuts the log lengthwise yielding unedged boards plus a slab; the **edger** trims the sides of unedged boards, yielding edged lumber plus waste strips; the **frame saw** (gang saw) cuts the cant into boards in one pass; the **crosscut saw** trims to length.
7. You know the **basic trade dimensions**: thicknesses 25/32/45/50/70/80 mm, widths 100/120/150/200 mm, lengths 2.5/3/4/5/6 m. You know that the nominal dimension = the dimension after drying, and the green (fresh) dimension is 5–8% larger to allow for shrinkage.

## Content

### The breakdown chain: from stemwood to board in five steps

Before a board reaches the customer, the log passes through 5 machine stages. Each stage produces a different assortment, which can be either a **final product** (sold to the customer) or a **semi-finished product** (passed to the next machine).

::: info
**5 steps of breaking down an oak log 4 m × 50 cm**:
1. **Crosscutting** on the log yard: stemwood 14 m → three nominal 4 m logs. Tool: chainsaw or mini crosscut gang.
2. **Debarking** (optional): the log passes through a debarker that strips off the bark. Purpose: protecting the saw band (bark contains sand) and keeping the chips clean (chips with bark fetch a lower price).
3. **Lengthwise cutting on the band saw**: the log rolls onto the table, the headrig cuts it lengthwise. The first kerf = **outer slab** (front slab). Subsequent kerfs = **unedged boards** of a width depending on the log profile. The last kerf = **back slab**. The middle can be kept as a **cant** (a rectangle for further processing) or a **half-sawn log** (a half-round for rustic applications).
4. **Edging on the edger**: unedged boards go to the *edger*, which uses two circular saws to trim the natural sides. Output: **edged lumber** (four straight edges) + **waste strips** (*edgings*, thin strips 10–30 mm wide).
5. **Crosscutting to length**: edged boards go to the crosscut saw, which trims them to the ordered length (2.5 / 3 / 4 / 5 / 6 m). End waste: **trim ends**, 5–15 cm, for firewood or chipping.
:::

**Output proportions**: from an oak log 4 m × 50 cm (volume about 0.78 m³) a typical sawmill obtains **50–65% edged lumber** (0.40–0.50 m³), **10–15% cant or unedged lumber** (0.08–0.12 m³), **10–20% slabs and strips** (0.08–0.16 m³), the rest being **chips and sawdust** (0.08–0.12 m³, for pellets or bedding). The sawmill earns on edged lumber, subsidizes itself on slabs and sawdust, and loses on trim ends.

### Assortment one: slab

Carlos stands before stack no. 1. He sees boards with a strange profile: **one side flat (from the cut), the other rounded with bark** (from the natural edge of the trunk). Thickness is uneven: 30 mm in the middle, 5–10 mm at the sides.

A **slab** (Polish: *oblina*, English: *slab*, German: *Schwarte*) is the first and last piece of a log after lengthwise cutting on a band saw. It has **only one flat side**, the one the saw passed through. The other side retains the natural arc of the trunk and the bark (if the log was not debarked). Its length equals the length of the log (2.5–6 m), its width varies from 0 at the ends to maximum in the middle.

::: tip
**Two slabs from one log**: the outer slab (front slab, from the first kerf) and the back slab (from the last kerf before the cant or the next board is separated). A typical 4 m × 50 cm log yields 50–70 kg of slabs in total, about 0.05–0.10 m³.
:::

**Where slabs go**:
- **Firewood** (homes, biomass boilers): 80–120 zł/m³. Pine is better than oak (higher calorific value per unit of green mass, oak dries more slowly).
- **Packaging boards, pallets**: after edging the sides and cutting out the middle. 200–350 zł/m³.
- **Energy chips** (*wood chips*): shredded in a chipper. 150–200 zł/m³ loose, 300–400 zł/m³ packaged.
- **Rustic furniture, natural countertops**: recently there has been a fashion for tables with a "live edge" (*live edge*): a wide oak or elm slab, after sanding and oiling, costs 500–1500 zł per piece. But it is a niche market.

**The helper's role with slabs**: you pick up the slab from the band saw outfeed with a special handle (rollers + hook), place it on a transport cart, and take it to the "valuable waste" zone (not the "throw-away waste" zone). **Never treat a slab like trash**: every cubic meter of uncollected slab is 100–200 zł of sawmill revenue lost. If you see a slab kicked aside on the floor, carry it back to the designated stack; the foreman will thank you rather than scold you for meddling.

### Assortment two: half-sawn log

Carlos walks up to stack no. 2. He sees thick pieces of wood **with a half-round cross-section**: one side flat (a cut through the middle of the log), the other rounded with bark (what is left of the trunk on the other side). Thickness in the middle 20–25 cm, width 30–50 cm, length 4 m.

A **half-sawn log** (Polish: *opół*, English: *half-sawn log*, German: *Halbholz*) is a log cut **through the middle into two equal halves**. It arises when the band saw makes only a single kerf through the log's axis, with no further cutting. The half-sawn log has one flat side (the kerf) and one natural side (with bark or after debarking).

**Applications**:
- **Rustic construction**: a ceiling beam in a stylish timber house, an element of a garden shelter, a terrace sleeper. The customer values the "natural look".
- **Semi-finished stock for cutting into smaller pieces**: the half-sawn log returns to the saw and is cut into edged boards. In that case a side slab remains, but it is already smaller than from a full log.
- **Sleepers, frames for agricultural machinery**: an oak half-sawn log 4 m × 50 cm half-round is 200–250 kg of construction material, hard, cheap.

**Price of an oak half-sawn log**: 500–800 zł/m³ (between unedged board grade B and edged grade B). In ash and elm, the half-sawn log is sometimes sought by artisan joiners for natural countertops, and a particularly wide specimen with an interesting grain can fetch a premium far above the standard band.

### Assortment three: cant

Stack no. 3 is something different: **four straight sides, a 90° angle on each, a rectangular cross-section**. Length 4 m, width 40 cm, height 35 cm. The wood is fresh, cream-coloured, with visible growth lines on the end grain.

A **cant** (Polish: *pryzma*, English: *cant*, German: *Scherblock*) is a **log squared down to a rectangular or square cross-section**, a semi-finished product before final cutting into boards. A cant is produced when the band saw makes 4 kerfs on the log (two vertical ones removing the side slabs, two horizontal ones removing the top and bottom), leaving a rectangular core.

::: warning
**A cant is not a final product for most customers.** It goes to a second headrig (a frame saw or a multi-rip saw), which will cut it in one pass into 6–12 boards of a set thickness (25 / 32 / 45 mm). From a cant 40×35×400 cm you get 12 boards 25 mm × 40 cm × 4 m = 4.8 m² of edged lumber.

**Why sell cants separately**: specialty customers (veneer mills, bent-laminated producers) prefer to buy the cant so they can **decide the thickness and direction of the final cut themselves**. A veneer-grade oak cant (40×40×400 cm from a grade A oak butt log): **2000–2500 zł/m³**, three to four times more than edged lumber from the same wood.
:::

**Cant price by species and grade**:

| Species + grade | Typical size | Indicative price (2025) |
|---|---|---|
| Oak grade A (veneer) | 40×40×300+ cm | 2000–2500 zł/m³ |
| Oak grade B | 35×35×300 cm | 1200–1600 zł/m³ |
| Beech grade A | 35×35×300 cm | 1000–1400 zł/m³ |
| Structural pine | 30×30×400 cm | 500–700 zł/m³ |

### Assortments four and five: unedged and edged lumber

Carlos looks at stacks 4 and 5 next to each other. Stack 4: **boards with flat top and bottom, but natural sides**: wide in the middle, tapering at the ends, with bark traces on the sides. Stack 5: **the same boards, but with four straight edges**: each has a perfect rectangular cross-section.

**Unedged lumber** (*unedged lumber*) = a board after the band saw that **has not been through the edger**. Top and bottom are flat (from the saw), the sides are natural (the trunk profile). The width varies along the board: 35 cm at one end, 25 cm in the middle, 35 cm at the other end again.

**Where it goes**: a **furniture joinery**. The joiner prefers to buy unedged lumber (20–30% cheaper per m³) because **they cut elements of optimal shape out of it themselves** (a furniture element can be slightly wedge-shaped and still fit the design). The natural sides also give a rustic floorboard its character.

**Edged lumber** (*edged lumber*) = a board after the edger, **four straight edges**, 90° angle. Constant width along the entire length (for example 150 mm everywhere). Standard dimensions: 100/120/150/200 mm width, 25/32/45/50 mm thickness.

**Where it goes**: **construction** (rafters, battens, soffits), **floorboards**, **export lumber** (Germany and the UK require edged stock). The construction customer wants a standard so that two boards fit together without fitting, and a contractor placing an order for 500 running meters expects every piece to match the nominal width to the millimetre. This is why edged lumber commands the highest price among raw sawmill products and why it dominates sawmill revenue.

::: example
**Price comparison from one pine log 4 m × 35 cm**:

| Assortment | Quantity | Price/m³ | Value |
|---|---|---|---|
| Edged lumber grade B | 0.18 m³ | 900 zł | 162 zł |
| Unedged lumber | 0.05 m³ | 650 zł | 32 zł |
| Slab | 0.04 m³ | 180 zł | 7 zł |
| Edger strips | 0.02 m³ | 200 zł | 4 zł |
| Sawdust + chips | 0.05 m³ | 100 zł | 5 zł |
| **Total from the log** | **0.34 m³** | | **210 zł** |

For a log bought in the forest at 300 zł/m³ (volume 0.38 m³ with bark = 114 zł in purchase cost) the sawmill obtains 210 zł. Gross margin 96 zł minus breakdown costs (band, electricity, labour) ~50 zł = **45 zł net profit per log**. One worker processes 60 logs per day.
:::

### How you know which assortment comes from which machine

Carlos sorts it out in his head:
- **Band saw (main headrig)**: log → **unedged boards + 2 slabs + optionally a cant**.
- **Edger** (*edger*): unedged boards → **edged boards + waste strips**.
- **Frame saw / multi-rip saw**: cant → **a package of edged boards of identical thickness** in one pass through 6–12 saws.
- **Crosscut saw**: a long board → **a board of the ordered length + end trims**.

The helper must understand this chain in order to know which board they are carrying and where. The foreman redirects logs between machines depending on the day's orders.

## Trainer notes

**Why exactly five assortments**: pragmatic selection. The full trade classification of lumber distinguishes 15+ assortments (batten, squared timber, strip, panelling, calibrated element, frieze, joist, rafter stock, and so on), but all of them are **variants of edged lumber of specific dimensions**. The fundamental assortments are **slab / half-sawn log / cant / edged / unedged**, because they define the **geometry of the cross-section and the degree of processing**. Dimensions are the second axis of classification (size), grade is the third (quality). These three together (assortment + dimension + grade) give the full price line.

**Guiding analogy**: an assortment is like a "cut of meat in a slaughterhouse". The same bull gives tenderloin (the most expensive, requires precise cutting), ribeye, loin, shoulder, brisket, and scrap for sausage. A sawmill assortment is analogous: an oak butt log for veneer (the tenderloin analogue), a structural cant (ribeye), grade B edged lumber (loin), unedged lumber (shoulder), slab (brisket), sawdust (scrap for sausage/pellet). Trainees from countries with a developed butchery tradition (Ukraine, Colombia, Venezuela, most of Latin America) grasp this analogy instantly.

**Where this lesson returns**: m2-w2-l6 grades A/B/C/D (quality, the third axis), m2-w2-l7 saws (which saw for which assortment), m2-w2-l8 band changing (band wear depends on the assortment: cutting an oak cant dulls the saw 3× faster than cutting a pine slab), m2-w3-l2 band saw in motion, m2-w3-l5 edger, m2-w4-l2 sorting and stacking finished lumber.

**Pitfalls for migrant trainees**:
- "Lumber = board": **not necessarily**. Lumber is a collective term for everything after lengthwise cutting (boards + beams + strips + slabs). A board is a specific assortment with the proportions thickness < width < length. Trainees from countries where the sawmill vocabulary has not historically developed (parts of Latin America) often use a single word corresponding to "cut wood" and do not distinguish.
- Slab "as waste to throw away": **no**, a slab is an 80–350 zł/m³ product. In countries without developed wood-waste management this is a hard mental change.
- Cant "confused with a beam": a cant is a semi-finished product (it goes to further cutting), a beam is a final product (for a rafter). Same geometry (40×40×400 cm), but different purpose. A cant has fresh colour and is not dried; a beam is dried to <20% and carries a CE C24/C30 marking.
- Edged lumber vs. floorboard: edged lumber is raw lumber with 4 straight edges, a floorboard is edged lumber after planing, jointing, and possibly with a tongue-and-groove profile.

**Assortment economics**: the sawmill earns mainly on edged lumber grade A/B (60–70% of revenue), little on slabs and chips (5–10%), but without them it would go bankrupt (it would have to pay for disposal). Every assortment has its role in the plant's microeconomy.

A sawmill is a visual world, a verbal description is not enough. A 45-minute walk through the hall after this lesson is the most valuable part of the course.
