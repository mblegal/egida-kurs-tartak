## Introduction

Monday, 7:00, Wood-Mizer LT40 bandsaw number two. The same machine on which, a week ago, Sofía assisted Tadeusz with a band change. The band is fresh – Silvertip 51 × 1.07 mm, fitted on Friday, two full days of work on pine behind it, still good for oak. Foreman Marek is standing next to the control panel, holding in his left hand a card with the five-point checklist from lesson 4 of week 1 of module two. Next to him, with gloves taken off and tucked into his vest pocket (no gloves near rotating parts, lesson 2 of week 1), stands a new junior operator: **Oleksij Bohdaniuk, 28 years old, a Ukrainian from Kharkiv**. Four years of work as a carpenter in a furniture workshop in his home city, handling hand saws and a small bench jigsaw, but **his first time at an industrial bandsaw**. He has been in Poland for three months, at the Fundacja EGIDA sawmill for three weeks: weeks 1 and 2 of module two are behind him (occupational safety, PPE, the five-point ritual, moisture, defects, grades, band change as an assistant). Today, the first thing he does **on his own, but under supervision**: he starts up the bandsaw following the six-step procedure.

This lesson is the **first independent operator action** in the whole of module two. Until now, you have been watching, measuring, reporting, handing over tools. Now **you press buttons in a defined sequence**, you observe the machine with your own eyes and ears, you make the first independent decision: "I start it up" or "STOP, something is wrong". Marek stands two metres from you the entire time, not in order to do it for you, but in order to show you what you noticed and what you missed. The week 3 mantra: **"I start, I adjust, I cut, I sort – but always under the foreman's or a senior's supervision"**.

Starting up the bandsaw is a **six-step procedure**, which today you perform for the first time. Each step has one purpose and one "pass / fail" indicator. You do not improvise the order. You do not skip a step even if "it looks like it is OK". The order was worked out from dozens of accidents and thousands of safe run-ups: every step that seems redundant has, at some point, saved someone.

## Objectives

After this lesson:

1. You know the **six steps of the start-up procedure** for the bandsaw in a fixed, irreversible order: guards → band (visual inspection, no adjustment) → main switch and STOP test → 30-second no-load run → observation of the band by ear and eye → only then material (test cut on a scrap board). Skipping a step breaks the mechanical protection built into the procedure.
2. You apply the **"left hand on STOP before right hand on START"** rule from lesson 3 of week 1: before you press the green start button, your other hand is touching the red emergency mushroom. This is a mechanical habit, not a symbolic one: at the sound of the first irregularity you stop the machine within a fraction of a second.
3. You know that the **30-second no-load run** (a trade number from lesson 8 of week 2) is a diagnostic window: eighty per cent of hidden faults reveal themselves in the first ten seconds. You do not shorten it, even when the machine "sounds fine right away". Authorization for independent start-up without foreman supervision comes after 10–20 repetitions under Marek's eye, usually after 2–4 weeks of daily work.
4. You can recognise the **five reasons for immediate STOP** during start-up and in the first seconds of work: a foreign sound (squeal, knocking, rumble), a foreign smell (burning, smoke), vibration stronger than the standard, a foreign object in the cutting zone, a signal from the foreman or a colleague. Each of these reasons = left hand on STOP, no discussion, no "I'll wait a moment".

## Content

::: info
At the Fundacja EGIDA sawmill, the six-step procedure takes **90–120 seconds** for an experienced operator (3 minutes for a junior in the first weeks). It is carried out in the morning at the first start-up and after every break longer than 15 minutes (material change, band change, breakdown, breakfast break). Shorter breaks do not require the full procedure: step 4 is enough (a 10-second control no-load run).
:::

### Six steps of the procedure – what you do, what you see, what you hear

**Step 1: Guards.** You approach the bandsaw from the operator side (right side of the machine, on the control-panel side). You visually inspect two guards: the **upper** one (above the upper tensioner wheel, about 900 mm wide) and the **lower** one (above the lower driven wheel, about 700 mm). You check: (1) both guards are in place, (2) all four M8 bolts of each guard are tightened (a quick touch with your finger: you are not turning them, only feeling that they are fully seated), (3) there are no visible slackness, cracks or bent sheet metal. If a guard is removed or loose, **you do not start up**; you report it to the foreman. Without a guard, a bandsaw that suffers a band break ejects a fragment of steel at up to 50 m/s, which means an amputation or a penetrating chest wound.

**Step 2: Band – visual inspection without adjustment.** You look through the inspection window in the guard (a 100 × 60 mm Plexiglas window on the right side of each guard: you do not open the guard, you only look through the window). You observe three things: (1) **the band is on the wheels** (not slipped off, not sagging), (2) **there is no visible crack** in the area you can see (rotating the band by hand using the outer wheel – only if Marek shows you how to do it safely), (3) **the pre-tension looks right** (the band does not sag loosely, but also does not look like a string under excessive pull – a visual assessment, not a gauge reading; the gauge belongs to the step from lesson 8 of week 2, band change, not to daily start-up). **You do not adjust** the tension, you do not touch the tensioner, you do not open the guard: visual inspection from the outside. Adjusting the guide rollers and tension is the topic of lesson 2 of this week.

**Step 3: Main switch and STOP test.** The main switch of the bandsaw is a **grey-and-green box on the left side of the control panel**; the handle in the ON position (vertical, pointing up) means that power is supplied to the machine. You check that the handle is in ON, that there is no LOTO padlock on it bearing the name of another operator, that the "DO NOT OPERATE" tag from the previous band change has been removed (Tadeusz closed the procedure in lesson 8 of week 2). Next comes the **STOP functional test**: you place your left hand on the red emergency mushroom (on the left side of the control panel, above the main switch). The mushroom must be in the **extended** position (ready to be pressed): if it is pressed in from a previous breakdown, you must release it by turning it clockwise. Touching the mushroom is not symbolic – **you check that it protrudes to its full height of 30 mm** (a locked-in mushroom only protrudes 10 mm). This second of contact with the STOP button is a mechanical habit: your left hand knows the position of the mushroom **before** the right hand reaches for START.

**Step 4: START and a 30-second no-load run.** The left hand stays on the STOP button. With your right hand you press the green START button on the panel. You announce aloud, so your colleagues in the hall can hear: **"Starting up bandsaw number two, thirty-second no-load run!"**. The band accelerates (2-second run-up to the working speed of 30–50 m/s); you hear the rising whine of the motor and the whistle of the band cutting through air. You look at the second counter on the panel (or at a watch) and you count out 30 seconds of full no-load running **without touching the material**. During those 30 seconds: you stand still, left hand on STOP, right hand lowered, eyes on the band (through the inspection window) and ears on the motor. You do nothing – you only observe.

::: tip
Trainer: "Thirty seconds is how long the gearbox, the bearings and the guide rollers need to enter their normal working rhythm. The first ten seconds detect eighty per cent of sudden failures – a cracked bearing starts squealing immediately, a poorly tensioned band starts beating the rollers from the first second. The next twenty seconds detect warm-up issues – a tight bearing starts to heat up gradually, a poorly set guide roller shows vibration after 15–20 seconds. Shortening the no-load run to 10 seconds looks like saving time. It is not a saving: it is giving up 20 seconds of the diagnostic window."
:::

**Step 5: Observation – sight and sound.** After the 30 seconds of no-load running **you do not yet press anything**. You stand at the panel for another 5 seconds and you assess the machine with three senses: (1) **sound** – a steady whine, no squeal, no knocking, no rumble; (2) **vibration** – the panel under your right hand does not tremble more than usual (vibration transmitted through the machine structure is the most frequent symptom of a poorly set roller); (3) **smell** – the air has no scent of scorching (a burnt bearing smells like burnt rubber, a burnt electric motor smells like ozone with burnt lacquer). If all three senses say "OK", you move on to step 6. If even one says "something is wrong", **left hand on STOP, no discussion**; you report to Marek.

**Step 6: Only then the material – a test cut.** The last step before production cutting is a **test cut on a scrap board**. The test board lies in a separate crate marked "FOR TESTING" (pine, 25 × 150 × 500 mm, not oak – oak is more expensive and reserved for production). You pick up the board with leather gloves (gloves only for handling wood **before** the machine; you take them off before you get within arm's reach of the band; no gloves near rotating parts, from lesson 2 of week 1). You feed the board onto the working table of the bandsaw **slowly**, a first cut of 30–50 cm into the depth of the board, then you pull back. You check the surface of the cut: smooth (like grit 120 sandpaper), no waviness, no dark burn streaks. If the test cut comes out well, the bandsaw is ready for production; you call Marek: "Test cut OK, please authorise for cutting". If the cut has waviness or burns, you stop the bandsaw, you report to Marek, you do not correct it yourself (parameter adjustment is lesson 3 of this week).

::: warning
**Five reasons for immediate STOP** during the first 30 seconds of work after start-up: (1) a foreign sound (bearing squeal, loose-roller knocking, rumble from a poorly balanced wheel); (2) a foreign smell (burning, smoke, sharp scent of burnt insulation); (3) vibration stronger than normal (you will know the norm after 20–30 start-ups; a junior in the first week asks Marek: "Is this normal vibration?"); (4) a foreign object in the cutting zone or on the work table (a rag, a wrench, a sheet of paper – every object pulled in by the band is ejected at 30–50 m/s); (5) a signal from the foreman or a colleague (a raised hand, a shout of "STOP", the hall's acoustic signal). Each of these reasons = **left hand on the mushroom within a fraction of a second**, no analysis, no "I'll wait, maybe it will pass". After stopping, you analyse with Marek, not with yourself.
:::

### The foreman's role during the first start-ups

Marek stands two metres from you for the whole procedure. **He does not take over**. He does not say "now step two, now step three": he knows your memory, he trusts it. He comments **after the sequence is finished**, not during. If he notices a safety-critical mistake (for example, forgetting the STOP test in step 3), he stops you with the words "Stop, go back to step three". If he notices a minor mistake (for example, rotating the band too quickly during the visual inspection), he notes it and tells you once the whole procedure is complete.

Authorization for independent start-up without supervision comes after **10–20 repetitions** under Marek's eye, usually 2–4 weeks of daily work for a junior with a craft background (like Oleksij), or 4–6 weeks for a junior with no prior contact with industrial machines. Marek does not give authorization "because a week has gone by"; he gives it when he sees that your hands move through the six steps without hesitation, without a question, without an error of order. It is not a question of time, it is a question of **repeatability**.

## Link to practice

**Exercise 1 (first start-ups under supervision, daily during the first week):** Every morning during the first week at the workstation you carry out the six-step procedure under Marek's eye. Marek notes in the observation notebook the pace (target: 90–120 seconds after 10 repetitions) and the number of order errors (target: zero after 15 repetitions). You do not grade yourself: Marek grades.

**Exercise 2 (order test, 30 seconds a day):** After the end of the first week, Marek asks once a day: "Give me the six steps". You answer from memory, without notes, without hesitation, within 30 seconds. Three seconds per step. If you hesitate, you must repeat the whole sequence from the beginning. After a week of this exercise, the six steps become an **occupational reflex**, not academic knowledge.

**Exercise 3 (test of the five STOP reasons, once a week):** Once a week during the first month, Marek asks: "What are the five reasons for an immediate STOP during start-up?". You answer: sound, smell, vibration, foreign object, signal. Five reasons, five seconds, zero hesitation. The foreman extends the question: "What does a foreign sound mean specifically?"; then you give examples (squeal, knocking, rumble). This exercise tells apart the operator who **knows** from the operator who **can perform it under pressure**.

## Trainer notes

**Pedagogical goal**: the first independent operator action in M2. Transition from observer (T2) to performer under supervision (T3). The key: **six steps in an irreversible order** as mechanical protection built into the sequence. The junior is to learn the order, not the philosophy of every step (that will come through repetitions and questions from the foreman).

**Decision to use a shortened procedure (6 steps instead of 8–10)**: panel details (buttons, lights, counters, mode selection) have been deliberately omitted. Reason: this is a training lesson, not a Wood-Mizer LT40 operating manual. Panel details are learned through 10–20 start-ups under Marek's eye, not through memorising text. The five STOP reasons are introduced as a warning box in step 6, not as a separate expanded section: a conscious decision in favour of a compact procedure.

**Protagonist Oleksij Bohdaniuk**: a Ukrainian from Kharkiv, ex-carpenter, craft background (furniture workshop, hand saws). Culturally: Eastern European craftsmanlike modesty, "I repeat, I do not invent", matches the spirit of week 3, in which the trainee performs procedures for the first time rather than improvising. Selected in line with sociological realism (about 1.5 million Ukrainians in Poland in 2025–2026, the largest migrant group in Polish sawmills). Expansion of the national base along the Colombian line (Sofía T2 Block 1+3) and the Venezuelan line (Carlos T2 Block 2).

**Week 3 mantra**: "I start, I adjust, I cut, I sort – but always under the foreman's or a senior's supervision". Introduced in l1, returns in l2 (guide-roller adjustment), l3 (cutting parameters), l4 (other machines), l5 (edger, planer), l6 (sorting), l7–l8 (drying). Repeating the mantra in every lesson builds the pedagogical rhythm of the week.

**PIP statistics 2019–2023**: shortening the procedure (start-up without band inspection, feeding the material before the 30 s no-load run has elapsed, skipping the test cut) accounts for **29% of accidents during start-up of sawmill bandsaws**. Not so much because of the omitted activity as because of the loss of the checking rhythm: the person who starts skipping loses the ability to notice what they have skipped. In addition, **7% of accidents** involve an unreleased mushroom after a previous breakdown (the operator pressed START, the machine did not run up, the operator opened the guard, the tensioned band jumped out); hence the STOP test in step 3 is not a formality.

**Facilitation traps**:
1. **Do not rush the junior**. The 90–120 s target for the procedure is for an experienced operator. A junior in the first week has the right to 3 minutes. Pressure on pace in the first week = loss of safety in the second.
2. **Do not give authorization too soon**. The criterion is not time but repeatability without hesitation. A junior who, when asked "step three?", answers "uhh… main switch?" after two weeks needs one more week, even if he "looks confident".

**Integration with earlier lessons**: l2 T1 M2 (no gloves near rotating parts) → step 6 (you take off gloves before getting close to the band). l3 T1 M2 (STOP > START, the mushroom) → step 3 (functional test). l4 T1 M2 (five-point checklist) → steps 1–3 are its practical application. l7 T1 M2 (5-step breakdown protocol STOP → WAIT → ANCHOR → REPORT → INVESTIGATE) → a reaction to each of the 5 STOP reasons in the warning box. l8 T2 M2 (band change, 30 s no-load run) → step 4 applies the same trade number to daily start-up.

**Preview of l2 and l3 of this week**: l2 introduces band-guide-roller adjustment (3–4 mm from lesson 8 of week 2 as a trade parameter; now the trainee sets it themselves under supervision). l3 introduces cutting parameters and feed rate: the first lesson in which the trainee matches the machine to the material, not only to themselves. Block 1 of week 3 = the full triad "switch on – adjust – cut" for the bandsaw, the heart of the Polish sawmill.
