## Introduction

Tuesday, 7:30 a.m., the EGIDA Foundation kiln hall – a separate building 20 m from the sawing hall, with six Nardi-type convection drying chambers (capacity 25 m³ each) and one Brunner-Hildebrand dehumidification chamber (capacity 18 m³). Oleksij Bohdaniuk walks in with Marek through the double-sealed steel door. Marek says: "Chamber three has been empty since Friday, today we are loading your pine batch SO-A-260420-012 from yesterday plus two more batches. You are the loader, not the kiln operator – kiln master Zbigniew sets the program, you stack the boards, check that the stickers are in position, close the door and report the closure. The rest happens without you for 4 days."

Kiln drying is the **final production stage** in a sawmill before shipment – the moment when green sawn timber (50-80% MC, from lesson 1 of week 2 of module 2) becomes commercial lumber (12-15% MC for export, 8-12% MC for the furniture industry). At the EGIDA Foundation sawmill, drying accounts for 35-40% of timber production cost – more than sawing, planing and sorting combined. The chamber operates 24 hours a day, 7 days a week, and consumes 2.5-4 kWh per 1 m³ of water evaporated. A **loading error** (wrong sticker, wrong gap, wrong label) equals a batch defect worth 3000-8000 PLN, impossible to repair once the batch leaves the kiln.

The week 3 mantra from lesson 1 reads: "I start, I adjust, I cut, I sort – but always under the supervision of a foreman or a senior." In lesson 7 we add three more activities: **I load, I close, I report** – but **I do not program** drying cycles (that is done by kiln master Zbigniew Majchrzak, 15 years of experience, E1 electrical qualifications). Your role as a loader ends the moment the door is closed and the control panel is handed over to the master.

## Objectives

After this lesson:

1. You distinguish the **two species categories** from a drying perspective: **softwoods** (pine, spruce) tolerate temperatures of 35-70 °C and have resin canals that help release vapour, while **hardwoods** (oak, beech) only tolerate 30-60 °C due to higher density and susceptibility to deformation. You know the risk of **resin** in pine (softens at 70 °C, flows at 80 °C – hence the upper limit) and the risk of **warping** in oak and beech (bow, twist, cup at excessive temperature).
2. You know the three **drying cycle phases** programmed by the master: (a) **warm-up** (35 °C, 2-3 days, air humidity 85-90% – a gentle introduction of the wood into the chamber climate), (b) **main drying** (50-56 °C for oak and beech, 50-70 °C for pine, humidity gradually lowered to 40-50%, 3-14 days depending on species and thickness), (c) **cooling** (a maximum of **6 °C per hour** – faster cooling causes **surface checks** in the outer layer).
3. You build a batch with **stickers** (drying battens) 20-25 mm thick, spaced **every 600-800 mm along the board length** (3000 mm board = 4-5 stickers, 5000 mm board = 7 stickers). Stickers go **one directly above the other, vertically** – every stack has "columns" of stickers from base to top, never shifted. A shifted sticker equals deformation of the board at that point under the load of the stack.
4. You keep the **three geometric gaps** critical for circulation: (a) between stacks inside the chamber **150 mm** (airflow passes through at 2-5 m/s), (b) between the stack and the chamber wall **100 mm** (no contact, otherwise a cold spot), (c) between the top board of the stack and the chamber ceiling **300 mm** (return airflow). An incorrect gap means uneven drying, with the core of the batch still wet and the outer layer already overdried.
5. You know typical **cycle times** for sawmill work planning: 25 mm pine = **4-6 days**, 50 mm pine = **10-14 days**, 70 mm pine = **14-18 days**, 25 mm oak = **14-18 days**, 50 mm oak = **18-25 days**, 100 mm oak beam = **28-42 days** (3-6 weeks). You understand that these times are planning figures – the kiln master adjusts them to the initial moisture of the raw material (green sawn timber at 70% MC needs longer than air-dry timber at 25% MC).

## Content

**Why the kiln loader is a position that demands discipline rather than engineering knowledge.** You do not need to know vapour-flow thermodynamics, you do not need to set the program on the Siemens S7-1200 controller, you do not need to calculate the chamber energy balance. You must: (1) know the geometric parameters of the stack (stickers, gaps), (2) know the batch codes and be able to transfer them from the sorting-yard label to the kiln charge label, (3) close the door mechanically and confirm the seal through the viewing port, (4) report the closure to the master both orally and in writing in the kiln logbook. Those are four operational tasks repeated daily for every chamber. A mistake in any of them is not an "art of the craft" error, it is a procedural error – it can be traced, the responsible person identified and the procedure corrected.

### Pine and spruce – softwoods, resin in the kiln

**Tolerance temperatures**: Scots pine (Pinus sylvestris) tolerates **35-70 °C** during the main phase; Norway spruce (Picea abies) likewise 35-70 °C. The lower limit of 35 °C is not a physical floor for wood, it is a **biological floor**: below 35 °C, with wood moisture above 20% MC inside the chamber, **blue stain** (the Ceratocystis fungus) and **mould** begin to develop – colonies penetrate 2-5 mm deep into the wood, weaken strength, leave blue-grey streaks and permanently downgrade the class (A to C automatically). The upper limit of 70 °C is the **resin limit**: pine resin melts at 62-65 °C, softens and becomes sticky at 70 °C, flows at 80 °C, seeps to the board surface creating **resin bleed** (a post-drying defect class). That is why kiln master Zbigniew programs pine at a maximum of **65 °C during the main phase** – he keeps a 5 °C margin from the flow threshold.

**Phases for 50 mm pine (example batch SO-A-260420-012)**: **warm-up** 35 °C for 48 h (2 days) at 88% air humidity, **main drying** 55 °C (air humidity lowered from 75% to 45% at a rate of 2% per day) for 168 h (7 days), **cooling** from 55 °C to 25 °C at 5 °C/h over 6 h. Total cycle: **just over 10 days**. A finished SO-A pine batch after 10 days: moisture from 65% (green) down to 12% (commercial), mass reduced by 53% (100 kg of green sawn timber becomes 47 kg dry). Resins stabilised, colour light yellow slightly darkened, knots without bleed.

**Resin risk – how to recognise an exceeded limit after a cycle**: if you open the chamber after the cycle and see **amber seepage** on the knots or on the board surface (sticky, smelling of turpentine), the temperature crossed 75-80 °C and resin flowed. This is not your fault (the master did the programming), but it is your job to report it – a batch with resin bleed needs extra sanding or reclassification. You report it to Marek orally and write it into the kiln logbook ("resin bleed, approximately 15% of boards in the batch").

**Spruce example**: spruce has less resin than pine (a species "low in resin canals"), the risk of seepage only appears at 80-85 °C. Because of this the master sometimes programs spruce at 68-70 °C (higher than pine), with a cycle 10-15% shorter. But as a loader you **see no difference** in loading – stickers, gaps, labelling are identical for pine and spruce.

::: warning
**Never open the chamber during a cycle.** Opening the door of a chamber running in the main drying phase (55 °C, 50% humidity) equals an immediate equalisation with the hall atmosphere (20 °C, 70%), which equals **thermal shock** for the batch, which equals mass surface checking and a lost batch. If the master tells you to check something inside a running chamber, you refuse directly and ask for the cycle to be paused for 30 min to equalise. Pausing the cycle costs 200-400 PLN in energy, opening a chamber in progress costs 3000-8000 PLN in lost batch value.
:::

### Oak and beech – hardwoods, no resin but prone to deformation

**Tolerance temperatures**: pedunculate oak (Quercus robur) tolerates **30-60 °C**, European beech (Fagus sylvatica) likewise 30-60 °C. The upper limit is **10 °C lower** than for pine, not because of resin (oak and beech contain almost none) but because of **anatomical structure**. Oak has medullary rays 0.5-2 mm wide (silvery bands visible in section) – at excessive temperatures they contract faster than the surrounding earlywood, creating **ray checks** (microcracks 20-50 mm long, 0.2-1 mm wide). Beech has diffuse-porous wood that is very dense (650-720 kg/m³) and releases moisture slowly – at above 60 °C the surface dries faster than the core, creating **desorption stresses** that exceed the tensile strength of the wood across the grain, resulting in **deformations** (longitudinal bow, diagonal twist, transverse cup).

**Phases for 50 mm oak (furniture grade)**: **warm-up** 30 °C for 72 h (3 days) at 92% air humidity (higher than for pine – the wood must slowly release its initial moisture, otherwise it cracks), **main drying** 45-55 °C (first 10 days at 45 °C to avoid surface checks, next 8-10 days at 55 °C to reach the 8% target moisture) for 432 h (18 days), **cooling** from 55 °C to 25 °C at 4 °C/h over 7.5 h. Total cycle: **22-25 days**. **More than double** the time for pine of the same thickness. Oak for furniture (target moisture 8%) rather than for construction (15%) requires an additional 4-5 days in the final low-temperature phase (40 °C).

**Beech has a specific risk of internal honeycomb checks**: with uneven cooling (faster than 6 °C/h), the interior of a beech board splits along the grain, 20-100 mm deep, **invisible from the outside**. It is only detected after the board is cross-cut or under an acoustic microscope (ultrasound scanner). A beech batch with honeycomb checks looks perfect but fails the moment the customer puts it to use – the worst category of complaint. That is why Zbigniew always programs beech cooling at **4 °C/h**, keeping a safety margin from the 6 °C/h threshold.

**Hardwood cycle times** for planning purposes: 25 mm oak = **14-18 days** (targeting 12% for construction) or **18-22 days** (targeting 8% for furniture), 50 mm oak = **22-28 days**, 75 mm oak = **35-45 days**, 25 mm beech = **12-16 days**, 50 mm beech = **20-25 days**. A 100 mm oak beam for heritage construction (restoration work) needs **42-56 days** – nearly two months in the chamber, with the chamber producing a single batch for that entire period, which has planning consequences: the EGIDA Foundation sawmill has only 7 chambers, and every 100 mm oak beam locks up one chamber for one-sixth of a year.

**Planning example for one week at the EGIDA Foundation kiln hall (4 convection chambers active in week 16 of 2026)**. Chamber 1: 25 mm pine, batches SO-A and SO-B (20 m³), start Monday, end Friday (5 days). Chamber 2: 50 mm pine, batch SO-A-260415-008 (15 m³), start last Wednesday, end this Wednesday (10 days). ### Stickers, gaps, circulation – stack geometry you control

Kiln stickers (called "stickers" in English, "listewki" in Polish practice) are dry pine or spruce battens (moisture below 15%) with a cross-section of **20 x 25 mm** or **25 x 25 mm**, 1200-1500 mm long (the width of the timber stack). Their functions: (a) a **gap** between boards for airflow, (b) an **even support point** along the full length of the board, (c) a **load-transfer point** for the upper layers of the stack (a board under a sticker carries the load, a board between stickers hangs free).

**Placement**: the first sticker is **150 mm from the end of the board**, each subsequent one every **600-800 mm** along the board. A 3000 mm board: stickers at 150, 750, 1500, 2250, 2850 mm (5 pieces). A 5000 mm board: stickers at 150, 800, 1600, 2400, 3200, 4000, 4850 mm (7 pieces). A 2500 mm board (the shortest length sorted at EGIDA): stickers at 150, 850, 1650, 2350 mm (4 pieces).

**The "vertical columns" rule**: stickers in the stack must sit **perfectly one above the other** from the base to the top of the stack. If on layer 1 a sticker is at 750 mm and on layer 2 it is at 720 mm (a 30 mm shift), layer 2 has a **zone with no support** above sticker 1 and an **overloaded zone** between stickers. The board in layer 2 will bow in the unsupported zone (a 5-15 mm bow after 10 days in the chamber), a dimensional defect after drying. You check the columns visually: standing at the side of the stack, stickers should form **vertical lines** from base to top. If a line is broken, you stop loading, pull the layer out and correct the sticker positions.

**Three gaps critical for circulation** (engineering parameters of the chamber that you have to respect geometrically):

- **Between stacks in the chamber: 150 mm** (airflow from the fans at 2-5 m/s). Less than 150 mm equals a turbulence zone equals uneven drying. More than 300 mm wastes chamber capacity and lowers cycle profitability.
- **Between stack and chamber wall: 100 mm** (thermal insulation, no contact). A stack touching the wall creates a **cold spot** (the metal wall conducts cooling away), with local mould and blue stain.
- **Between the top of the stack and the chamber ceiling: 300 mm** (return airflow from ceiling fans back to the lower circulation). A blocked return means the air only circulates through the lower layer, and the upper layers of the stack dry 30-50% slower.

### Batch labelling – sorting-yard codes transferred to the kiln

Every batch loaded into the chamber has **double labelling**: (a) the stack label (left over from the sorting yard in week 2 lesson 6, with a code like SO-A-260420-012), (b) the chamber label (a new one that you fit before closing the door, with the format: CHAMBER-NUMBER-BATCHCODES-STARTDATE). On the door of chamber 2 you hang a metal plate with the following data:

```
CHAMBER 2 - CYCLE ACTIVE
Start: 21.04.2026, 08:00
Dominant species: pine
Initial moisture: 65% MC
Batches:
  SO-A-260420-012 (6 m³, pine A 50x150x3000)
  SO-B-260420-013 (4 m³, pine B 50x150x3000)
  SO-A-260418-009 (5 m³, pine A 45x200x3000)
Total: 15 m³
Target: 12% MC / 10 days
Kiln master: Z. Majchrzak
Loader: O. Bohdaniuk
```

You fill in the plate with a pencil (not a pen – the chamber door heats up to 60 °C and pen ink runs). After writing you sign with your initials (O.B.) and hand it to the master for his signature (Z.M.). A double signature is the formal closure of the loader's responsibility and the beginning of the kiln master's responsibility.

::: tip
**Check three things before closing the door**: (1) the chamber door viewing port is clean (the master must see the measurement panel inside), (2) the door gasket is intact with no cuts (a leak means lost vapour and an inhomogeneous climate), (3) no loose items inside the chamber (a sticker on the floor, a detached label, a water bottle left behind by the previous loader). Finding a loose item after closing means a broken cycle and 200-400 PLN of wasted energy. The check takes 30 seconds and saves 200 PLN.
:::

### Loader's role vs kiln master's role – the line of responsibility

**The loader (you)** is responsible for: (a) placing stickers according to the geometry, (b) keeping the 150/100/300 mm gaps, (c) labelling the charge (chamber plate), (d) mechanically closing the door with a seal confirmation, (e) recording the event in the kiln logbook ("chamber 2, start 08:00, loader O.B., 15 m³ pine"). **You are not responsible for**: selecting the program, setting the temperature, setting the air humidity, the cycle time or the cooling parameters.

**The kiln master (Zbigniew)** is responsible for: (a) choosing the controller program (Siemens S7-1200 plus Lignocam software), (b) the parameters of the 3 phases (warm-up, drying, cooling), (c) monitoring the cycle across 4-30 days (a daily check through the viewing port, continuous monitoring through the panel), (d) the decision to end the cycle (target moisture reached equals chamber opening), (e) the closing act: moisture measurement of the batch before removal and the cycle quality protocol.

**Shared responsibility**: safety of work in the kiln hall (temperature 30-60 °C in summer with chambers running, hall ventilation, fire protection – kilns are fire class D, the wood inside at 60 °C does not ignite, but around the chambers no smoking or open flame is allowed). This division is not cosmetic: if a cycle goes wrong (a post-drying batch defect), the first conversation always starts with "who programmed it?" and "who loaded it?" – if you loaded correctly (plate, geometry, closing) and the cycle was wrong, the master is at fault. If the master programmed well and you placed the stickers shifted, you are at fault. The division exists so that everyone knows their own line and responsibility is not blurred.

## Key terms

- **Kiln drying** (PL: suszenie komorowe; EN: kiln drying; ES: secado en cámara; UK: камерне сушіння) – the process of reducing wood moisture from 50-80% MC to 8-15% MC inside a closed chamber with controlled temperature and air humidity, lasting 4-45 days depending on species, thickness and target moisture.
- **Sticker** (PL: przekładka; EN: sticker; ES: separador / rastrel; UK: прокладка) – a pine or spruce batten with a 20x25 or 25x25 mm cross-section, 1200-1500 mm long, placed every 600-800 mm along the board length to ensure air circulation and an even load distribution.
- **Drying cycle phases** (PL: fazy cyklu suszenia; EN: drying cycle phases; ES: fases del ciclo de secado; UK: фази циклу сушіння) – three consecutive stages of the program: warm-up (35 °C, 2-3 days, humidity 85-90%), main drying (45-70 °C depending on species, 3-30 days), cooling (a maximum of 6 °C/h, 5-10 hours).
- **Gradient cooling** (PL: chłodzenie gradientowe; EN: gradient cooling; ES: enfriamiento gradiente; UK: градієнтне охолодження) – a controlled reduction of temperature at a rate not exceeding 6 °C per hour; faster cooling causes surface checks in the outer layer of the board.
- **Target moisture content** (PL: wilgotność docelowa; EN: target moisture content; ES: humedad objetivo; UK: цільова вологість) – the final moisture of a batch after a drying cycle: 12-15% MC for construction and export lumber, 8-12% MC for furniture lumber, 6-8% MC for musical instruments.
- **Pine resin** (PL: żywica w sośnie; EN: pine resin; ES: resina de pino; UK: смола в сосні) – the terpene secretion of pine resin canals, which softens at 65 °C and flows above 80 °C – hence the upper drying-temperature limit for pine is set at 65-70 °C (a 5-15 °C margin from the flow threshold).
- **Kiln master** (PL: mistrz suszarni; EN: kiln master; ES: maestro del secadero; UK: майстер сушарні) – a kiln operator with electrical qualifications and 5+ years of experience, responsible for program selection, cycle monitoring and the decision to end drying; the loader does not program cycles.

## Trainer notes

**Lesson length 120 minutes**. Breakdown: 10 min introduction plus kiln hall (a visit to the chamber entrance if available), 30 min pine and spruce (temperature, resin, phases), 30 min oak and beech (deformations, beech honeycomb checks, long cycles), 25 min stickers and gaps (a physical stack demonstration if possible), 15 min labelling and roles (chamber plate, loader vs master division), 10 min Check yourself plus discussion.

**Kiln hall visit**: if the partner plant allows it, take the group to a CLOSED chamber (running) and an OPEN chamber (empty, after unloading). Never to an active chamber. Show the measurement panel through the viewing port and point out temperature and humidity on the display. In the empty chamber, physically show stickers, vertical columns and wall gaps. If the plant does not allow it, photographs and diagrams are enough, but the kiln hall is a place of very high educational realism.

**Pedagogical risk – "I already know this"**: a junior who previously worked in a furniture workshop (like Oleksij) often thinks they know drying ("in our workshop we dried boards in the attic for a year"). Natural drying (air drying, down to 20% MC) is a different process from kiln drying (active, down to 8-15% MC). Flag the difference at the start: this course teaches **industrial** drying, not home drying.

**Integration with lesson 2 of week 2 of module 2** (resistance moisture meter): in lesson 2 you learned how to measure moisture, in lesson 7 you learn **why** we measure (to verify the target output moisture of the batch). In lesson 8 we will link the two threads: while removing a batch from the chamber, you measure moisture with the meter from lesson 2, assess cracks from lesson 6 of week 2, and reclassify the class.

**Risk of "memorise every temperature"**: there are many of them (35, 45, 50, 55, 60, 65, 70, 80 °C, plus 6 °C/h, plus three air-humidity values 40/50/85/90%). The student has to remember **two key thresholds**: pine MAX 70, oak and beech MAX 60, cooling MAX 6 °C/h. The rest is detail that in practice is found in the chamber program table. Do not enforce the numbers, enforce the safety limits.
