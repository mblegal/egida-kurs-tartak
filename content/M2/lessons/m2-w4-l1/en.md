## Introduction

Monday, 6:30 a.m., production hall of the EGIDA Foundation. Andrij Tkaczenko is standing next to band saw no. 2, the same machine where four months ago he sat as a helper in module 1 and watched operator Tadeusz Kowalski saw pine logs. Andrij is 24 years old, comes from Lviv, has spent seven months in Poland, finished module 1 (sawyer's helper), and then completed the first three weeks of module 2 (work under a senior's supervision: machine start-up, adjustment, cutting, sorting, drying). Today he begins the final week of module 2, the week in which he stops being a "junior taught by a senior" and becomes an **operator in the crew**: responsible for his own shift, his own saw, and his own output.

At 6:32 foreman Marek Nowak walks in, the same person who guided Oleksij through all of week 3 and Sofía through week 2. Marek skips the greeting, pulls a yellow A4 form out of his folder, and places it on the small table next to the saw. "Andrij, from today on the work order is written. No more spoken instructions like 'cut that stack of spruce into beams'. Every shift starts with a **signed piece of paper** that tells you, in black and white: what to cut, how much, from what raw material, by when, and for whom. Read the whole thing, ask questions, then sign the acknowledgment. Let's start." Andrij takes the form. In the corner is a code: **DP-2026-042**. It is the 42nd sawing work order at the EGIDA sawmill since 1 January 2026.

This lesson opens week 4 of module 2 and shifts the **professional perspective of the trainee**. The first three weeks taught you to operate the machine under the eye of a senior: you watch what Tadeusz does, you repeat it, Tadeusz corrects you. In week 4 there is no longer a Tadeusz hovering over your head. There is a foreman who issues an instruction in writing, and there is a shift dispatcher who expects a result at the end of the shift. The **sawing work order** is the piece of paper that translates the client's and the dispatcher's decision into a concrete task for your machine. Being able to **read it**, **question it when something does not match**, and **acknowledge it** is not a bureaucratic activity: it is the competence that distinguishes a crew operator from a junior standing at the machine. Today you learn this document from the inside.

## Objectives

After this lesson:

1. You read the whole **sawing work order** (10 fields) in a fixed order: **priority**, **species**, **target assortment**, **quantity**, **deadline**. You know that you check priority and species first, because they decide whether you can even start the job before you verify the remaining parameters.
2. You ask **four types of questions** when something is unclear: (a) about dimensions and tolerance, (b) about the exact species (Norway spruce vs. Sitka spruce, English oak vs. sessile oak), (c) about priority (if the paper says B, but the dispatcher called to say "push it up to A"), (d) about whether the work order matches the real state of raw material in the yard. You address every question to the foreman; you never decide on your own.
3. You perform the **acknowledgment procedure** in five steps: you read the whole document, you underline 3 critical parameters on your copy, you ask questions, you sign both copies (the foreman's original and yours), and you file your copy in the operator's folder at the workstation (archived for five years in line with the Council of Ministers Regulation of 2009).
4. You recognise **four typical discrepancies** between the work order and the real situation, and you know what to do in each of them: (i) species missing from the yard, (ii) wrong moisture content of the raw material, (iii) the ordered quantity exceeds physical stock, (iv) priority changed mid-shift. You solve none of the four on your own: each one goes to the foreman with a specific description of the problem.
5. You understand the professional difference between a **"junior under supervision" (module 2, weeks 1-3)** and an **"operator in the crew" (module 2, week 4 and onward)**: the junior asks "what should I do?", the operator asks "did I understand correctly that I am supposed to do X?". In the first case the senior makes the decision for you; in the second case you propose your understanding and the senior confirms or corrects it. That difference is grammatical, but it changes the whole dynamic of the work.

## Content

::: info
**Why the work order must be written, not spoken.** Polish labour law does not require operational instructions to be written (Labour Code art. 100 § 1 only says that the employee follows the instructions of superiors), but a professional sawmill keeps written documentation for three reasons. First: **evidentiary archive**. If five months from now the client files a complaint about a batch of 100×200×4000 beams claiming they ordered 120×200, the sawmill pulls the signed work order DP-2026-042 out of the folder with the entry "100×200" in black and white, and the case is resolved at the after-sales stage. Without the paper it turns into word against word. Second: **health and safety archive**. Statistics from the National Labour Inspectorate (PIP) for 2019-2023 show that roughly 4% of accidents at sawmill machines stem from carrying out an instruction that does not match the state of the raw material: the operator hears "cut spruce into beams", walks over to the yard, takes oak instead of spruce (because spruce is temporarily missing), pushes a feed rate too high for oak, snaps the blade, and injures himself. A piece of paper with an unambiguous "Norway spruce" entry forces a stop when the raw material does not match. Third: **shift-to-shift communication**. Your replacement at 2:00 p.m. must know what was completed on your shift and what you have not started yet. A work order signed by the first-shift and second-shift operators builds a chain of responsibility without memory gaps.
:::

### The ten fields of sawing work order DP-2026-042

The EGIDA sawmill form is a single A4 sheet, portrait orientation, two self-carbonating copies. The fields are filled in by the shift dispatcher, signed by the foreman, acknowledged by the operator. Here are all ten fields with the concrete example of Andrij's work order:

| No. | Field | Value DP-2026-042 |
|----|------|---------------------|
| 1 | Work order number | DP-2026-042 |
| 2 | Date and time of issue | 20.04.2026, 6:15 |
| 3 | Operator (name, code) | Andrij Tkaczenko, code H-02 |
| 4 | Foreman (signature) | Marek Nowak (signature) |
| 5 | Machine | Band saw no. 2, Wood-Mizer LT40 |
| 6 | Species of raw material | Norway spruce (picea abies) |
| 7 | Target assortment | Structural beam 100 × 200 × 4000 mm |
| 8 | Quantity | 45 pieces (approx. 3.6 m³ of assortment) |
| 9 | Priority | **B** (standard, by Friday 24.04.2026 at 14:00) |
| 10 | Client and order number | "Dębowy Dom" Carpentry (Wieliczka), order SK-2026-018 |

**Priority** has three levels in the EGIDA system: **A urgent** (to be completed within 24 h, usually for a client whose construction project is already in progress), **B standard** (3 working days, about 70% of all work orders), **C flexible** (a week or more, usually stock inventory). Priority A takes precedence in every resource conflict: if you have an A and a B work order on the same saw at the same time, A goes first, even if B was issued earlier. **Target assortment** is the final dimension, not the raw-material dimension: 100 × 200 × 4000 means a beam with a cross-section of 100 mm × 200 mm and a length of 4000 mm after sawing (not after planing, if the assortment does not go on to the planer). Standard dimensional tolerance: **±1 mm** for thickness and width, **+50 / −0 mm** for length (the client accepts slightly longer, never shorter). **Quantity** is given in pieces for piece-based assortments and in m³ for volume-based assortments; for beams, usually both.

### Acknowledgment procedure for the work order - five steps

Acknowledging a work order is not "a quick signature and I grab the paper". It is five steps performed **in this order** for every work order, regardless of whether it is your first or your five hundredth.

**Step 1: you read the whole document from top to bottom.** No skipping. It takes 60-90 seconds. Yes, I know it feels like a waste of time with the foreman watching you. It is not. 90 seconds of reading save four hours of faulty production.

**Step 2: you underline three critical parameters on your copy.** Use a pen, not a pencil (pencil rubs off). The three parameters are: **species** (field 6), **assortment dimension** (field 7), **priority** (field 9). The other fields are important, but species, dimension, and priority are "errors you cannot undo": if you saw 45 beams out of oak instead of spruce, the material is unrecoverable.

**Step 3: you ask questions when something is unclear.** Always frame them in the pattern "I see X, I understand Y, is that right?". Not "what does this mean?", but "do I understand correctly that 100 × 200 means the cross-section after sawing, not after planing?". The foreman answers "yes" or corrects you. If you frame the question as "what does this mean", you dump the interpretive work onto the foreman: a professional operator proposes an understanding, the senior confirms it.

**Step 4: you sign both copies.** The original (white) stays with the foreman, your copy (yellow) stays with you. You sign the "Acknowledging operator" field and enter the time of acknowledgment (not of issue: the two times are different, sometimes by 15 minutes, sometimes by two hours).

**Step 5: you file your copy in the operator's folder at the workstation.** The folder hangs on the wall next to every machine, A4 format, 50 pockets, replaced every quarter and archived for five years (Council of Ministers Regulation of 5 August 2009 on employee documentation, § 5). The folder is shared by all operators of this machine on all shifts: thanks to that, your replacement can see what you were given and what you are accountable for.

### Four types of questions when something is unclear

Not every unclarity is the same. Four types, each with a concrete framing pattern:

**Type a - question about dimension and tolerance.** Example: the work order says "100 × 200 × 4000". You ask: "Is the tolerance on thickness 100 the standard ±1 mm, or does this client have a contract for ±0.5 mm?". It matters, because a tolerance of ±0.5 mm requires a slower feed rate and a sharper blade, so it changes the pace of the shift.

**Type b - question about the exact species.** The Polish name "świerk" (spruce) covers two commercial species: Norway spruce (picea abies), which grows in the Carpathians and the Sub-Carpathian region, and Sitka spruce (picea sitchensis), imported from Lithuania and the Baltic states. They differ in density (Norway 440 kg/m³, Sitka 400 kg/m³) and in their susceptibility to grain twist. Work order DP-2026-042 says "Norway spruce", but what if what is actually in the yard is Sitka? You ask: "I can see that today we mostly have Sitka in the yard, do I substitute, or do I wait for a delivery of Norway spruce?".

**Type c - question about priority after a spoken change.** A classic situation: the paper says B, but at 7:00 a.m. the shift dispatcher phones the foreman: "Hey Marek, that 042 is now A, the client called, they need it on Wednesday". The foreman walks over to you: "Andrij, 042 is on A". You reply: **"OK, please update the paper, sign next to field 9"**. A spoken priority change without a written record is the most common documentation gap in sawmills: it results in "he said, she said" when a complaint arrives. A professional operator forces the paper to be updated.

**Type d - question about whether the work order matches the state of the raw material.** The work order trusts the warehouse computer; the computer shows logical stock (entered on delivery intake); the yard shows physical stock (what the operator sees). The difference between logical and physical is not an error: it is the normal reality of a sawmill, resulting from wet assortment, natural shrinkage, and measurement errors. You ask: "I can see about 4 m³ of class A spruce in the yard, the work order assumes I need 4.5 m³ of raw material for 45 beams - do I go with 42 pieces from what is there, or do I wait for a top-up?".

### Four discrepancies and what to do

Four situations in which the paper says one thing and reality shows another. In each of them: **STOP work, report to the foreman, do not make a decision on your own**.

**Discrepancy (i) - species missing from the yard.** The work order says spruce; the yard has oak and pine. You do not substitute "because it is similar", because they are not similar (different density, different feed rate, different beam strength). You go to the foreman: "Marek, I don't have any spruce in the yard, there is only oak and pine. Do I wait for a delivery or do we change the work order?". The decision is made by the foreman together with the dispatcher, not by you.

**Discrepancy (ii) - wrong moisture content of the raw material.** The work order assumes fresh raw material (moisture content >35% MC), the yard has material pre-dried in the field piles (25-28% MC). That changes feed rate and the risk of splitting. You ask: "The paper says fresh, I see pre-dried, do we change the procedure or do we change the raw material?".

**Discrepancy (iii) - the ordered quantity exceeds the stock.** The work order says 45 beams = ~4.5 m³ of spruce raw material, the yard has 3.8 m³. Arithmetically you can make 38 beams out of what is there. You ask: "Do I produce 38, wait for a top-up of 0.7 m³ and finish up to 45, or do we change the quantity?". You do not produce 38 without asking, because a work order with quantity 45 signed by you for 45 commits you to 45.

**Discrepancy (iv) - priority changed mid-shift.** The foreman walks in at 10:30: "Andrij, 042 is now A, finish by the end of the shift". You react in line with type c questions: "Change the priority on the paper, sign it, and I expect to finish 42 pieces out of the current stock". You do not push the feed rate above the safe range "to make it in time": priority A does not suspend the safety rules.

::: warning
**Three common junior mistakes when acknowledging a work order.** First mistake: **acknowledging without reading**. The foreman slides the paper over, the junior wants to make a good impression ("I'm quick, I catch on fast"), signs in 15 seconds, and two hours later he comes back with "I didn't know it was 100 × 200, not 120 × 200". You signed, so you knew. Second mistake: **working from memory "because yesterday was the same"**. Yesterday was DP-2026-041, today is 042: two different work orders, two different clients, often different parameters despite the similar look. You never work from the memory of yesterday's work order. Third mistake: **species substitution "because it is similar"**. Spruce and pine look similar to a junior, but spruce has a density of 440 kg/m³, pine 520 kg/m³, and a client ordering a structural beam buys a specific density for their project. Substitution without the foreman's authorisation is **document falsification**, not "a bit of flexibility".
:::

::: tip
**How to take notes on the work order without damaging the original.** Your copy is an archival document kept for five years: you do not write your own remarks on it with a pen. You take notes on **sticky notes** (post-it, 76 × 76 mm) which you stick on the margin of your copy and remove when the job is done. Example: at 8:30 you notice that blade number 4 is starting to cut unevenly. You write on a yellow note "08:30 blade 4 replaced, fitted no. 7" and stick it on the work order. At 14:00, as you finish the shift, you peel the note off and transfer the entry to the shift logbook (that is the topic of lesson 2 this week). The original work order stays clean and archival, and your own technical documentation goes to the right place.
:::

::: example
**Case Andrij - work order DP-2026-042, first one in writing.** 6:35, Andrij picks up the form. He reads the whole thing from top to bottom. With a blue pen he underlines "Norway spruce" (field 6), "100 × 200 × 4000" (field 7), and "B" (field 9). He walks over to the sorting yard, 60 m from the saw. He counts the spruce pile: the package marked "SW-A-190420" (class A spruce from 19 April) is about 4.2 m³, which is enough for roughly 42 beams out of the 45 required. He also checks the species: every third board carries a "PICEA ABIES" label, so it is Norway spruce, matching the work order. He walks back to Marek. "Marek, two questions. First: is the ±1 mm tolerance on thickness 100 the standard, or does this client have ±0.5 mm?" Marek looks into order SK-2026-018: "Standard, ±1 mm". Second: "The species matches, but the quantity is enough for 42 beams, not 45. Do I make 42 and wait for a delivery, or do we change the quantity?". Marek frowns. "Delivery is tomorrow morning. Do 42 today, tomorrow from 6:00 a.m. you top it up to 45. I will sign the update on field 9: I am changing the completion deadline from Friday 14:00 to Friday 10:00, so the client still gets the beams on time despite the break". Marek adds in pencil next to field 9 "42 today + 3 tomorrow by 10:00, M.N." and initials it. Andrij signs both copies in the operator field. He files the yellow copy in the folder. 6:48, first work order acknowledged, first "junior to operator in the crew" arc test passed.
:::

## Key terms

Po polsku / In English / En español / Українською:

- **Dyspozycja przetarcia / Sawing work order / Orden de aserrado / Наряд на розпил** - a written work order for the saw operator, specifying species, assortment, quantity, priority, and client; an archival document kept for five years.
- **Sortyment docelowy / Target assortment / Surtido objetivo / Цільовий сортимент** - the final dimension of the product after sawing (cross-section × length), with a fixed tolerance; not to be confused with the raw-material dimension.
- **Priorytet zmianowy A/B/C / Shift priority A/B/C / Prioridad de turno A/B/C / Змінний пріоритет A/B/C** - the three-tier system in the EGIDA sawmill: A urgent (24 h), B standard (3 days), C flexible (a week or more); A takes resource precedence in case of conflict.
- **Potwierdzenie odbioru / Acknowledgment of receipt / Confirmación de recepción / Підтвердження прийняття** - the operator's signature on the copy of the work order with the time of acknowledgment; the moment when responsibility for the job passes from the dispatcher to the operator.
- **Niezgodność surowcowa / Raw-material discrepancy / Discrepancia de materia prima / Невідповідність сировини** - a situation in which the work order describes the state of the raw material differently from what actually appears in the yard; the four typical cases are missing species, wrong moisture content, insufficient quantity, and changed priority.
- **Eskalacja do brygadzisty / Escalation to the foreman / Escalado al capataz / Ескалація до бригадира** - the formal handover of a decision-making problem to the superior; the operator does not resolve a discrepancy on their own, but describes it concretely and asks for a direction.
- **Operator w brygadzie / Operator in the crew / Operario en la brigada / Оператор у бригаді** - the professional role of the trainee after week 4 of module 2: responsible for their shift and their machine, reads the written work order, proposes an understanding, escalates discrepancies; unlike the junior under supervision, who asks "what should I do".

What do you do: (a) cut from class B, because oak is oak, (b) stop, escalate to the foreman with the question "do we accept B or do we wait for A?", (c) cut half from B and wait for the other half from A, (d) call the dispatcher bypassing the foreman?

3. The work order says "Norway spruce", the yard has Sitka spruce. Which reaction is correct: (a) cut from the Sitka, because it is also spruce, (b) stop and ask the foreman to change the work order, (c) call the client directly, (d) change field 6 with a pen to "Sitka" and sign?

5. What is the difference between a "junior under supervision" and an "operator in the crew" in terms of how they frame questions to the foreman? Answer in one sentence.

## Link to practice

At the EGIDA Foundation training facility you will get three practical exercises with work orders. First: reading five archival work orders (DP-2025-001 to DP-2025-005) and marking the three critical parameters on each; the instructor checks whether you underlined the right fields. Second: a simulation of the acknowledgment with a foreman-instructor who deliberately hands you a work order with two errors (e.g. an impossible assortment dimension or a quantity larger than the stock); your task is to detect both errors within 3 minutes and frame questions in the "I see X, I understand Y, is that right?" pattern. Third: a written test on recognising the four typical discrepancies: you get 8 short scenarios (2-3 sentences each), you have to assign each one to one of the four categories and describe the reaction. Pass threshold: 6 out of 8 scenarios correct.

## Trainer notes

This lesson opens the paradigm of week 4 and most often generates resistance from trainees who have spent three weeks learning "how to operate the machine" and suddenly get the topic "how to read a piece of paper". It is worth saying openly at the start of the session: "this is not a step back, it is a promotion: week 4 means that we trust you with the machine, and we are showing you what your work looks like from the foreman's perspective". A second typical pushback: "but here at the Foundation we talk things through, nobody uses forms". The answer: EGIDA introduced the DP form in 2024, it is used every day, and spoken instructions remain only for urgent escalations (priority A), and even then they must be written up within 30 minutes. A third risk: in the Andrij case, the trainee wants to treat "42 instead of 45 is a minor change, I would not ask, I would just do it" - here the trainer goes back to the accountability question: "if the client gets an invoice for 45 and you deliver 42, who tops up the 3?". Whoever produced them, always, because that is what is entered on the work order they signed. An introductory exercise: the trainer hands the trainees a photocopy of a real (anonymised) work order and asks them to find the three critical parameters within 60 seconds. Most find two, not all three: that is the starting point for the conversation about why reading top to bottom without skipping matters.
