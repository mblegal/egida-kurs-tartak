## Introduction

In the previous lesson you saw the **whole day** at the sawmill: hours, shifts, briefing, report, three pillars (entry, production, exit). That was the **macro** view. Today we go to the **micro** view: **your workstation**. That one square metre (or three square metres, or a numbered patch of yard) where you spend most of the shift.

In Week 1 (Safety) you learned the **hazards**: saws, dust, noise, fire. In W2 (Material) you learned **what you work with**: log, lumber, species, defects. In W3 (Processes) you learned **what you do**: handling, feeding, stacking, housekeeping.

But for all of this to work together, **the workstation has to be prepared**. The saw will not run if there is no power. The operator will not cut if there are no logs. You will not lift a board if you have no gloves.

A helper's workstation is **not only the place where you stand**. It is a **set of conditions**: lighting, air (ventilation), cleanliness, access to tools, distance to the machine, visibility of the operator, distance to the emergency stop. All of this **together** makes your "safe one square metre".

In this lesson you will learn the **checklist** you go through **every morning** (seven minutes between 6:55 and 7:02, before you step into the briefing). Six items. Each has to be **green** (OK) before work can start. If anything is **red** (not OK), you **do not work** and you go to the foreman.

This is not paperwork. It is your **insurance**. If an accident happens on an unprepared workstation, the Social Insurance Institution (ZUS) and the Labour Inspectorate (PIP) ask: "did the helper check the workstation before starting work?". If **he did not**, part of the responsibility lands on you.

## Objectives

After this lesson:

- You know the **six points of the morning workstation check**: (1) PPE, (2) cleanliness, (3) walkways, (4) lighting, (5) tools, (6) emergency stop.
- You know **in which order** you check these points (logic: from yourself, through the space, to the machine).
- You can **recognize a "no-work" situation**: what counts as "red", when you are required to **refuse to work** (Article 210 of the Labour Code, known from W3).
- You know **what to do when something is wrong**: whom you go to, what you say, what you do NOT do on your own.
- You understand why **your workstation ≠ the operator's workstation**: the boundaries are defined; you check yours, the operator checks his.
- You know the check is **not only in the morning**: you redo it after every break and after a task change.

## Content

### Why you check the workstation every day

A lot of things happen **between shifts** at a sawmill. The outgoing shift may have left sawdust behind. The night shift may have taken your hearing protection because theirs broke. Someone may have moved a pallet. A technician may have changed a blade during the night and not put the tools back. A cleaner may have spilled cleaning fluid on the floor.

A sawmill is a **living organism**: it changes from day to day. That is why you start **every morning** with an **independent check**. You do not trust yesterday's state. You check now.

This is a rule that came from aviation. The **pilot** of an aircraft, even with 20 years of experience, **always** goes through the checklist before takeoff. Even if he was flying the same plane five minutes earlier. **Because an aircraft is a system, and systems change in minutes.** A sawmill is a similar system, only slower.

::: info
**Why can I not just "look around"?** Because the eye on its own **does not see what it is not looking for**. Without a list, your gaze slides over the workstation and **misses details**: a missing wedge, a burnt-out light, a guard shifted 2 cm. The list forces you to go **point by point**, and that is how you see things you would not otherwise notice.
:::

### Six points of the morning check

Take this scheme down. You will need it in the first weeks. After a few months you will do it **on autopilot**, but in the first week, **keep a card in your PPE pocket**.

**Point 1 – PPE (personal protective equipment)**

Before you enter the hall, you are wearing:

- Steel-toe boots (in good shape, laced).
- Work clothes (overalls or shirt + work trousers with high-visibility bands).
- Helmet (light, uncracked).
- Safety goggles (no scratches).
- Hearing protection (ear muffs or plugs, depending on the station).
- Dust mask FFP2 or FFP3 (if you work near hardwood cutting: oak, beech).
- Work gloves (only for hand tasks; NOT at rotating machines).
- High-visibility vest (if you move along internal roads with forklifts).

**Quick test**: stand in front of the changing-room mirror (most sawmills have one). See whether you look "complete". If something hangs, something is missing, something is undone, **fix it**. If you have worn-out gloves or a cracked helmet lamp, **replace them from the PPE store**. If the store is closed, the foreman has the key.

**Point 2 – Workstation cleanliness**

You step onto the station. You look at the floor (the 2–3 m² around you). You look for:

- **Sawdust and chips**: slippery and flammable. If there is a lot, you grab a broom and sweep before you start.
- **Spilled liquids**: oil, grease, water from the sprinkler, spilled coffee. You wipe it or call the cleaner.
- **Scattered tools**: a hammer on the floor, a wedge underfoot, a screw. You pick them up and put them away.
- **Pieces of tape, string, twine**: packaging leftovers, they can get caught in a trolley or pulled into a conveyor.
- **Foreign objects**: a water bottle, lunch paper, a bag. You throw them in the bin.

**The 5S principle** (originally from Japanese industry, but it applies in Polish sawmills too): **Sort, Set in order, Shine, Standardize, Sustain**. If an object is not needed at the workstation, **it is not there**. If it is needed, it has **its specific place**. If it is dirty, you **clean it right away**, not "later".

::: warning
**Sawdust is more dangerous than you think.** First, slipping: a helper's foot on sawdust is a classic fall with injury. Second, **fire**: sawdust from resinous wood (pine, spruce) ignites from **one spark**. Third, **dust explosion**: in a closed hall, a dense cloud of wood dust plus a spark equals a blast. There are recorded cases (a sawmill in Rogalin, 2019, two workers with severe burns). **That is why you clean sawdust right away, not at the end of the shift.**
:::

**Point 3 – Walkways**

Walkways are the **yellow lines on the floor** (known from lesson l6 of Week 1). You have to see that:

- The **line is continuous** (not hidden by a pallet, a stack, or bags).
- The **width is at least 1 metre** (required for pedestrian traffic; if forklifts run, 1.5 m plus the forklift width).
- The **path to the emergency stop is clear** (this point is key, it comes back in point 6).
- The **path to the first-aid kit and fire extinguisher is clear** (not blocked).
- The **path to the evacuation route is clear** (green "emergency exit" sign).

If something blocks a walkway, you **remove it** (if it weighs under 10 kg and is safe to lift) or **report to the foreman**. You do not work with a blocked walkway. It is not your call, it is a **regulation**.

**Point 4 – Lighting**

Sawmills often work **from dawn**, and in winter it is dark through half of the first shift. That is why **artificial lighting** is critical.

- **Ceiling lamps over the station**: on, not flickering, no burnt-out tubes (for a workroom: minimum 300 lux for a general station, 500 lux where measurements are taken, under the Polish standard PN-EN 12464-1).
- **Task lamps** (if any): working, cables not hanging.
- **Windows and roof skylights**: clean, not coated in dust (sawmill dust builds up on glass over months).
- **No shadows** over your tools and the material you work with.

**Quick test**: pick up a hand tool (ruler, pencil, wedge) and look at the markings: numbers, ticks, printing. Can you see them clearly? If you have to squint or lean in, the lighting is **too weak**. You report it to the foreman.

::: example
**From real life**: a helper from Belarus, a sawmill near Olsztyn, November; the first shift started at 7:00, with darkness until 8:00. The lamps over his station were partly flickering, partly dim. The helper "did not want to impose" and worked in half-light. After 40 minutes he cut off the tip of a finger with a hand plane: he thought the board was where it was not. After the accident, the Labour Inspectorate fined the sawmill 8,000 PLN for **failing to provide adequate lighting**. **The helper also shared some blame**: he had not reported it. Since then, a notice has hung on the wall at that sawmill: "lighting failure = immediate stop + report".
:::

**Point 5 – Tools at the workstation**

A helper has a **set of hand tools** that has to be complete and in working order. You check:

- **Tape measure**: undamaged, ticks visible, retracts smoothly.
- **Square**: the 90° angle is true, not bent.
- **Carpenter's pencil or permanent marker**: writing (not dried out).
- **Hammer**: handle not cracked, head fitted firmly (iron rule: a hammer with a loose head is a missile for the person standing next to you).
- **Wooden wedges**: a stock of at least 10 pieces.
- **Handsaw** (if your station has one): teeth intact, handle not cracked.
- **Hand plane**: blade in good condition, no rust.
- **Screwdrivers / cordless drill**: batteries charged (if cordless), full set of bits.

If one item is missing, you **do not improvise** ("I will do it without wedges", "I will borrow from a colleague"). You **go to the tool store** and draw the missing item. Sawmills keep a dedicated tool store with a checkout card: you sign for what you take, and return it after the shift.

**Point 6 – Emergency stop within reach**

This is the **most important point** on the whole list. The emergency stop (a red mushroom on a yellow background, known from W1 l8) is a device that **stops the machine immediately** in case of an accident. It is mandatory at every production line (Minister of Economy Ordinance of 21 October 2008, on minimum safety requirements for machinery).

You check:

- **The stop is in its place** (remember where; in the first week, "map" every stop at your station and the neighbouring stations; at least three within 10 m).
- **It is not hidden** (by a pallet, a sack, a box).
- **The mushroom is not pressed in** (if it is pressed, someone hit it yesterday and did not reset it: **you report this to the foreman**; you do not reset it yourself, because the machine would then start uncontrollably).
- **Your path to the stop is clear** (max three steps, no obstacles).
- **The stop's function has been confirmed**: the operator tests it every morning; you do **not** run this test yourself, but you **know that the operator did**.

::: tip
**Map the stops in the first week.** Take a sheet of paper, draw your station layout, mark every emergency stop within 10 m with a dot. Show it to the foreman; he will check whether you missed any. Then carry the sheet in your PPE pocket for two weeks. **After two weeks you have it in your head** without the sheet.
:::

### What to do when a point is "red"

Each of the six points can be **green** (OK, work) or **red** (not OK, no work).

**General rule**: if a point is red, you **do not work**; you go to the foreman and say concretely what is wrong.

**Examples of talks with the foreman**:

> "Chief, my hearing protection is cracked. I cannot work by the saw without it. Where do I get new ones?"

> "A pallet of lumber from yesterday is in my walkway. Should I move it, or is someone else going to?"

> "The lamp over my station is flickering and it is dark on the station. Who fixes this?"

> "The emergency stop at the band saw is pressed. I did not reset it, I do not know since when. What now?"

**Rule 1**: you say a **concrete thing**, not "something is wrong". The foreman solves a concrete problem in 30 seconds; a vague one he will misread.

**Rule 2**: you **do not improvise**. If a lamp flickers, you **do not climb a ladder with a bulb** (that is not your authorization, that is the electrician). If the stop is pressed, you **do not reset** (the operator does). If you are missing hearing protection, you **do not borrow from a colleague** (each person has their own, which prevents ear infections). **You report; the system solves.**

**Rule 3**: the **right to refuse work (Article 210 of the Labour Code)** applies here. If management says "do it without it, you will manage somehow", you have the right to answer: "no, I cannot work without (hearing protection / lighting / saw guard / clear evacuation route). I reported it. I am waiting for a fix." The employer **cannot fire you for this**.

### Your station vs. the operator's station: boundaries

An important distinction: **you check your station; the operator checks his**.

**Your station** (the helper's) is:

- The zone for handling and sorting lumber.
- The stack you work on.
- The log yard (if you are in the yard).
- The manual station (sawing, planing, measuring).
- The hand trolley and what is on it.

**The operator's station** is:

- The machine itself (saw, planer, milling machine).
- The control panel.
- The machine's working zone (the area within 2–3 m of rotating parts).
- Fences, guards, blades/discs.
- The dust extractor on that machine.

**You do NOT check**: the state of the saw's blade (the operator does), blade tension (operator), guards (operator), fences (operator), planer knives (operator). Those are **his duties**, not yours, and **his authorizations**, not yours.

**But you MUST REPORT** anything that worries you. For example:

> You see the blade guard has slid down 3 cm. You go to the operator: "Mr Marek, please take a look; the guard has slid." Operator: "thanks, I will fix it right away", and he checks. That is the **correct chain of communication**.

This is not **stepping into someone else's territory**. This is **shared vigilance over safety**. The operator appreciates it (especially a good operator). A bad operator will say "none of your business", and then you go to the foreman. The foreman sorts it.

::: warning
**Trap**: a senior helper says to a new one "the operators do not check this; I adjusted the fence myself yesterday; I will show you how". You **refuse**. You never adjust a machine, never change a blade, never clean inside a saw while the power is on. Even if "the older guys say it is fine". That is the road to amputation. **A helper does not go inside a machine.** A helper **reports** things for repair or adjustment.
:::

### Re-check after a break and after a task change

The morning seven minutes are a **full check of six points**. But that is not the only check during the day.

**After a 15-minute break**: when you return to the station, you run a **short re-check of three points**:

1. PPE: you put it back on (you took it off in the clean area, you put it on before entering the hall).
2. Station: has anything changed during the 15 minutes (dust, scattered chips, a tool a colleague left)?
3. Emergency stop: a glance at the clear path.

It takes **one minute**. But it saves lives, because the break is a window in which someone could have moved something.

**After a task change**: when the foreman says "up to now you were feeding the operator; now go to the yard and stack", that is a **new workstation**. Before you start, you run a **full six-point check** on the new station. Yes, it costs five minutes. Yes, it is worth it.

**After a return from absence** (sick leave, holidays, even a single day): a **full check + a conversation with the foreman** about what has changed. "I was on leave for a week; what has changed at my station?". That question means professionalism, not naivety.

### The 7-minute rhythm of the morning check

For six points to fit in seven minutes (6:55–7:02), you need a **rhythm**:

- **0:00–1:00**: point 1 (PPE): quick mirror test + fixes.
- **1:00–2:30**: point 2 (cleanliness): walk around the station, sweep if needed.
- **2:30–3:30**: point 3 (walkways): a look at the yellow lines, the path to the stop.
- **3:30–4:30**: point 4 (lighting): a look at the ceiling + tool-legibility test.
- **4:30–6:00**: point 5 (tools): inspection of the toolbox/rack.
- **6:00–7:00**: point 6 (emergency stop): look, accessibility test.

After seven minutes you have a picture: "my station is ready" or "I am reporting X to the foreman". At 7:02 you step into the briefing half-circle.

After the first month of work, those seven minutes **drop to three or four**, because you run on autopilot. But **never under three minutes**. If someone tells you "I do it in 30 seconds", he is **not doing it**; he is coasting on yesterday's state.

## Summary

Today you learned the **six-point morning workstation check**:

1. **PPE**: boots, clothes, helmet, goggles, hearing protection, mask (if required), gloves (where permitted).
2. **Cleanliness**: sawdust, spilled liquids, scattered tools, foreign objects. The 5S principle.
3. **Walkways**: yellow lines, minimum 1 m width, access to the stop, first-aid kit, extinguisher, evacuation route.
4. **Lighting**: ceiling + task lamps, tool-legibility test (300–500 lux under PN-EN 12464-1).
5. **Tools**: ruler, square, marker, hammer, wedges, handsaw, plane, screwdrivers. Complete and working.
6. **Emergency stop**: visible, accessible, not pressed, clear path, operator confirmed the test.

**Green/red rule**: every point OK = you work. Any point not OK = **you do not work**; you go to the foreman with a **concrete report**.

**Yours vs. the operator's**: you check **your** station; the operator checks **his machine**. If you see something on the machine, **report to the operator or the foreman**. You do not adjust, repair, or clean.

**Not only in the morning**: a short re-check after every break, a full check after a task change and after a return from absence.

**Right of refusal** (Article 210 of the Labour Code) applies: if something is "red" and management pushes you to "manage somehow", you **refuse**; that is your right, and the employer cannot fire you for it.

**Seven minutes a day** for this check. That is the cost of **40 years of retirement-age health**: one of the best time investments you will make in your life.

In the next lesson (l3) we go deeper still: **crew communication in noise**. When machines run, words cannot be heard. How the operator tells you "stop", "dawaj" (go on), "step back" without words.

- 🟢 **Zielone stanowisko** (pl) – green workstation (en) – puesto en verde (es) – зелене робоче місце (uk). A state in which all six check points are OK. Work can start. You report "all ready" in one word to the foreman at the briefing.

- 🔴 **Czerwone stanowisko** (pl) – red workstation (en) – puesto en rojo (es) – червоне робоче місце (uk). At least one of the six points is not OK. **You do not work** until it is fixed. You report the concrete issue to the foreman.

- 🧹 **5S** (pl) – 5S methodology (en) – metodología 5S (es) – методологія 5С (uk). A Japanese workstation-management principle: Sort, Set in order, Shine, Standardize, Sustain. At the sawmill: every item has its place, you remove what is not needed, you clean dirt right away.

- 💡 **300–500 lux** (pl) – 300–500 lux (en) – 300–500 lux (es) – 300–500 люкс (uk). Required workstation illumination under PN-EN 12464-1 (300 lx general, 500 lx for measurement tasks). If you have to squint, it is too dark; you report.

- ✋ **Art. 210 Kodeksu pracy** (pl) – Article 210 of the Labour Code (en) – artículo 210 del Código del Trabajo (es) – стаття 210 Трудового кодексу (uk). The worker's right to refuse work under conditions of danger. Applies at a "red" station; the employer cannot fire you for the refusal.

- 🧭 **Mapa wyłączników** (pl) – emergency-stop map (en) – mapa de paros de emergencia (es) – мапа аварійних вимикачів (uk). A sketch of your and the neighbouring stations, with emergency stops within 10 m marked. Done in the first week; carried in the PPE pocket for two weeks; then in your head.

- 🚫 **Granica stanowiska** (pl) – workstation boundary (en) – límite del puesto (es) – межа робочого місця (uk). The line between what you (the helper) check and what the operator (the machine) checks. The helper reports, but **does not adjust, repair, or clean** the machine.

- 🔄 **Skrócona kontrola** (pl) – short re-check (en) – verificación rápida (es) – коротка перевірка (uk). A one-minute re-check of three points (PPE, station, stop) after every 15-minute break. A full six-point check after a task change or a return from absence.

## Notes for the Trainer

**Pedagogical aim**: turn the abstract "check the station" into a **concrete 6-point × 7-minute procedure**. The learner leaves with a **list in his head** and an A5 card in his pocket. This is a strongly operational lesson, with practice outweighing narrative. Ideally, run it **on the hall floor**, not in a classroom, even quietly between shifts.

**120-minute rhythm**:

- **0–15 min**: opening. Question to the group: "How do you know you can start work at the station?". Collect answers; most will say "because the operator came and told me". **That is wrong.** Introduce the rule "each person checks **himself** before the machine starts". A short story from aviation (the pilot's checklist) as an analogy.
- **15–70 min**: **six points on the hall floor or in a classroom with a mock-up**. Walk the group **physically** through each point: (1) PPE: check each learner, what he is wearing, whether it is complete; (2) cleanliness: show a "good" station and a "bad" one (point out sawdust, spilled coffee, tools left behind); (3) walkways: show the yellow line, measure it, show one blocked by a pallet; (4) lighting: switch off the lamp, show a tool in half-light, switch it on, show the difference; (5) tools: show the helper's complete set; (6) stop: show the mushroom, map with the group every stop within reach.
- **70–85 min**: **practice talks with the foreman**. Pairs of learners: one helper, one foreman. The helper says "something is wrong". The foreman asks "what exactly?". The helper must answer **concretely** (not vaguely). Rotate roles. The trainer corrects form ("not 'something with the lamp', but 'the lamp over me is flickering and it is dark'").
- **85–100 min**: **workstation boundary**. Draw a diagram on the board: "yours" and "the operator's". Discuss what you check, what the operator checks. **Act out the scene "senior helper shows junior how to adjust a fence"**: the learner has to refuse politely but firmly.
- **100–115 min**: **the 7-minute rhythm**: stopwatch, learners run six points on the floor/classroom. First try: 10–12 minutes. Second try: 8 minutes. Third try: 7 minutes. **They learn the tempo.**
- **115–120 min**: key terms to write down; preview of l3 (gestures).

**Common mistake**: learners check the station "with their eyes" without system, looking but not seeing. **Correction**: force them to **say each point out loud**. "Point 1: PPE – I have boots, I have a helmet, I have goggles – do I have gloves? yes, I do – done." Saying it out loud **forces focus**.

**Second common mistake**: learners report "something is wrong" instead of a concrete item. **Correction**: break the scene into parts, repeat in pairs, until learners automatically speak a sentence like "item + location + what it does". Example: "glove + left + ripped at the finger". Three elements. Two weeks of practice.

**Practical recommendation**: hand out an A5 card with the list of six points (icon + name + one sentence "what I check"). Laminated, in the PPE pocket. Let them use it through the **entire first month** at work.

**Link to the next lesson**: l3 "Crew Communication in Noise". When the saw runs, words cannot be heard. The operator speaks to you through **gestures**. Five main gestures (stop, pass, wait, step back, back up), each consistent with ISO 7010 or the Polish sawmill standard. A practical lesson with paired gesture exercises.
