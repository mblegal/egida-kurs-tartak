## Introduction

Week 1 is coming to an end. You have learned the hazards (l1–l4), you know what to protect yourself with (l5), you know where you may be (l6), you know what to do when someone is injured (l7). Today the last piece of the foundation: **what you do when you have to evacuate**.

Evacuation is a situation in which **the whole hall must empty in a few minutes**. The cause can be fire, gas leak, structural damage, a police summons, or a drill. Whatever the reason, the rules are the same. What matters is **calm, speed, and order**. Panic kills as surely as fire.

The second topic of this lesson is the **emergency STOP button**: not only at one machine (we discussed that in l2), but in the context of the whole hall: when you stop **everything**, who gives the command, how to tell a technical fault from a situation requiring evacuation.

In this lesson you will learn the **three types of alarm signals**, the **5-step evacuation pattern**, the difference between **local STOP** and **master STOP**, the rules for **returning to your station after an alarm**, and about **evacuation drills**: why they are mandatory at least once a year.

## Objectives

After this lesson:

- You recognize three types of alarm signals (audible, visual, voice) and understand what they mean.
- You can execute the **evacuation pattern** in 5 steps: STOP → SECURE → GO → REPORT → WAIT.
- You tell apart a **local STOP** (one machine) from a **master STOP** (the whole hall).
- You understand why **evacuation drills are mandatory** and how to behave during them.
- You know when **NOT to return** to your station and who gives the "return" order.

## Content

### Three types of alarm signals

A sawmill uses three alarm systems, often together:

**1. Audible signal (siren, bell).** The basis. In Poland, the fire alarm in industrial plants is usually a **modulated siren sound** (a wailing up-down) lasting 3 minutes or until cancelled. A drill alarm has the same melody, **you must react exactly as to a real one**.

**2. Visual signal (red rotating lamp, strobe).** Used in noisy halls where the siren may be unheard, or where hearing-impaired people work. Usually a red flashing light above the evacuation exit.

**3. Voice signal (loudspeaker message).** In larger plants. A foreman or an automated message says: "Attention! Attention! Fire alarm. Please leave the hall via the evacuation route. Do not use the lift." Repeated every 15 seconds.

::: info
If you hear two different signals at once, evacuation. If a signal lasts longer than 30 seconds, evacuation. If you do not know what the signal means, **react as to evacuation**. Better to leave needlessly than to stay in a fire.
:::

### The 5-step evacuation pattern

When you recognize an alarm, you perform 5 steps in this order:

**1. STOP, stop the machine you are operating (or are near).**
- Press the local emergency stop (the red mushroom).
- The operator (if you are a helper) will do it themselves, but you also have the right, if you see they do not react (deafened, panicked).

**2. SECURE, put down what you are holding.**
- Do not run with a tool or a board in your hand.
- Do not try to hide documents, finish a cut, save material. **Material can be replaced, you cannot.**
- If something burning lies on the table, move it away from fuels, but do not try to extinguish.

**3. GO, via the evacuation route to the assembly point.**
- You walk, not run. Running on a sawmill floor means falls and sprains.
- Watch the colleagues around you, help a disabled person, an interpreter, someone who is lost.
- Do not return for personal items (phone, jacket, keys).
- Do not use the lift.
- If the route is smoky, stoop, go crouched. Less smoke near the floor.

**4. REPORT, check in at the assembly point.**
- The assembly point is outside, away from the building. The foreman or a designated person **checks attendance**: they have the shift list and tick off who is present.
- Report who is **missing** (if you saw a colleague at their station and they are not at the assembly point). This is crucial information for the fire service, where to search.

**5. WAIT, for the return order.**
- You do not return to the hall on your own. Even if you see that "it is over".
- The return is announced **only by an authorized person**: the shift manager, the foreman, or the fire commander.
- Without that order you stand at the assembly point, even in the cold, even for hours.

::: warning
In 2019 at a factory fire in Poland, a worker returned to the hall for his phone when the fire "was out". In fact, it was burning inside a wall. Smoke poisoning. A funeral instead of a phone. **Unauthorized return is one of the most common causes of fatalities in plants where the fire itself was controlled.**
:::

### Local STOP vs master STOP

You now understand that STOP is not a single thing:

**Local STOP**: a red mushroom **at a specific machine**. Stops only that machine. Used in an accident at that machine, a mechanical fault, or when someone has stepped into the danger zone (you learned this in l2).

**Master STOP**: stops **the whole hall or a section of it**. Usually a button in the foreman's office or in a special glass-fronted cabinet. Used for fire, evacuation, or a large-scale threat.

::: tip
A helper usually **does not press the master STOP**: that is done by the shift manager or the foreman. But you must know it exists and where it is. When you see a colleague fall into a machine, you press the local STOP (your duty). When you see a fire covering the whole hall, you trigger the fire alarm and evacuate, and the master STOP is handled by someone higher up.
:::

### Evacuation drills

Polish law requires evacuation drills **at least once a year** (the 2010 Ministry of Interior regulation on building fire protection). In larger plants, twice a year.

**How a drill looks:**
- The foreman announces "evacuation drill" in advance (usually an hour, sometimes unannounced for a real test).
- The fire alarm is triggered.
- **You react as to a real alarm**: stop the machine, walk the evacuation route, report in.
- Time is measured: from the alarm to "everyone at the assembly point". Target: **under 5 minutes** for a 100-person hall.
- Debrief, what went well, what went wrong. You are not punished for drill mistakes, the point is to learn.

::: example
A drill at a sawmill in Podkarpackie, 2023: first time, 7 minutes for 40 people. Problem: three workers did not hear the siren (hearing protection + a running saw), two returned for phones. Second drill a month later, 3 minutes 40 seconds. Changes: an extra strobe lamp above the saw, a reminder "zero return". **What did the drill cycle improve?** A result of awareness and technical additions. One drill is not enough, a series is needed.
:::

### Evacuating a disabled or new colleague

Not everyone in the sawmill is equal in evacuation:

- **A disabled person** (leg in a cast, trouble walking), the foreman assigns them an **evacuation buddy** (a specific colleague whose job is to get them out). If you are the buddy, you do not leave alone, you wait and go together.
- **A new worker** (first week, does not know the way), someone in the group leads them. If you see a newcomer nearby, "come with me, I know the way".
- **Someone who does not speak Polish**: a hand gesture "follow me" and a matching pace. Language is not a barrier in evacuation, because everyone goes the same way.

### After evacuation: the return order

After the situation is under control (fire out, fault repaired), you return to the hall. But **only after the announcement**:

1. The shift manager (or the fire commander) announces "return to stations".
2. You walk together with the group, do not overtake.
3. Before starting the machine, **check the workstation** (cigarette butts, crumpled material, water on the floor from extinguishing, electrical damage).
4. Report any observation to the foreman.

::: info
The first 30 minutes after returning from evacuation is a **high-attention hour**: fire sometimes hides and rekindles. Stay alert for the smell of smoke, unusual sounds, sparks. Report any suspicion immediately.
:::

## Check yourself

1. You hear a modulated siren lasting 2 minutes. What do you do first?
   - A) I ask the foreman whether it is a real alarm or a drill.
   - B) I stop the machine, put the material down, walk the evacuation route to the assembly point. I react as to a real alarm.
   - C) I lock the hall so no one comes in.

   → **B**

2. You are at the assembly point after evacuation. You see that colleague Jan was working at the band saw, but is not here. What do you do?
   - A) I wait 5 minutes, maybe he is late.
   - B) I report to the foreman immediately: "Jan is missing, he was at the band saw."
   - C) I run back into the hall alone to check on him.

   → **B**

3. After the evacuation the fire service says the situation is under control. Can you return to your station?
   - A) Yes, right away.
   - B) Yes, but only when I hear "return to stations" from the shift manager or the fire commander.
   - C) Only after I call my wife.

   → **B**

## Link to practice

Within your first three months at the sawmill you **will go through at least one evacuation drill**. If you have not yet, ask the foreman when one is planned. The drill is the best school: only after walking the evacuation route will you truly believe you can do it.

In the first week the foreman will show you the **three key elements**: your evacuation route (from your station), the assembly point (outside the building), and the **nearest local STOP** at your station. The third element is your first reaction, not only in evacuation, but in any accident.

## Trainer notes

**Pedagogical goal:** after this lesson the learner has a **mental "evacuation drill"**: they imagine what they do when they hear the alarm. This closes week 1 OHS, everything learned before (hazards, PPE, zones, first aid) combines into a **systemic reaction** to a critical situation.

**120-minute rhythm:** 10 min recap of l7 (first aid) + introduction: the difference between individual reaction (l7) and systemic (l8), 15 min on three alarm signal types (play siren sound from YouTube + show a strobe-lamp photo), 25 min on the 5-step evacuation pattern with a simulation (trainer calls "alarm", learners go through the steps dry), 15 min local STOP vs master STOP (show on hall photos where they are), 20 min evacuation drills, what they look like, why they are mandatory, how to behave, 10 min special cases (disabled, newcomers, non-Polish speakers), 10 min return to station, 10 min terms and self-check, 5 min recap of the whole week 1.

**"Virtual evacuation" exercise:** learners sit in the classroom. You call "fire alarm". Learners in turn say what they do **in order**: first says "stop the machine", next "walk the route", next "report in". Show that good evacuation is not theoretical knowledge but **a behaviour you know by heart because you practised it**.

**Common mistake:** learners say that during an alarm they "will go and check where the fire is". Trainer's reply: "No. You are not a firefighter. Your role is to leave. Checking ends with calling 112, the rest is done by professionals. Your plant has people trained in firefighting, you are not one of them."

**Week 1 recap (important!):** show the learners a map of the 8 lessons on the board and show how they connect: l1 (why OHS), l2 (machines), l3 (dust+noise), l4 (fire), l5 (PPE), l6 (zones), l7 (first aid), l8 (evacuation). Each lesson is one answer to "what can go wrong and what do you do". After week 1 the learner **knows the OHS foundation**: everything that follows in the course builds on this foundation.

**Link to the next week (Week 2, Material):** "Week 1, closed. You know how not to die. Week 2, what you process on the floor. Wood, species, defects, tools. You start to get to know the **material** you work with."
