## Introduction

Third lesson of Week 3. You know **who you are** (l1) and **how to carry timber without wrecking your spine** (l2). Today you go **to the log yard**: the outdoor or covered area where the **logs and long timber** freshly delivered from the forest lie before being cut.

The log yard is **the most dangerous place** in a sawmill. Why? For three reasons:

1. **Weight**. A log is not a board. A standard pine log 40 cm × 4 m weighs **about 480 kg**. An oak log 50 cm × 5 m, **about 1200 kg**. These are **masses that kill** if they roll.

2. **Potential energy**. On the yard, logs **lie in stacks** or on bearers. If the stack is built badly, logs **roll** from all directions, fall from 1.5–2 metres. The accident is **as fast as lightning**.

3. **Loading/unloading dynamics**. A truck brings 10–20 logs that are dropped from the bed. The loader operator **does not see people behind the logs**. **Logs in motion**: **at any moment they may roll in an unforeseen direction**.

National statistics: according to **PIP (Labour Inspectorate) reports** and **GUS** (Central Statistical Office), in the sawmill industry **20–30% of fatal accidents** take place **on the log yard**. NOT at the saw (that's only second place). **Log yard = number one on the death statistic.** Why? Because a saw **cuts**: most often a finger, a hand, rarely kills. A 480 kg log rolling onto a person **kills instantly**, or crushes the chest cavity and the person dies en route to hospital.

That is why this lesson is **not about technique** but about **geography**. You'll learn **where you may stand, and where never**. You'll learn **how to read a log stack**: whether it's stable. You'll learn **how to use wedges and bearers** to build a stack that **won't slide apart**. And above all you'll learn **the five situations in which you walk away and call the foreman**.

## Objectives

After this lesson:

- You understand the **unloading danger zone**: radius 5 m from truck or loader, where a helper **never** stands.
- You know **where a log stands**: **on two wooden bearers** minimum 10 × 10 cm, **wedged from the side**, so it won't roll.
- You know the **3-4-3 pyramid rule** (base 3–4 logs, second layer 3, third layer 2), NOT to build stacks higher than 3 layers for loose logs.
- You know that **a wet log rolls more easily** than a dry one (lower friction coefficient), so in the wet season **more wedges**.
- You can name **five walk-away situations**: you step back and call the foreman.

## Content

### Anatomy of a log yard

Every sawmill has some **log yard**: can be covered, can be under the open sky. Most often it looks like this:

- **Delivery (inbound) area**: where the truck from the forest pulls in. Hard ground (best: concrete or compacted gravel). Signs and markings.
- **Storage area**: where **log stacks lie**, usually sorted **by species and diameter**. Pine separate from oak. Thick logs separate from thin ones.
- **Transport corridor**: the path along which the loader/tractor **moves** logs from the yard to the sawmilling line. You **do not stand** in this corridor, unless you have a task explicitly there.
- **Waiting area**: single logs **pulled aside** from the main stack, because they are going into the saw shortly.

**Your task as a helper** is NOT to unload the truck (that's the loader operator). **Your task** is:

1. **Wedge and bearer** logs as the operator sets them down.
2. **Sort** logs (species, diameter, length), on the foreman's instructions.
3. **Mark** logs with chalk or marker (species, date, delivery number), lesson **l8**.
4. **Sweep debris** from the yard (bark pieces, branches, mud), lesson **l7**.
5. **Hand a wedge** or **bearer** to the operator when needed.

::: info
**Basic rule**: a helper **always to the side of the operation**, never **in the line of the log's motion**. If the loader approaches with a log, you are **to the side**, you see the operator, you have eye contact. If you don't, **you don't approach**.
:::

### Unloading danger zone: radius 5 m

When a truck **unloads** logs (or a loader **loads** them onto a carriage), around it **is a danger zone**. Its **radius is a minimum of 5 metres**. In that zone NOBODY stands, except:

- The **operator** of the loader/truck (who **sees what he is doing**).
- The **banksman/signaller** (a helper with authorisation to signal the operator's movements, rare, mainly at large sawmills).

As a helper without a banksman's authorisation, **you are OUTSIDE the zone**. You stand **to the side**, best **behind a fixed barrier** (a building wall, the mass of a stack already set, a pillar). You **see** what's happening, but **a log won't reach you** if it rolls.

**Why 5 metres?** Because a standard log is 4 metres long. If it rolls, it can **spin** and **kick out** 1–2 metres past its original position. 5 metres is **a safe margin**.

::: warning
**Never enter the unloading zone to "fix something".** Even if you see that **the log is sitting crooked** and you should "just give it a quick shove", **no**. You wait for the loader to drive off, for the operator to give a signal, for the foreman to say "come in". Migrants who "want to help" by entering the unloading zone, **die most often**.
:::

### Bearers and wedges: the foundation of stability

A log does NOT lie **directly on the ground**. Reason: (1) it soaks up moisture from the soil, (2) rots from below, (3) sits on uneven ground, hard to grab afterwards with a loader.

A log lies on **bearers**. What is a bearer? A **timber beam** 10 × 10 cm (minimum) or 15 × 15 cm (for large logs). Length **twice the log diameter**: so that it sticks out on both sides. Material: most often an **old beam** from the mill's own production, often oak or beech (harder, lasts better).

**Under one log, two bearers**. One at a distance of **1/4 of the length** from one end, the other **1/4 of the length** from the other end. For a 4 m log = 1 m from each end. That way the log **sits stably**, doesn't sag.

**Wedges** are **wooden triangles** 20–40 cm long, **driven in from the side of the log**, to block its **rolling**. Minimum **two wedges per log**, driven in **from opposite sides** (one from the left, one from the right). For large logs (>50 cm diameter), **four wedges** (one at each end on each side).

Wedge material: **hardwood** (oak, beech, acacia). **Not a pine wedge**: too soft, crushes under pressure from the log.

::: example
**Example of a good set-up**: an oak log 50 cm × 5 m (weight ca. 1 tonne). Two bearers 15 × 15 cm, placed 1.25 m from each end (= length 5 m − 2 × 1.25 = 2.5 m between bearers). **Four wedges**: one 30 cm wedge from each side at each end. The log **does not roll**, even if somebody bumps it lightly. **It stands like built into the ground.**
:::

### Log stacks: the 3-4-3 pyramid rule

When there are many logs, **they don't lie singly**. They are built into **pyramidal stacks**:

- **Bottom layer**: **3–4 logs** laid next to each other, each wedged individually. Between logs **small gaps** (5–10 cm), for ventilation and easier gripping.
- **Middle layer**: **3 logs** laid **in the valleys** (i.e. **between** the bottom-layer logs). This layer **stabilises itself** through the pyramid effect.
- **Top layer**: **2 logs** in the valleys of the middle layer.
- **Possibly further layers**: but **only** on stacks **mechanically supported** (side walls, straps, mesh).

**Maximum stack height for a helper**: **3 layers**. Above, **only the loader operator** builds the stack.

::: warning
**Golden rule of log stacks**: if you see a stack that has **more than 3 layers** and **no side walls**: **do not approach**. That is **an unstable pyramid** and may collapse **at any moment**. Go to the foreman and say: "That stack looks unstable, I want to help but I don't know where to stand." You'll get instructions or somebody else will go.
:::

### Five walk-away situations: you step back and call the foreman

There are **five situations** on the log yard in which **you do not help, you walk away**:

**1. A log has started rolling in an unforeseen direction.** The loader operator set it down, the log "started moving". **Don't try to stop it**: neither with your arm nor by sticking a bearer under it on the run. **Step quickly to the side** (never in the line of rolling), shout "log!" to those nearby. The foreman or the operator with the crane will stop it.

**2. A stack looks "crooked".** One of the layers has shifted. You can see that the middle layer **isn't sitting in the valleys**, but on **a single log**. **That is a sign of serious instability.** Step away, call the foreman.

**3. Wedges have fallen out or bearers have slid apart.** The log **is sitting on nothing**: it may start rolling **at any moment**. Mark the zone with **warning tape** (if available), call the foreman.

**4. The truck is parked crookedly.** A truck **entering the yard** has tilted, wheels on one side in mud, on the other not. **The logs on the bed may start rolling** at the moment of unloading. **You do not enter the zone**, you wait for instructions from the driver or the foreman.

**5. The ground is unstable.** After heavy rain the yard is **very muddy**: bearers **"float"**, don't hold steady. Wedges **push into mud** instead of blocking the log. **A dangerous working day.** The foreman should halt operations, **but if he doesn't**, your "no, I'm not going there without stable ground" **is correct** (Art. 210 Labour Code, again).

### Wet logs vs dry: different dynamics

A log **just delivered from the forest** is **wet**. After a week on the yard, **half-humid**. After a month, **drier**. The practical difference:

- **Wet log**: smoother surface (water = lubricant), **rolls more easily**. Wedges must be **driven in deeper**. Bearers must be **wider** (larger contact area = less sinking).
- **Wet log**: **heavier** by ca. 30–50% than the same log once dried. The rolling energy is **higher**: a roll-off is more dangerous.
- **Dry log**: rough surface (bark coming off), **rolls more slowly**, but **breaks more easily** on impact with other material.

Practical implications:
- **Spring and autumn** (lots of wet logs), **more wedges**, wider bearers.
- **Summer** (dry period), standard wedging is enough.
- **Winter** (frozen logs), wedges **won't drive in well**, because frozen wood is like stone. **You must warm the wedge tips** or use metal wedges (with a wooden cap, so they don't shred the log's fibres).

::: tip
**How to check whether a stack is stable**: stand **to the side** (not in front and not behind, those are the rolling lines). Look at the layers, is each one **sitting in the valley of the one below**? Is any log sticking out "in the air"? Are wedges driven in **at top and bottom**? Are all **bearers perpendicular** to the logs, not parallel? If all "yes", stack OK. If even one "no", **call the foreman**.
:::

### Yard marking: zones and signs

A well-organised log yard has **markings**:

- **Yellow and black stripes** on asphalt/concrete, **zone boundaries**. Behind a yellow line = storage zone (walking allowed). Behind black-and-yellow stripes = danger zone (do not enter).
- **Warning signs** (triangular, yellow), "Caution, logs in motion", "Unloading zone", "Slippery terrain".
- **Arrows** on concrete, **permitted direction** for pedestrian movement.
- **Stations** numbered, "Station A", "Station B", for **sorting logs** by species.

If the yard **has no markings**: that itself is **an OHS sign**: the sawmill does not meet the standard. **Be especially careful** on such a yard and **report** the absence of markings **to the foreman** (he will pass it on).

## Summary

Today you learned **how to survive on the log yard**:

- **Log yard = number 1 on the death statistic** in a sawmill. A 480 kg log rolling, **kills instantly**. Therefore every move on the yard is **conscious and careful**.
- **Unloading danger zone**: **minimum 5 m** from a truck/loader in motion. You don't enter to "fix something", **never**.
- **Bearers + wedges** = the foundation of stability. A log sits on **two 10 × 10 cm bearers**, **1/4 of its length from each end**, wedged **on opposite sides**. Wet log = more wedges, wider bearers.
- **Pyramidal stack 3-4-3**: bottom 3–4 logs, middle 3, top 2. Maximum 3 layers. More = only the loader operator.
- **Five walk-away situations**: log in uncontrolled motion, crooked stack, wedges/bearers out, truck parked crookedly, unstable ground (mud, ice). In these situations **you don't help, you call the foreman**.

The log yard is **not a place for improvisation**. It is a place for **iron discipline**. A log has no opinion about your "shortcuts", if it rolls, it rolls. Your only tool is **knowing where not to stand**.

## Key terms

- 🪵 **Plac kłód** (pl), log yard (en), patio de troncos (es), майданчик колод (uk). Storage area for freshly delivered logs before cutting. Number 1 on the sawmill death statistic.

- ⚠️ **Strefa niebezpieczna rozładunku** (pl), unloading danger zone (en), zona de peligro de descarga (es), небезпечна зона розвантаження (uk). Radius 5 m from truck/loader in motion. The helper does NOT enter, ever.

- 🪨 **Podkład** (pl), bearer / support timber (en), durmiente / traviesa de apoyo (es), підкладка / лежень (uk). Timber beam 10 × 10 cm (min.) on which a log rests. Minimum two per log, at 1/4 of length from each end.

- 📐 **Klin** (pl), wedge (en), cuña (es), клин (uk). Wooden triangle 20–40 cm long, blocks the log from rolling. Minimum two per log, driven in from opposite sides. Material: hardwood (oak, beech, acacia).

- 🔺 **Stos piramidalny 3-4-3** (pl), pyramidal log stack (en), pila piramidal de troncos (es), пірамідальний штабель колод (uk). Stack-building rule: bottom layer 3–4 logs, middle 3, top 2. Max 3 layers for the helper. Above, only the loader operator.

- 🚜 **Ładowarka** (pl), loader / wheel loader (en), pala cargadora (es), фронтальний завантажувач (uk). Machine (Wacker Neuson, CAT, JCB) for moving logs. The helper **does not operate**: only a UDT-licensed operator. The helper stands to the side, sees the operator.

- 💧 **Kłoda mokra vs sucha** (pl), wet vs dry log (en), tronco húmedo vs seco (es), сира vs суха колода (uk). Wet = smoother, heavier, rolls more easily, more wedges. Dry = rough, lighter, rolls more slowly, standard wedging.

- 👁️ **Sygnalista** (pl), signaller / banksman (en), señalizador (es), сигнальник (uk). Authorised person giving signals to the loader/crane operator. The helper is **not** a banksman without separate training.

- 🟡⚫ **Pasy ostrzegawcze czarno-żółte** (pl), black-and-yellow warning stripes (en), franjas amarillas y negras de advertencia (es), чорно-жовті попереджувальні смуги (uk). Marking of danger zones on the yard. You do not step past the stripes, unless you have an explicit task.

- 🛡️ **Art. 210 KP, return** (pl), Labour Code Art. 210 (en), artículo 210 CT (es), стаття 210 КП (uk). Right to refuse work in dangerous conditions. Unstable ground, no markings, unstable stack = situations in which you may refuse.

## Trainer's notes

**Pedagogical goal**: build **respect for the log yard** and **practical knowledge of the geography of the safe zone**. After this lesson the learner must **automatically** stand to the side of operations, never in the line of rolling, and **know** what to do when they see an unstable stack.

**120-minute rhythm**:
- **0–20 min**: opening, the statistic "log yard = number 1 on the death statistic". Question to the group: "How much does a 4-metre pine log weigh, do you think?", they'll usually guess 100–200 kg. Say: **480 kg**. Nobody guesses correctly. Then show **a log on the yard** (if training is at the sawmill), let them approach **from a distance**, see the size.
- **20–40 min**: yard geography, on the board **you draw the yard plan** with zones (delivery, storage, corridor, waiting). You mark **the 5 m zone** around the truck. Learners draw in their notebooks. Discussion, **where the helper stands** (to the side, sees the operator, behind a barrier).
- **40–65 min**: **practical demonstration of wedging**: if there is a chance to go out to the yard (**under the foreman's supervision**). You show: a log (medium, safe), two bearers, four wedges. Each learner **drives a wedge in** (with a wooden mallet). You correct technique. If no yard, models on a mock-up or CIOP-PIB video.
- **65–85 min**: **3-4-3 stack**: you draw on the board, analyse what's "good", what's "bad". Show a crooked stack (middle layer not in the valleys), let learners notice by themselves. "Who sees the problem?", discussion.
- **85–105 min**: **five walk-away situations**: each with an example (photo, video, shop-floor story). Role-play scenarios: "you see a rolling log, what do you do?" One learner answers, the group corrects.
- **105–115 min**: yard marking, PN/EN standards, colours, examples. If the yard is on site, walk-through. If not, photos.
- **115–120 min**: key terms, oral quiz ("How many metres is the unloading danger zone?"), announcement of l4 (stacking timber with stickers, **no more logs, now sawn boards after cutting**).

**Frequent learner mistake**: "I'm quick, I'll dodge if a log goes at me". **Trainer's response**: "A 480 kg log rolling at 3 m/s has **the energy of a car at 20 km/h**. You won't run away. The only route is **not being in the line of rolling**. And that isn't a question of reflex, it's a question of **a decision, where to stand**. Reflexes never measure against physics."

**Practical recommendation**: introduce the **"circle gesture" rule**. The helper, entering the yard, **sweeps their eyes around** (360°) for **2–3 seconds**: where is the loader, where the truck, where are unstable stacks, where am I standing. **Only after the circle gesture** does he start work. That is the gesture of **a pilot doing a walkaround before take-off**: a sawmill works the same way.

**Link to the next lesson**: l4 is **stacking timber with stickers**. No more logs, **boards after cutting**. Stickers every 30–50 cm, ventilation, stability. Practically: the bearers from l3 also work here, but now they are **horizontal, not vertical**: the stability principle the same, geometry different.
