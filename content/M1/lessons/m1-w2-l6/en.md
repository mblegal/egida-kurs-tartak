## Introduction

Yesterday you learned **hand tools** — the ones you hold in two hands. Today you take a step toward **machines**. Machines that **you don't operate** — but **you have to know** what they look like, what they're called, where their danger zone begins, and what to do if the operator says to you: "**you're standing in the wrong place**".

In a Polish sawmill you'll meet dozens of machines. But **three of them** do **80% of the work** — these are the **sawmill saws**. Three main types: **frame saw**, **band saw**, **circular saw**. Each one **cuts the log differently**. Each one has **a different sound**, **a different reach**, **a different danger zone**.

This lesson is the first one in which we talk about **machines with engines** in detail. That doesn't mean today you'll learn to operate them. **No.** Your relation to saws as a helper is a relation **at a distance**. You watch, recognize, walk around, hand material from a safe place. That's it.

But "that's it" requires **knowledge**. A helper who **doesn't know what a "band saw" is**, when the operator shouts "**come to the band saw!**" — stands in the middle of the hall and looks. That's lost time. A helper who **knows** comes over in 5 seconds.

## Goals

After this lesson:

- You recognize the **frame saw** (Polish: **pilarka ramowa / trak ramowy**), the **band saw** (Polish: **pilarka taśmowa**), and the **circular saw** (Polish: **pilarka tarczowa**) — by appearance and by sound.
- You understand that **each of these saws has a different danger zone** — and you know **where it runs**.
- You know that a helper **never** stands **in the line of cut** — because **kickback of the material** can kill him.
- You remember that the **emergency STOP button** is **on every machine**, and you know **when** you have the right to use it.

## Content

### Why three different saws?

Short answer: because **a log is round and a board is rectangular**. The first saw **cuts the log lengthwise** into flat pieces. The second one **trims** those pieces to even thickness. The third one **cuts** them to the desired length. Each saw **does a different stage** of the same road "from log to board".

In practice, in Polish sawmills you'll most often meet:

1. **Frame saw (trak ramowy)** — first stage, main log cutting.
2. **Band saw** — second stage, trimming and precise cutting.
3. **Circular saw** — third stage, cross-cutting and trimming to length.

Some sawmills have **only two** of these three (for example, replacing the frame saw with a band saw of greater width). It depends on **plant size** and on the **species** being cut.

### Frame saw — "the grandfather of the sawmill"

The **frame saw** (Polish also: **trak ramowy**, or simply **trak**) is the **oldest** type of sawmill saw. It looks like **a huge rectangular frame** hanging vertically, with **several parallel blades** (saws) tensioned vertically.

Working principle: **the frame moves up and down**, the log **enters under the frame** horizontally, on rollers. Each blade cuts the log in a different place. From a single passage of the log come **several parallel boards** at once.

**Characteristic features:**

- **Size**: huge, 3–4 m tall. Takes up the whole hall.
- **Sound**: rhythmic, **heavy "thud-thud-thud"** with each downward stroke of the frame. Heard across two halls.
- **Cutting speed**: slow (1–3 m/min). One log cut in 3–10 minutes.
- **Advantage**: cheap, reliable, gets all the material from a log in one pass.
- **Disadvantage**: slow, low precision, much waste (wide kerf).

**Danger zone of the frame saw:**

- **The whole longitudinal axis** of the log — **in front of** the machine and **behind** it. This is the **line of cut**. You never stand here.
- **The sides of the machine** are **safe** if you stand **outside the safety guard**.
- **The log entry zone**: the log enters **on rollers** — don't put **hands between the rollers** and **don't walk under the log**.

::: warning
**The most common accident** at the frame saw: the helper stands **behind the machine**, near the place where **the boards come out**. The operator gives him a sign "stop, something doesn't fit". The helper **walks up to the log to look**. At that moment the operator **stops the frame**, but the rollers **push the log on** by 30–50 cm. The helper **doesn't expect movement** and **gets hit by a board**. Therefore: **when you're going to walk up**, first **the operator turns everything off** — rollers, frame, feed. Only then do you walk up.
:::

### Band saw — "the hard-working senior"

The **band saw** (Polish: **pilarka taśmowa**) is a machine in which the blade has the shape of **a long, continuous band** — like a transmission belt — stretched between **two flywheels** (one on top, one on the bottom).

Working principle: the band **rotates continuously** in one direction, at high speed. A log or board **comes up under the band** from the side — the band **cuts through** the material with **high precision** and **little waste**.

**Characteristic features:**

- **Size**: medium to large (most often 2–3 m tall).
- **Sound**: **a high, whistling tone** + the hum of the band. Different from the "thud-thud" of the frame saw.
- **Cutting speed**: fast (5–15 m/min). One log — 1–3 minutes.
- **Advantage**: precise, low waste, **narrow kerf (3–4 mm)**, energy-efficient.
- **Disadvantage**: the band **breaks** from time to time — it requires changing. This is **a dangerous moment** — the band **flies out** of the machine with great force.

::: info
In Polish sawmills, **the band saw** is increasingly displacing the frame saw. One of the best-known brands is **Wood-Mizer** (American, but produced in Poland in Koło, Wielkopolska). Their saws are **mobile** — you can transport them on a truck to the forest and cut logs **without transport to the sawmill**. This changes the organization of work. A helper in a modern sawmill **must know the band saw** better than the frame saw.
:::

**Danger zone of the band saw:**

- **The line of cut** (axis along which the band moves the material) — safe **distance 3 m** in each direction.
- **The plane of the band** — if the band breaks, it **flies in the direction of its plane**. Stand **perpendicular** to the band, never "in line".
- **The zone under the log** — the log is **held** by the machine's arms. Sometimes it **drops slightly**. Don't put your hands under the log.

### Circular saw — "the precise younger sister"

The **circular saw** (Polish: **pilarka tarczowa**) is a machine with a **round blade** (disc) — looks like **a huge round saw** mounted on an axle rotating at high speed.

Two types:

- **Stationary circular saw** (on a workbench) — the disc sticks up through the table, the material is fed by hand across the table.
- **Sliding circular saw** (with a carriage) — the disc is on an arm that **drops onto the material** placed on the table.

In a sawmill, most often used for **cross-cutting** boards — trimming to the desired length.

**Characteristic features:**

- **Size**: small to medium.
- **Sound**: **high, bright** + changes pitch when cutting material (louder).
- **Cutting speed**: very fast — a few seconds per cut.
- **Advantage**: precise, clean cut, the piece is ready for further work at once.
- **Disadvantage**: **the most dangerous** of the three saws — the disc rotates at **3000–4000 rpm**, **kickback of the material** can fly at speeds up to 30 m/s.

::: warning
**Kickback** (Polish: **odrzut materiału**) is the most dangerous accident with the circular saw. A piece of wood **flies out of the saw** toward the operator or helper. Speed — like a rifle bullet. **It kills.**

That's why a helper **NEVER stands in the kickback line** — that's the axis of the material being cut by the disc, **straight backwards**. Stand **to the side** of the saw table, **out of the line of cut**, **best behind a guard**. If the operator changes the disc or you adjust something nearby — **the machine must be off** and **the emergency STOP pressed**.
:::

### Emergency STOP button — your right and obligation

Every machine in the sawmill **must have an emergency STOP button**. It is **a big red mushroom** (button) on **a yellow background**. Usually it is:

- **On the machine housing** in an easily reachable place (most often on the side).
- **Sometimes on a post** next to the machine, at waist height.
- **In larger plants** — STOP is also **on the hall wall**, for **the whole production line**.

**When do you have the right to press STOP?**

- When you see that **someone is in danger** (operator, helper, anyone).
- When you see that **the machine is running abnormally** (strange sound, smoke, sparks).
- When you see that **someone doesn't know the machine is running** (e.g. a new worker walked up from behind).
- When **you have doubts** — you press STOP, then you sort it out.

**"You pressed STOP for no reason?"** The foreman's answer is always the same: "**good that you pressed it**". You will never be criticized for **a careful** STOP press. You will be criticized for **not pressing** when you should have.

::: tip
On your first day in the sawmill **walk through** the hall with the foreman and **locate every STOP button**. Every saw, every conveyor, every forklift has one. **Learn them by heart.** After two weeks you have **a STOP map** in your head. It can save your life or a colleague's.
:::

### The helper's zone — where you stand when the machine is running

Whatever the type of saw, as a helper you have **three rules of standing**:

1. **NOT in the line of cut.** The line of cut is the axis **in which the material moves** (in front of the machine, behind, in the direction of kickback).
2. **To the side, outside the guard.** Most machines have **a physical guard** (mesh, plexiglass) — stand **outside it**, never inside.
3. **In the operator's line of sight.** The operator must **see you** — to know that you're safe. Never come up **from behind the machine** without a hand sign that the operator sees you.

These three rules together we call the **"helper's safety triangle"**. After a month you do this **reflexively**.

## Check yourself

1. Which saw has **the shape of a rectangular frame** hanging vertically with several blades?
   - A) Circular saw.
   - B) Frame saw (trak ramowy).
   - C) Band saw.

   → **B**

2. What is **kickback** and why does a helper never stand in the line of cut of a circular saw?
   - A) Kickback is the noise of the machine — the helper stands to the side to hear better.
   - B) Kickback is a piece of wood being shot out of the saw at high speed — it can kill, so the helper stands to the side, never in the line of cut.
   - C) Kickback is a system for rejecting bad boards — the helper stands to the side so as not to block the conveyor.

   → **B**

3. When do you have the right to press the **emergency STOP button** on a machine?
   - A) Only when the foreman explicitly orders it.
   - B) Only with the written consent of the shift manager.
   - C) In any situation where I see a threat to life or health, including my own doubt — "good that you pressed it" is always the right reaction.

   → **C**

## Practice link

In the second week, when you're in the hall, the foreman will show you **the three main saws** of your plant. You'll go on **a tour** — first without machines running (only static viewing), then with **the operator at work** (from a safe distance).

Your task:

1. After two days **you'll recognize the sound** of every saw without looking. "Thud-thud" → frame saw. High whistle → band saw. High, bright tone → circular saw.
2. After a week **you'll point to the STOP button** of every machine in 3 seconds.
3. After two weeks **reflexively** you stand "to the side, outside the guard, in the operator's line of sight" — without thinking.

When the operator starts to trust you, **he'll suggest you feed material** to the band saw. This is **the first moment** when you work **near a machine in motion**. **Listen carefully** to the instructions. **Ask** about every detail. **Don't rush** — the operator prefers a slow helper to an injured helper.

## Trainer notes

**Pedagogical aim:** **recognition** of three types of saws + **understanding kickback** + **locating STOPs**. We don't teach operation (that's outside the helper's role), we teach **a safe position** and **awareness**.

**120-minute rhythm:**
- 10 min — short recap of L5 (hand tools, safety paradox).
- 25 min — group reading of the content with a tutor, with special emphasis on the **helper's safety triangle** and **kickback of the material**.
- 35 min — **a tour of the hall** (if at all possible!) — showing the three saws, listening to their sounds, locating the STOP buttons. **From a safe distance, with the operator leading.**
- 15 min — "Key terms" section.
- 25 min — "Check yourself" + a discussion about a helper's **rights** ("am I allowed to press STOP?" — YES).
- 10 min — buffer.

**Helpful materials:**
- **A tour of the hall** — the best method. Nothing replaces seeing the machines in motion.
- If no access to the hall: **YouTube videos** "frame saw at work", "band saw Wood-Mizer", "circular table saw" — show 2-minute clips from each.
- **Photos of STOP buttons** of various types (red mushroom on yellow background).
- A "**Helper's safety triangle**" poster — can be printed and hung on the wall.

**A common trainee mistake:** thinking "**I would never stand in the line of cut**". Explain: most accidents happen to people who thought the same. **Distraction**, **chatting with a colleague**, **rushing to help** — these are reasons people end up in the line of cut. **Position awareness** must be **reflexive**, not conscious.

**Trainee questions:**
- "Can I touch the saw when it's not working?" — Yes, but **only if the operator knows** you're doing it, and **the machine is disconnected from the main power source** (main switch, not just STOP). Otherwise there is always a risk of **accidental start-up**.
- "What to do if the machine starts smoking?" — **STOP**. Immediately. Then **walk away** from the machine and **call the foreman**. Never try to repair or extinguish on your own — in the sawmill **smoke = burning wood at the blade**, the situation is dangerous.

**Practical recommendation:** arrange with the foreman **a "STOP demo"** — the operator shows what happens when STOP is pressed (silence, light blinks). This is **a strong impression** for the trainee — from that moment STOP is **a concrete thing**, not an abstraction.

Tomorrow we'll see the **machines that don't cut** — forklifts, conveyors, debarkers. These are machines **you walk around all day**. A different safety logic, different traps."
