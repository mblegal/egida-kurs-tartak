## Introduction

Yesterday you learned to **distinguish species**. You already know how pine differs from oak. Today you'll get the second part of the same knowledge: **how wood is named at different stages of work in a sawmill**.

Because the foreman **will never tell you "bring some wood"**. He'll say:

- "Bring a two-metre **deska** (board)."
- "There's a **bal** (thick plank) lying there — move it aside."
- "Check whether the **dłużyca** (long log) has arrived."
- "Throw that **oblina** (slab off-cut) into the bin."

If you don't know how a **bal** differs from a **deska**, or a **dłużyca** from a **kłoda** — you'll stand in the middle of the hall with a stupid look on your face, and the work will stop. After two situations like that, the foreman will stop trusting you.

That's why today we learn **eight Polish words** that are **the language of the yard**. Few? **Few, but the right ones.** With these eight words you'll understand 80% of the orders you'll hear in the first month.

## Goals

After this lesson:

- You can name **in Polish** the eight basic forms of wood in a sawmill: **dłużyca**, **kłoda**, **tarcica**, **deska**, **bal**, **krawędziak**, **łata**, **oblina**.
- You understand the **order** in which the raw material moves through the sawmill — from forest transport to finished product.
- You know that **these words are not trivia** — they are the words you'll hear in the first order of your first day of work.
- You remember that **dimension** is what tells **deska** from **bal** and **łata** from **krawędziak**.

## Content

### Why the foreman doesn't say "wood"

Imagine the foreman saying to you: "**Bring some wood**." What are you supposed to bring? A 30 cm piece? A 4-metre beam? A 800 kg log? **You don't know.**

That's why in a sawmill every form of wood **has its own word**. Thanks to that, instructions are **short and unambiguous**. "Bring a three-metre **łata**" always means the same thing. Everyone in the hall knows what a **łata** is, what its dimensions are, where it lies.

These words are **older than the modern Polish sawmill**. Most of them come from the 18th and 19th centuries, when the first big sawmills were being built in Poland. Today — **unchanged**. A carpenter in 1850 said "bal" the same way your foreman says it in 2026.

This is **good news for you**: you'll learn eight words once and use them for your whole working life — in every sawmill in Poland.

### The sequence: how raw material moves through a sawmill

Wood **does not arrive** at the hall as a finished board. It goes through several stages. Each stage has its own name.

**Stage 1: Forest → sawmill.** The tree is cut in the forest. After felling and limbing (removing branches), the trunk becomes a **dłużyca**.

> **Dłużyca** — a long round log (usually 10–20 m), without branches, with bark. Not yet cut to length. This is how wood is transported from the forest to the sawmill.

**Stage 2: Dłużyca → kłoda.** When it arrives at the sawmill, the dłużyca is cut to shorter lengths — usually 2.5, 4, or 6 metres. Each such piece is a **kłoda**.

> **Kłoda** — a short round log (2–6 m), still with bark, ready for further processing. The "starting raw material" of the sawmill.

**Stage 3: Debarking.** The log goes through the **debarker** (Polish: **korowarka**), which removes the bark. Now the log is "**naked**", ready to be cut.

**Stage 4: The frame saw (sawmill saw).** The naked log enters the **trak** (frame saw) or the **band saw**. There it is **cut lengthwise** into flat pieces. These pieces are already **tarcica**.

> **Tarcica** — the collective name for everything that comes out of the saw. In Polish "**tarcica**" is a general word for wood **already cut into flat pieces**, but not yet sorted.

**Stage 5: Sorting.** Tarcica is not uniform. From a single log come different thicknesses and widths. That's why the helper (and the operator) **sort** it into four main types: deska, bal, krawędziak, łata.

::: info
In a Polish sawmill, "**dłużyca**" is anything longer than 6 metres, and "**kłoda**" is anything shorter. Some sawmills use 5 m or 7 m as the boundary — it depends on the plant. The foreman will show you how it's done where you work. The important thing is that **dłużyca = long, kłoda = short**.
:::

### Four types of tarcica

These are the four most important words you'll hear **every day**. Dimensions vary between plants, but the general rules are the same everywhere.

**Deska** 🪵 — the most common form of tarcica. **Flat, thin, long.**

- Thickness: usually 19–32 mm (1.9–3.2 cm).
- Width: 8–25 cm.
- Length: 2–6 m.
- Use: floors, ceilings, cladding, furniture, packaging.

The deska is the "**paper**" of the sawmill — the most universal material, we make the most of it.

**Bal** 🟫 — a **thick** board. It's **a board, but thicker**.

- Thickness: 50–100 mm (5–10 cm). That's the main difference.
- Width: 18–32 cm (usually wide).
- Length: 3–6 m.
- Use: workbenches, stairs, dance floors, structural underlays.

The bal is **heavy** — a 4 m oak bal can weigh 60–80 kg. **Always carried in a pair.**

**Krawędziak** 🟩 — wood with a **square cross-section** (or nearly so). Looks like a "beam".

- Cross-section dimensions: 10×10, 12×12, 15×15, 20×20 cm. **The sides are equal** or nearly so.
- Length: 2–6 m.
- Use: roof structures, load-bearing posts, roof trusses, fences.

The krawędziak is **strong** — because it's thick on all sides, not just flat like a board.

**Łata** 🟨 — **thin and narrow**. A small piece of wood, but long.

- Cross-section dimensions: 4×6, 5×7, 5×8 cm (small).
- Length: 2–6 m.
- Use: structures under roof coverings (shingles, tiles), elements of cladding.

The łata is "**the smallest of the four**" — easy to lift, easy to break. It often lies in stacks of dozens at a time in the yard.

::: example
Imagine you're building a wooden house. You start with **krawędziaks** (wall posts and roof beams — the skeleton of the house). On the outside wall you'll nail **deskas** (cladding). On the roof, under the tiles, you'll lay **łatas**. And if you want to build a terrace or solid stairs — you'll use **bals**. **Four different names, four different places in one house.** That's how you know why a sawmill has to tell them apart.
:::

### Oblina — by-product

Looking at a freshly cut log, you'll notice that **not everything from it becomes a clean board**. The first and last cut from each log is a **non-symmetric piece** — flat on one side, and **with bark left on** and a rounded edge on the other.

> **Oblina** — a piece of tarcica with **one side that keeps the roundness** of the log (and often with bark). A by-product of cutting.

Oblina doesn't go to the customer who buys boards. It goes to:

- **Pallets** (simple, made from the cheapest tarcica).
- **Briquettes** (after being cut up and pressed).
- **Firewood**.
- **Compost** (for garden centres).

For a helper, oblina is **always either waste or second-grade material**. You never hand an oblina to an operator who asked for a "deska". You'll see the difference at once: a deska has **four straight sides**, an oblina has **three straight and one round**.

::: tip
On your first day at work, when you have a free moment, **walk over to the bin with the oblinas**. Take a piece in your hand. You'll see: on one side it's a **smooth board**, on the other — a **rounded piece** with bark. That's the best image you can have in your head. After one touch **you'll never get it wrong again**.
:::

### A small map of the road through the sawmill

Imagine a pine log cut down in a Polish forest:

1. **Forest** — the tree is felled. The trunk is a **dłużyca** (12 m, with bark).
2. **Truck transport** to the sawmill.
3. **Cross-cutting** — the dłużyca is cut into three **kłodas** of 4 m each.
4. **Debarker** — the bark is removed, the logs are "**naked**".
5. **Frame saw (trak)** — the log is cut lengthwise. Out come:
   - 2 **oblinas** (the first and last cuts),
   - 8 **deskas** (clean, flat pieces from the middle),
   - 1 **krawędziak** (from the very middle, with the pith),
6. **Sorting** in the yard.
7. **Drying** in the kiln (more on this in lesson 8).
8. **Packaging** and sale.

That's "the road of one log". Your work as a helper concerns mainly **stages 6, 7, and 8** — sorting, transport around the hall, building stacks. But **you understand** where the material comes from and what it's called.

::: warning
**Don't change the names**. If the foreman says "bal" and you think "that looks like a thick board" — **say "bal"**. The Polish names are **precise**. A "thick board" in one sawmill is a **bal**, in another it's still "a thick board". Across Poland **bal = bal**, **łata = łata**. Learn the Polish words once — and you'll be understood in every plant.
:::

## Key terms

- **dłużyca** 🌲 — long log (10–20 m, with bark) / rollizo largo / довгий кругляк
- **kłoda** 🪵 — log (2–6 m, with bark) / tronco / колода
- **tarcica** 🟤 — sawn timber (general term) / madera aserrada / пиломатеріали
- **deska** 🪵 — board (thin and wide) / tabla / дошка
- **bal** 🟫 — thick board / plank (5–10 cm thick) / tablón grueso / товста дошка / брус
- **krawędziak** 🟩 — square timber (square cross-section) / escuadría / брусок квадратний
- **łata** 🟨 — batten (small cross-section, long) / listón / рейка
- **oblina** 🌳 — slab / off-cut (with bark on one side) / costero / горбиль
- **trak** ⚙️ — frame saw (machine cutting logs lengthwise) / sierra de bastidor / лісопильна рама
- **korowarka** ⚙️ — debarker / descortezadora / окорувальник

## Check yourself

1. What is a **dłużyca**?
   - A) A short, thick piece of wood ready for cutting.
   - B) A long round log with bark, straight from the forest, before being cut into shorter logs.
   - C) A flat board 25 cm wide.

   → **B**

2. How does a **bal** differ from a **deska**?
   - A) A bal is round, a deska is flat.
   - B) A bal is thicker (5–10 cm) and heavier; a deska is thinner (1.9–3.2 cm) and lighter.
   - C) A bal is made only of oak, a deska only of pine.

   → **B**

3. The foreman says: "Take that two-metre **krawędziak**." What are you supposed to bring?
   - A) A short piece of wood with roughly square cross-section (e.g. 12×12 cm), 2 m long.
   - B) A thick board 2 m long.
   - C) A thin batten with bark scraps, 2 m long.

   → **A**

## Practice link

The day after this lesson, **you go out to the yard** with the foreman. The foreman will show you **physically** all eight forms: dłużyca usually lies on the entry ramp, kłodas — by the debarker, tarcica — in the sorting hall, oblina — in the "industrial waste" bins.

Your task: **touch each form** and name it in Polish. The foreman will correct you if you mix them up. This is **the fastest way to learn** — see, touch, name, repeat the next day.

After a week you'll no longer have to think — **"bal" will automatically connect** in your head to a thick board in the hall, "łata" — to thin slats in a stack next to the saw.

Helpers who in the first week don't start using these words spontaneously **have problems later**. So practise consciously: **when you see material, say its name in Polish in your head**. It takes five days — after that it's automatic.

## Trainer notes

**Pedagogical aim:** to build the **basic professional vocabulary**. After this lesson the trainee should know 8 Polish words by heart and be able to **point** to each of them in the yard or in a photo. This is **the only lesson in W2** that **requires** memorizing words — the other lessons build understanding, this one builds vocabulary.

**120-minute rhythm:**
- 10 min — short recap of L1+L2 (anatomical structure + 5 species).
- 30 min — reading of the content with a tutor, **with emphasis on pronunciation** of the Polish names. Repeat every word three times: "dłu-ży-ca", "kra-wę-dziak", "o-bli-na".
- 35 min — **visual exercise**: lay 8 photos on the table (one for each form). Each participant draws one, comes up, and names it. Others confirm or correct. **Second round**: lay 8 Polish labels (on cards), participants match them to photos.
- 15 min — "Key terms" section — saying the Polish names out loud (in chorus, then individually).
- 20 min — "Check yourself" + a discussion: was any form **new** to the trainees (sometimes some know "deska" and "kłoda" from home, others don't).
- 10 min — buffer.

**Helpful materials:**
- **8 A5 photos** — essential. You can print from the internet (terms "sawmill long log", "oak plank", "structural square timber", "roof batten", "pine slab off-cut") — all easy to find.
- **8 cards** with the Polish names written in BIG letters.
- If possible: **a physical visit to the yard** instead of part of the theory. Show **real** dłużycas, kłodas, deskas, bals, krawędziaks, łatas, oblinas. This is **the best method**.

**Common trainee mistake:** **confusing "bal" with "balia"** (balia is a wooden tub, e.g. a washing tub — a completely different word!). Say clearly: "**bal** is a thick board. **Balia** is something else."

**Pronunciation tip:**
- "**dłużyca**" pronounce slowly: "**doo-zhyy-tsa**". The letter "ż" is like English "zh" in "treasure".
- "**krawędziak**" — stress on the second syllable: "kra-**vehn**-jak".
- "**oblina**" — simple: "o-blee-na", stress on the second syllable.

**Trainee questions:**
- "And **belka** (beam)? I've heard the word." — A "belka" is **a common name for any large rectangular piece of structural wood**. It can be a bal or a krawędziak. But in a sawmill we use **more precise words**. "Belka" is colloquial speech, "bal" / "krawędziak" — professional speech.
- "And **klocek** (block)?" — A klocek is a **small piece** of wood, short. We don't use it in a sawmill as a sorting category, but you may hear it. "Give me a klocek under that log" means "give me a piece of wood to put under it".

**Practical recommendation:** **do short recaps** during the following lessons of W2. Start each lesson with "who remembers — what is a **łata**?" After a few days the trainees answer at once.
