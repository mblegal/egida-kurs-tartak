## Introduction

Wednesday, the eighth day of the second week. Monday: Sofía understood what the number 38% means. Tuesday: she obtained that number correctly. Today she steps out onto the log yard, because **before any log goes to the saw carriage, somebody has to decide how to crosscut it into logs**. That decision happens twice: first in the forest (harvester operator), second at the sawmill yard (foreman). Today Sofía watches the second decision and learns what information the foreman holds in his head at that moment.

At 6:50 a truck arrives from the State Forests (Lasy Państwowe): two oak trunks, each about 14 metres long, 55 cm diameter at the base, 32 cm at the top. **These are whole logs** (in Polish: *dłużyce*) – entire tree trunks, felled by a harvester and hauled to the sawmill intact, without being divided into shorter logs. Foreman Marek pulls an industrial crayon out of his pocket (white, thick, waterproof) and motions Sofía over: "Come. I'll show you how to read this."

This lesson is about **what the foreman sees when he looks at a fifteen-metre trunk, and why the same trunk, crosscut differently, may be worth 30% more or less**. You will not learn to cut today. You will learn to understand the cut, so that in six months, when you become a junior operator with authorisation, you will know what you are looking at in the crayon mark drawn on the bark.

## Objectives

After this lesson:

1. You understand what a **whole log** (*dłużyca*) is: an entire tree trunk, usually 10–20 m long, delivered to the sawmill intact, before being divided into sawmill logs.
2. You know the **three quality zones of a whole log**: the **butt log** (*odziomek*, first 3–5 m from the ground, the most valuable, knot-free, class A), the **middle log** (*środek*, 5–10 m, class B/C), the **top log** (*wierzchołek*, the last 2–4 m, class D, often for firewood or pulp).
3. You know why **the foreman, not the operator and not the helper, decides where the cut marks go**: it is a commercial decision (value maximisation) and a planning decision (matching orders), requiring knowledge of the market, prices, stock levels, and the skill of reading defects from the bark.
4. You know the **nominal log lengths** used in Polish sawmills: 2.5 m (short log), 3 m, 4 m (most common), 5 m, 6 m (special orders). You know that nominal length plus a 5–10 cm overlength at each end equals the actual cutting length.
5. You understand what the **foreman's crayon mark** means: the location of the crosscut, a sequential number, an arrow pointing towards the butt (so the operator knows which side is "the good one"), sometimes a short class code (A/B/C/D).
6. You know **your role as a helper during whole-log handling**: you assist with unloading (front-end loader, never by hand), with placing the log on rests (sawhorses) for crosscutting, with receiving the logs after the cut. **You do not step under the whole log.** **You do not stand in the line of the trunk's kickback during the cut.**
7. You know the **three basic hazards of whole-log handling**: the trunk rolling (instability on the ground), kickback during cutting (internal stresses), strapping failure on the truck during unloading.

## Content

### What a whole log is and why it is hauled intact

In the forest the harvester fells the tree, cuts off branches (delimbing) and **cuts the trunk into segments**. If the harvester operator has an order for "oak logs 4 m class A for veneer", the trunks come back already crosscut. But if the order reads "mixed-class oak roundwood", or if the sawmill wants to make the decision itself, the trunk comes back **intact as a whole log** (*dłużyca*), and the decision is made at the sawmill.

::: info
**Why haul a whole log rather than already-crosscut logs?**
- **Flexibility**: the sawmill foreman knows current orders and stock levels better than the harvester operator in the forest. He can kill two birds with one stone on a single trunk.
- **Less waste**: every crosscut means a loss of 5–10 cm (kerf width plus end-trim overlength). One cut in the forest plus three at the sawmill equals less waste than four cuts in the forest made "by eye".
- **Better price for thick butt logs**: a whole log with a 55 cm diameter butt will go to the band-saw carriage of a large primary breakdown machine, which turns it into veneer flitches. Crosscut in the forest to 4 m, it could have landed as a "standard log" at a price 3× lower.
:::

**Sofía's delivery today**: two oak whole logs, 14 m long. The first has a 55 cm butt (the first 4 m from the ground, thick, knot-free – promising class A for furniture veneer). The second is thinner (42 cm at the base) and has a cluster of large knots visible at 6 m height where branches were cut off.

### Three zones of a whole log: why the same trunk has different value at different heights

A tree grows from the bottom up, and **the oldest, calmest wood is in the butt**. That is where the trunk was thickest, where it grew the longest, where it sent out the fewest side branches. The higher you go, the younger the wood, the more knots from branches, and the greater the spiral grain (the tree twists under the wind). That is why the market pays radically different prices for logs from different heights:

::: example
**Indicative price bands for pedunculate oak (2025, Poland):**

| Zone | Length | Diameter | Class | Indicative price |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Butt log** (1–4 m) | 3–4 m | ≥40 cm | A (veneer, furniture joinery) | 800–1400 zł/m³ |
| **Middle log** (4–10 m) | 3–4 m | 30–40 cm | B/C (structural lumber) | 400–700 zł/m³ |
| **Top log** (10–14 m) | 2–3 m | 20–30 cm | C/D (pallet wood, firewood) | 150–300 zł/m³ |

**The same trunk is worth 3–5 times more at the butt than at the top.** A mistake in the cut mark on the butt costs the sawmill 500–1000 zł on a single whole log.
:::

**Butt log** (*odziomek*; German *Stammholz*, English *butt log*): the first 3–5 m from the ground. Thickest, most homogeneous. For oak and beech: veneer stock, furniture joinery. For pine and spruce: roof structure, glued large-section elements. **The biggest traps of the butt log**: root rot (especially in oaks from old forests), false heartwood, wind shakes (in very large trees).

**Middle log** (*środek*; English *middle log*, German *Mittelholz*): 4–10 m. Most often class B/C. Sound knots (dark, round, generally healthy) or loose knots (that fall out during machining). Structural lumber, graded floorboards, building elements.

**Top log** (*wierzchołek*; English *top log*, German *Zopfholz*): 2–4 m at the top. Thin (usually under 25 cm), with numerous knot clusters and heavy spiral grain. Class C/D: pallet wood, packaging boards, briquette sawdust, firewood. In oak and beech: paper pulp (profitable); in pine: often wood wool for horticulture.

### What the foreman sees and what he marks in crayon

Marek walks along the first whole log, presses a tape measure to it every few metres, feels the bark with his hand, taps it with a hammer (tapping reveals internal rot – a dull sound instead of a ringing one). After five minutes he takes the crayon and **draws four crosscut marks**:

- Mark 1 at **4.10 m from the butt** (labelled "1" with a small arrow pointing down towards the base, letter "A" in a circle).
- Mark 2 at **8.15 m** (labelled "2", "B").
- Mark 3 at **11.20 m** (labelled "3", "C").
- The rest (11.20 to 14 m, about 2.8 m) goes unmarked – that is the top log, it goes to firewood together with the branches.

::: tip
**Crayon convention**: in most Polish sawmills, a vertical line means **the location of the cut** (this is where the chainsaw or the crosscut mini-gang saw will pass). The arrow **always points towards the butt** – that is, towards the side that will go into the more valuable lumber. The letter in the circle (A/B/C/D) is **the target order** – from the sequence of cuts the operator can read that log 1 is headed for a veneer flitch (client X's order), log 2 for structural use, log 3 for pallet wood.
:::

**Why three logs, not five?** Because Marek has a client order in his head: a furniture joinery needs 6 m³ of class-A oak butt logs by Friday. The second whole log will yield only middle and top (due to the thin butt and knotty trunk). Marek plans **to cut two 4 m butt logs out of the first whole log today**, rather than three shorter 2.5 m pieces or a single 5 m piece. This is a decision: 4 m gives a good proportion for a 55 cm diameter (it will fit the band-saw carriage), 5 m would be hard to turn on the table, and 2.5 m is too short a log for the veneer customer.

**This decision is the foreman's, not the operator's, not yours.** The MoE Regulation of 14 April 2000 (Polish OSH regulation for woodworking machinery) § 3 states that the operator performs work **in accordance with the instruction of the employer or the person directing the work**. In practice the foreman is the "directing person" – his crayon mark is an order. The operator may ask, may propose an alternative, but the final decision is sealed in crayon.

### Your role as a helper at the whole log: what you do, what you do NOT do

At M2 week 2 Sofía is **not yet operating whole-log crosscutting**. That task belongs to a senior operator holding a chainsaw authorisation (Regulation of the Minister of Labour of 28 July 2023 on portable chainsaws), or to a crosscut mini-gang saw (operated by someone with station-specific training). Sofía **assists**:

::: warning
**What you do as helper/junior at a whole log:**
1. **You assist the front-end loader operator** with unloading from the truck. You stand **well back (at least 5 m from the unloading zone)**, within the loader operator's line of sight, wearing a hard hat and a high-visibility vest. You do not step under the raised whole log.
2. **You help set the log rests (special wooden or steel supports, *kozły* / sawhorses) under the intended cut points**, so that the whole log lies stable and the cut does not pinch the saw (the trunk must not press on the blade from both sides during cutting).
3. **After the cut you receive the logs** (using a cant hook, in Polish *hak gospodarczy*) – you roll them along the rests onto the stack, according to the class on the crayon mark.
4. **You report visible defects to the foreman** that he may have missed on the first walk-around – e.g. a deep end check, resin bleeding from a knot hole, discolouration signalling rot.

**What you do NOT do:**
- **You do NOT step under the whole log** – ever. Not even for a moment, to pick something up from the other side. Walk around.
- **You do NOT stand in the line of the trunk's axis** during the crosscut. A whole log under tension (because it is not lying perfectly) can "snap" its end 1–2 m towards the axis, not towards the cut.
- **You do NOT crosscut the whole log with a chainsaw without authorisation and without the foreman's clearance.** You operate the crosscut mini-gang saw only after station-specific training (M2 week 3).
- **You do NOT move the foreman's crayon mark**, even if "it seems to you that it could be done better". Ask Marek before you propose. He knows about orders you cannot see.
:::

### Hazards specific to the whole log

A trunk is not a log. A trunk is three times longer, significantly less stable, and under a harder-to-predict distribution of internal stresses.

**Hazard 1: rolling**. A round 14 m trunk, even on flat ground, will rotate if the yard is uneven by 2–3 degrees. State Labour Inspectorate (PIP) statistics 2019–2023: **11% of fatal accidents on the log yard are a trunk or log rolling onto a worker**. That is why the whole log always lies on rests or is chocked with wooden wedges on both sides.

**Hazard 2: kickback during the cut**. A trunk resting on two sawhorses works like a lever. When you crosscut it, **the weight of the severed piece drops under its own weight, and the end of the trunk on the other side jumps up** – sometimes 30–50 cm. If you were standing in that line, you get hit in the head. That is why the standard is to **stand on the cut side, 2 m from the saw line, off to the side, never in the axis**.

**Hazard 3: strapping**. A truck carrying whole logs has tensioning straps, usually two per trunk, tightened to 2000–4000 kg of force. **A strap snapping during unloading throws the buckle 20–40 m at 80–100 km/h**. Hence the rule: **while a strap is being released you stand on the opposite side of the truck from the released strap, behind the cab**. The loader operator releases the first strap, then the helper releases the second – the two straps are never released simultaneously, because the whole load would slide off.

## Link to practice

Tomorrow (lesson 4) you will meet **the wood defects relevant to primary breakdown**: spiral grain, false heartwood, root rot, bark pockets. These are the same defects Marek was "tapping with a hammer" at the whole log. Before then, today:

- **Ask the foreman to show you a whole log with the marks on it next time**. Try to read the arrows and letters before Marek explains.

## Trainer notes

**Leading analogy**: a whole log is like a long baguette. The beginning (the butt) is the best, the middle is standard, the end (the top) is the heel – edible too, but not for a fancy lunch. Every learner knows a baguette, the analogy works across cultures. Extension: the baker cuts the baguette at points that avoid making a hole in the "slice with jam" for the customer – the same way the foreman chooses the cut point so as not to lose the butt.

**Why three zones, not five**: a five-step classification (butt–stem–middle–near top–far top) exists in the literature, but for a junior operator it is needlessly fine-grained. Three zones plus four quality classes (A/B/C/D from l6) are enough to understand the foreman's decision. Leave the full five-zone classification for master-level training.

**Where this lesson returns**: m2-w2-l4 wood defects (what Marek was looking for when tapping), m2-w2-l5 product assortments (what is produced after cutting), m2-w2-l6 classes A/B/C/D (formalising the "letter in the circle"), m2-w3-l1 band saw (where the butt log ends up), m3-w1-l3 harvester plus forest process (the first decision, in the forest).

**Traps for migrant learners**:
- "Whole log = big log, why a different word?" – **no**, a whole log is a commercial category (the entire trunk), a log is a segment after crosscutting. Show photos of both. In Spanish and Ukrainian the distinction already exists (rollizo largo vs tronco, кругляк vs колода), worth reinforcing in the glossary.
- Confusing "butt log" with "root" – the butt log is the **first 3–5 m of the trunk above ground**, the roots are below ground and do not go to the sawmill. Learners from countries with a tropical-forest tradition (Colombia, Venezuela, parts of Honduras) may fall into this trap because the commercial classification of timber there looks different from the temperate-forest one.
- "Since the butt log is the most valuable, let's cut everything into butt logs" – **no**, from an old trunk you get one butt log, the rest has to be used. This rule returns in m2-w4-l1 cutting planning.

**Case Sofía – to be expanded**: why is Marek not giving her the cut today? Because crosscutting a whole log with a chainsaw requires authorisations (training plus medical clearance plus an entry in the station workbook) that Sofía will receive after chainsaw training in week 4 of module 2. For now Sofía **understands the decision**. Understanding before doing – that is the core of M2.

**Gesture glossary**: the "foreman's crayon mark" often uses local shorthand in sawmills (e.g. "F" for veneer/*fornir*, "K" for structure/*konstrukcja*, "P" for pallet wood/*paletka*). When a learner arrives at a sawmill, the first day she should ask what shorthand is in use. The EGIDA standard is A/B/C/D, matching the quality classes in PN-EN standards.

**Hazards 1-2-3 as scenario exercises**: the scenario "log rolling on the yard, bystander's reaction" fits the practical exercises (art. A3), and so does "strap snap during unloading" and "kickback during the crosscut". These three scenarios, together with the bleeding-control scenario from l8, make up the full hazard family of weeks 1+2 of module 2.
