## Introduction

Welcome to **Week 3**. Two weeks ago we started with **OHS (occupational health and safety)** — so you would understand **why a sawmill is different** and **where the hazards hide**. In Week 2 we talked about the **material** — what a log is, what sawn timber is, species, defects, dry wood vs wet wood.

Now we enter **Week 3: Processes**. That means — **what you actually do** during a working day. Not theory, not hazards, not material — just **activities**. But before we get into the specifics (manual transport, stacking, feeding, hand tools, housekeeping, marking) — **one foundational lesson first**.

This lesson answers the question: **who are you in the sawmill?**

You are not the saw operator. You are not the warehouse keeper. You are not the forklift driver. You are a **helper** (in Polish: **pomocnik**). And that word — "helper" — means **something specific**. Not just "someone who helps". It has a **limit**. There is a sentence that should be **your mantra** during your first months at work:

> **"I help, but I don't operate."**

What does that mean? That you **help carry a board to the saw** — but you **don't start the saw yourself**. You **help load a log onto the cart** — but you **don't drive the cart yourself**, until you have the licence. You **help sort timber on the yard** — but the decision **which species goes where** is made by **the foreman or operator**, not by you.

It sounds like a limitation. **Because it is a limitation.** And it's **your protection**. Migrant workers who try to "prove themselves" and grab the machines without licences — **end up in hospital**. Migrant workers who say "I don't operate, I don't know how" — **stay alive, keep working, keep learning, and six months later have their licences** and operate the machines themselves.

In this lesson you'll understand **where you are in the production chain** (between whom and whom), **what a helper is allowed and not allowed to do**, and **how to react** when someone says: "come on, do it for me".

## Objectives

After this lesson:

- You understand that **"helper" is a specific role**, not "anyone who helps" — with a boundary of duties and **authorisations**.
- You know **where in the production chain** you stand: **between the machine operator and the warehouse keeper / forklift driver**, supporting both but replacing neither.
- You can say **"no, I don't have the licence"** when someone tells you to operate a machine — and you know that is **your right**, not your weakness.
- You understand that **every day in the sawmill starts with motion** (walking onto the floor, PPE check, first task) — and **ends with housekeeping and a report**.
- You know the **five typical helper tasks** during the day and can name them.

## Content

### The production chain in a sawmill — who does what

Several roles work in a sawmill at the same time. Each one has **their slice of the process**. If you don't understand who is who, you'll **always be confused**. Let's look at it from the beginning, **from the gate to the outbound loading of finished timber**:

1. **Truck driver** brings **logs from the forest**. Unloads on the **log yard**. His job ends on the yard — he doesn't go further in.

2. **Forklift / loader driver** (or tractor operator) **moves logs from the yard to the sawmilling line**. Holds a **UDT licence** (Polish Office of Technical Inspection) for his machine. Without that licence, nobody may operate the tractor or forklift.

3. **Sawing line operator** (band saw or circular saw) **cuts the log into boards**. He is **the most important person in the production line** — the **quality of timber** depends on him. Holds licences, training, often many years of experience.

4. **Helper to the operator** — **THIS IS YOU** — **feeds** the operator with the next pieces, **receives** the cut timber, **sorts on the yard**, helps **build stacks**.

5. **Warehouse keeper** takes the finished timber into the **warehouse** or issues it for sale. Issues paperwork. Has access to the system.

6. **Foreman** (shift supervisor) **organises the work** of everyone above. Says who does what, when the break is, when the end of shift is.

**Where are you?** **Between point 3 and point 5.** On one side you have the **operator** (who cuts), on the other the **warehouse keeper** (who receives). You **carry, feed, stack, sort** — but you **do not decide** on cutting or paperwork. **You are the bridge**.

::: info
**Why is this important?** Because many helpers — especially migrants who feel they "have to prove themselves" — **cross the line**. They try to "help" the operator by starting the machine. They try to "speed up" the forklift driver by jumping behind the wheel. They try to "save time" for the warehouse keeper by entering a dispatch into the computer. **Each of those moves breaks your authorisations** — and in case of an accident **you have no insurance cover**, because you stepped outside your role.
:::

### What "I help, but I don't operate" means

A sawmill has machines. Many machines. **Band saws**, **circular saws**, **planers**, **forklifts**, **overhead cranes**, **receiving conveyors**. Almost every one of them requires **authorisation** — either **completed and documented workplace training**, or a **UDT licence (Office of Technical Inspection)**, or both.

The helper **does not have those authorisations** during the first months. That means:

- **NO** — you don't start the saw. (The operator starts it.)
- **NO** — you don't sit on a forklift, loader, tractor. (Someone with a UDT licence does.)
- **NO** — you don't operate a planer, router, stationary grinder. (The operator or a qualified worker.)
- **NO** — you don't change the saw band, don't adjust the guides, don't lubricate bearings of running machines. (Maintenance team or the trained operator.)
- **NO** — you don't enter anything into the warehouse system. (The warehouse keeper.)

What **is** the helper allowed to do?

- **YES** — feed material to the operator (from behind the working-zone line, on his signal).
- **YES** — receive material from the outbound conveyor.
- **YES** — carry timber manually (within limits) or on a hand truck.
- **YES** — build a stack with stickers under supervision.
- **YES** — sweep sawdust, chips, wood waste.
- **YES** — mark timber with chalk or marker (per instructions).
- **YES** — carry wedges, bearers, stickers onto the yard.
- **YES** — use **hand tools** (hand saw, hand plane, measuring rule, wedge and hammer).

::: warning
**Golden rule**: if the tool or machine **has a plug or a combustion engine** — a helper without a licence **does not start it**. Hand tools (without motor) — go ahead, that's your world. Powered hand tools (angle grinder, drill) — **only after workplace training** and only when the foreman tells you "you may".
:::

### "Come on, do it for me" — how to react

A moment will come when the operator or a colleague says:

- "Mate, cover me at the saw for a minute, I need to make a phone call."
- "Take the forklift and grab that pallet, I'm busy."
- "Switch on the planer, I'll be right back."
- "Enter it in the computer that I issued 2 cubic metres for order X."

**In all these cases the answer is:**

> **"No, I don't have the licence. I'll wait or I'll call someone."**

This is not **an insult**. This is not **lack of engagement**. This is **a basic OHS rule** which the plant manager **expects from you**. If the operator gets offended — that's **his problem**, not yours. If he insists — **go to the foreman and say**: "X told me to turn the saw on, I don't have the licence, what should I do?" The foreman **will handle it**.

::: example
**A real story** (incidents reported by the Polish Labour Inspectorate PIP in Podlaskie voivodeship): a helper from Ukraine, first week of work at a sawmill. The saw operator stepped out for a cigarette and said: "Mate, cut these five boards, I'll be back in a second." The helper, wanting to be helpful, started the saw. On the third board **the band snapped** — a fragment of the band hit his hand. **Four fingers amputated**. In hospital it was established that **he did not have a licence**, so **the Social Insurance (ZUS) did not recognise it as a workplace accident** — he had to pursue the case in civil court for two years to get compensation. **All because he couldn't say "no".**
:::

**Labour Code (Article 210)**: an employee has the **right to refrain from performing work** if direct danger threatens him. That means, if someone tells you to do something you are not authorised to do — **you have the right to refuse**, and the employer **cannot dismiss you for it**.

### A typical helper day — five tasks

What does a helper actually do during an 8-hour working day? Most of the time falls into **five main categories**:

**1. Manual transport of material** (boards, laths, wedges, stickers). That will be your lesson number **2 this week**. Limits, technique, when you call a second person.

**2. Unloading and storage on the yard** (logs just delivered, timber coming out of the mill, finished timber awaiting dispatch). Lessons **3** and **4** of this week.

**3. Feeding and receiving material for the operator**. The most important activity in the production line — and the most demanding in terms of **synchronisation**. Lesson **5**.

**4. Using hand tools** (hand saw, plane, rule, wedge) — because sometimes you need to trim something without a machine. Lesson **6**.

**5. Workstation housekeeping** — sawdust, chips, wood waste. That is **incredibly important**, because an untidy floor = **slips + fires**. Lesson **7**.

Plus **lesson 8** — **marking and identification of material** — because every board and log in the sawmill has a **history** (species, dimension, operator, date) which you **must know how to read**.

::: tip
**What a typical day looks like**: 7:00 arrival at the station and PPE check (10 min). 7:10–9:30 first task — most often helping to start production (machine start-up, feeding first logs). 9:30–9:45 break. 9:45–12:30 main production — feeding, receiving, sorting. 12:30–13:00 lunch. 13:00–15:30 continuation, often task change (e.g. stacking). 15:30 end of production — **housekeeping, marking, report to the foreman**. 16:00 leave. **You start with motion (7:00) and finish with housekeeping + report (15:30–16:00)** — this structure repeats every day.
:::

### Where you stand, and where you don't stand

One important operational note. **In a sawmill there are places where a helper NEVER stands alone**:

- **Under a working overhead crane** (gripper with a log above your head).
- **Behind the back of the saw operator** (kickback, flying chips).
- **Between logs on the yard** (a log may roll, especially a wet one).
- **On top of a timber stack** ("to see from above") — the stack can collapse.
- **Directly under a working dust-extraction planer outlet** (failure = ejection).
- **In the transport corridor** (forklifts).

You'll learn about those places **gradually** in the following lessons. But **already now** imprint this rule:

> **When you're not sure where to stand — stand to the side and ask the foreman.**

That sentence has saved the life of more than one migrant in the first week of work.

### The helper's first rule — revision from Week 1

In the first lesson of Week 1 we said:

> **If you don't know what to do — stand, watch, ask. Don't touch.**

That is still **the first rule**. In Week 3 **we add the second**:

> **If you don't have the licence — you don't operate. End of discussion.**

These two rules together are **your shield** during the first 3–6 months of work. Later, when you complete training, get UDT certificates, learn the machines from the inside — the rules will **expand**. But **they will never disappear**. Even an operator with 20 years of experience still says "I don't know, I don't touch" on a machine he has not operated — and "I don't have the licence" on something he hasn't taken a course on.

**That is not weakness. That is professionalism.**

## Summary

Today you understood **who you are in the sawmill**:

- You are a **helper (pomocnik)** — a specific role between the machine operator and the warehouse keeper. Not an operator, not a forklift driver, not a warehouse keeper. A **helper**.
- Your mantra: **"I help, but I don't operate."** You help to carry, feed, receive, sort. **You don't start machines or forklifts.**
- Your **right to refuse** (Article 210 of the Labour Code) — if someone tells you to do something without a licence, **you have the right to say "no"** and the employer cannot dismiss you for it.
- A typical day is **5 task categories**: manual transport, unloading/storage, feeding the operator, hand tools, housekeeping. Plus marking. Those are your **lessons 2–8 of this week**.
- A day **starts with motion** (PPE check, first task) and **ends with housekeeping + report**. That structure repeats.

**Two shield-rules**: (1) don't know — don't touch. (2) don't have the licence — don't operate. With them **you'll survive the first months**. Without them — **I don't guarantee anything**.

## Key terms

- 🔧 **Pomocnik** (pl) — operator's helper (en) — ayudante del operario (es) — помічник оператора (uk). Role in a sawmill: supports the machine operator, carries and sorts material, **does not operate the machines**. **That's YOU**.

- 🏭 **Ciąg produkcyjny** (pl) — production line (en) — línea de producción (es) — виробничий ланцюг (uk). Sequence of stations from log arrival to timber dispatch: driver → forklift/tractor operator → mill operator → helper → warehouse keeper, with the foreman as coordinator.

- 🚜 **Operator** (pl) — machine operator (en) — operario de máquina (es) — оператор машини (uk). Person with a licence operating a specific machine (saw, planer, forklift). Your "other side" — you feed him material.

- 👷 **Brygadzista** (pl) — shift foreman / team leader (en) — jefe de turno / capataz (es) — бригадир / майстер зміни (uk). Shift supervisor, organises work, resolves conflicts ("X told me to start the machine"). Your first point of contact in case of doubt.

- 📦 **Magazynier** (pl) — warehouse keeper (en) — almacenero (es) — комірник / кладовник (uk). Person receiving finished timber into the warehouse and issuing paperwork. You don't type into the system for him.

- 📜 **Uprawnienia UDT** (pl) — UDT (Technical Supervision) authorisations (en) — autorizaciones UDT (licencia técnica equivalente) (es) — допуск УДТ (дозвіл на обслуговування техніки) (uk). Formal permit to operate forklifts, overhead cranes, certain saws. **Earned by examination**.

- 🛡️ **Art. 210 Kodeksu pracy** (pl) — Article 210 of the Labour Code (en) — artículo 210 del Código del Trabajo (es) — стаття 210 Трудового кодексу (uk). Employee's right to refrain from work in hazardous conditions — including lack of authorisation. The employer cannot dismiss for this.

- ⛔ **Pomagam, ale nie obsługuję** (pl) — "I help, but I don't operate" (en) — "ayudo, pero no manejo" (es) — «допомагаю, але не керую» (uk). The helper's mantra during the first months of work. Protects against the temptation to "prove yourself" and against accidents with loss of insurance cover.

- 📋 **Szkolenie stanowiskowe** (pl) — job-specific training / workplace induction (en) — formación específica del puesto (es) — навчання на робочому місці (uk). First training after joining work (minimum 8 hours), concluded with a test and a record in personnel files. Without it you don't touch the machines.

## Trainer's notes

**Pedagogical goal**: to set the **role frame** for the whole of Week 3. All subsequent lessons (l2–l8) will be **technical details** — but without an understanding of "who am I", the learner won't apply them. That is a classic mistake: to teach the migrant "how to lift 30 kg" without first setting "who you are in this plant at all". Lesson **l1 W3 = the keystone of the week**.

**120-minute rhythm**:
- **0–15 min**: opening, context from W1 and W2, preview of W3. Question to the group: "Who knows what a helper in a sawmill is?" — collect answers, don't comment. Return to them at the end.
- **15–40 min**: production-chain diagram on the board. **Draw** 6 roles (driver → forklift driver → operator → helper → warehouse keeper, foreman as coordinator). Learner draws in their notebook.
- **40–65 min**: the "I help, but I don't operate" rule. List of "what is allowed / what is not" — **first ask the group, only then show the table from the material**. Let them make mistakes — a corrected mistake sticks better.
- **65–85 min**: the "come on, do it for me" scenario. **Act it out in pairs**: one learner = operator, the other = helper. Task: operator asks to start the saw, helper refuses. Different versions (gentle operator, pressuring operator, close friend). The PIP case with the Ukrainian helper — **present as fact, not as a scare story**.
- **85–105 min**: typical day + where not to stand. If possible — a short walk through the hall (if the situation is safe), pointing out "red zone" places.
- **105–120 min**: repetition of the two shield-rules. **Oral quiz** — ask each learner to answer one question ("What do you do when the operator tells you to start the saw?" / "Where do you never stand under the overhead crane?"). Key terms to write down.

**Frequent mistake**: learners (especially with experience in another industry) try to "prove themselves" and say "but I know how to operate a saw back home". **Trainer's response**: "Very good that you know. Here **you are not allowed** until you complete the workplace induction. Not because we don't trust you — because that is what the regulations and the insurance contract say. If you start the machine without training and cut yourself — **you won't get compensation**. That is not a punishment, that is a rule."

**Practical recommendation**: at the end of the lesson hand out an **A6 card with the two shield-rules** (in Polish + in the learner's native language). "Don't know — don't touch. Don't have the licence — don't operate." Into the PPE pocket. Let them carry it for the first 2 weeks.

**Link to the next lesson**: l2 is **manual transport of timber** — the first concrete **"what" the helper does**. Lifting limits (man, woman, occasional, continuous work), technique with bent knees, when you call the second person. Straight into practice: learners carry a board, the trainer shows **wrong → right**.
